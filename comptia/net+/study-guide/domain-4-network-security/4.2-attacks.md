# Objective 4.2 — Summarize various types of attacks and their impact to the network

Objective 4.2 is the "know your enemy" objective: you must recognize each attack from a **symptom description** and know its **impact and primary mitigation** (the mitigations themselves are applied in 4.3). The exam loves scenario stems like "Users report being redirected to a fake banking site even though they typed the correct URL" (DNS poisoning) or "After a visitor plugged a small device into a conference-room jack, some users received the wrong default gateway" (rogue DHCP). For every attack below, learn three things: **how it works conceptually, what layer/system it abuses, and which defense stops it**. This is defensive knowledge — you need to recognize these attacks in order to detect, report, and mitigate them.

## Denial-of-Service (DoS) / Distributed Denial-of-Service (DDoS)

A **DoS attack** overwhelms a system, service, or network with traffic or resource-exhausting requests so legitimate users cannot use it — a direct attack on **availability** (the A in CIA). A **DDoS** is the same goal launched simultaneously from **many distributed sources**, typically a **botnet**: thousands to millions of malware-compromised computers and IoT devices ("bots"/"zombies") remotely coordinated by an attacker's command-and-control (C2) infrastructure. Distribution makes DDoS far harder to block — you can't just filter one source IP, and total attack bandwidth can dwarf the victim's links.

### Categories of DoS/DDoS attacks

| Category | How it exhausts the victim | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Volumetric** | Saturates raw **bandwidth** with a flood of traffic (measured in Gbps/Tbps) | UDP floods, ICMP floods, amplification floods |
| **Protocol** | Exhausts **connection-state resources** on servers/firewalls/load balancers by abusing protocol behavior | **SYN flood** (half-open TCP connections that never complete the handshake), fragmented-packet attacks |
| **Application-layer (L7)** | Exhausts **server application resources** with seemingly legitimate requests (low bandwidth, high cost per request) | HTTP GET/POST floods, Slowloris (holding connections open with painfully slow requests) |

### Reflection and amplification

In a **reflection** attack, the attacker sends requests to innocent third-party servers with a **spoofed source IP = the victim's address**, so replies "reflect" onto the victim. **Amplification** adds leverage: the attacker picks protocols whose **responses are much larger than the requests** — small query in, huge answer out, all aimed at the victim. Classic amplifiers are UDP services that answer without a handshake: **DNS** (small query → large answer, especially with DNSSEC records), **NTP** (the old `monlist` command), memcached, SSDP.

**Impact:** service outages, saturated WAN links, collateral damage to everything sharing the infrastructure, revenue/reputation loss. **Mitigations to recognize:** upstream scrubbing/CDN DDoS services, rate limiting, blackhole routing, and ISP cooperation.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** DoS = one source; DDoS = many sources (botnet). "Small spoofed queries to open DNS resolvers causing massive traffic to a victim" = **reflection/amplification** attack. A SYN flood is the classic **protocol** attack — half-open connections exhaust the TCP backlog.

## VLAN Hopping

VLAN hopping lets an attacker send traffic into a VLAN they are not assigned to, defeating the segmentation you built in 4.1. Two techniques:

### Switch spoofing

The attacker's device pretends to be a **switch** by negotiating a **trunk link** using Cisco's **Dynamic Trunking Protocol (DTP)**. If the real switch port is left in a dynamic/auto mode, it happily forms a trunk — and a trunk carries **all VLANs**, giving the attacker access to every VLAN's traffic.

### Double tagging

The attacker crafts frames with **two 802.1Q VLAN tags**. The first switch strips the **outer tag** (which matches the port's **native VLAN** — native-VLAN traffic is sent untagged on trunks), then forwards the frame with the **inner tag** still attached; the next switch reads that inner tag and delivers the frame into the target VLAN. Limitations worth knowing: it works when the attacker sits on the **native VLAN**, and it's **one-way** (responses don't come back the same path) — still enough to inject malicious traffic.

**Mitigations:** disable DTP (`switchport nonegotiate`, statically set access/trunk mode), **change the native VLAN** to an unused VLAN ID (don't use VLAN 1), don't put any access ports in the native VLAN, and shut down unused ports.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Students mix up the two methods. **Switch spoofing = tricking DTP into forming a trunk** (mitigate: disable auto-negotiation). **Double tagging = two 802.1Q headers abusing the native VLAN** (mitigate: unused native VLAN, tag native traffic). Both answers may appear in the same question — match the mechanism described.

## Media Access Control (MAC) Flooding

A switch learns source MAC addresses into its **CAM table** (content-addressable memory) so it can forward frames only out the correct port. In a MAC flooding attack, the attacker blasts the switch with frames from **thousands of bogus source MAC addresses** until the CAM table is **full**. Unable to learn or look up entries, the switch **fails open** and floods unicast frames out **every port — behaving like a hub**. The attacker then runs a packet sniffer to capture traffic (including credentials) that they should never have seen. Impact: loss of confidentiality plus degraded switch performance.

**Mitigation:** **port security** — limit the number of MAC addresses learned per port and shut down/restrict violating ports (details in 4.3).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The exam phrase to recognize: an attack that "causes the switch to act like a hub" or "fills the CAM/MAC address table" = MAC flooding; the answer to "which feature prevents it" = port security.

## ARP Poisoning and ARP Spoofing

ARP (Address Resolution Protocol) maps IP addresses to MAC addresses on a LAN — and it has **no authentication**: hosts believe any ARP reply they receive, even unsolicited ("gratuitous") ones.

- **ARP spoofing** is the act of **sending forged ARP messages** that claim the attacker's MAC address belongs to another host's IP (typically the default gateway).
- **ARP poisoning** is the **result**: victims' **ARP caches now hold the falsified entry**, so they unknowingly send traffic destined for the gateway (or another host) to the attacker instead.

| | ARP spoofing | ARP poisoning |
|---|---|---|
| What it names | The **technique** — transmitting forged ARP replies/announcements | The **corrupted state** — victims' ARP caches contain false IP→MAC mappings |
| Relationship | Spoofing is *how*; poisoning is the *outcome*. In practice (and often on the exam) the terms are used interchangeably for the same attack | |
| Attacker's goal | Become an invisible relay (**on-path/MITM**) to intercept, read, or modify LAN traffic; can also blackhole traffic (DoS) | |

**Impact:** on-path interception of a LAN segment's traffic (confidentiality + integrity), session hijacking, or denial of service. **Mitigations:** **Dynamic ARP Inspection (DAI)** on switches (validates ARP packets against the DHCP snooping binding table and drops forgeries), static ARP entries for critical hosts, and encryption (TLS/SSH) so intercepted traffic is unreadable.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** ARP attacks are strictly **local** — ARP does not cross routers, so the attacker must already be on the same LAN/VLAN. If a scenario involves interception on the local subnet with "duplicate IP/MAC warnings" or gateway MAC changes, think ARP poisoning, not DNS attacks.

## DNS Poisoning and DNS Spoofing

DNS translates names to IP addresses; corrupt the answer and you control where victims go — while the browser's address bar still shows the correct name.

- **DNS spoofing** is the broad technique of supplying **forged DNS responses** — e.g., an on-path attacker answering queries before the real server can, or a rogue DNS server handed out by rogue DHCP.
- **DNS poisoning** (cache poisoning) specifically means **inserting false records into a DNS resolver's cache**. Once poisoned, the resolver serves the malicious answer to **every client that queries it** until the TTL expires — one successful attack redirects thousands of users.

| | DNS spoofing | DNS poisoning (cache poisoning) |
|---|---|---|
| Mechanism | Forged responses delivered to the victim (race/on-path/rogue server) | False records planted **in a resolver's cache** |
| Scope | The targeted client(s)/session | **Everyone using that resolver**, for the record's TTL |
| Relationship | Spoofed responses are the usual *means* of poisoning a cache | Poisoning is the *persisted result* |

**Impact:** victims are transparently redirected to attacker-controlled sites for credential harvesting, malware delivery, or interception — typed URLs are correct, certificates may warn (users often click through). **Mitigations:** **DNSSEC** (cryptographically signs DNS records so resolvers can verify authenticity and integrity), encrypted DNS (DoH/DoT) against on-path spoofing, patched/randomized resolvers, and short local attack surface (no open resolvers).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Users typed the correct URL but landed on a fraudulent site" = DNS poisoning/spoofing (this passive redirection is also called **pharming**). The mitigation answer is **DNSSEC**. Contrast with ARP poisoning: ARP redirects at Layer 2 on the local LAN; DNS redirects victims anywhere on the Internet via bad name resolution.

## Rogue Devices and Services

A **rogue device** is any unauthorized hardware or service attached to the network — planted by an attacker or innocently added by an employee. Either way it bypasses security controls and creates an uncontrolled entry point. The objective names two:

### Rogue DHCP

An unauthorized DHCP server answers clients' broadcast DISCOVER messages — and whichever server answers first wins. A rogue DHCP server can hand out:

- a **malicious default gateway** (the attacker's machine) → all victim traffic flows through the attacker = **on-path attack**;
- a **malicious DNS server** → enables DNS spoofing/redirection;
- **bogus addresses** → widespread connectivity failures (accidental rogue DHCP, like a home router plugged into the office, usually causes this symptom).

**Mitigation:** **DHCP snooping** on switches — only designated **trusted ports** (toward the real DHCP server) may send DHCP server responses (OFFER/ACK); server messages arriving on untrusted access ports are dropped. DHCP snooping also builds the IP-to-MAC-to-port **binding table** that Dynamic ARP Inspection relies on.

### Rogue AP (access point)

An unauthorized wireless access point connected to the wired network — e.g., an employee's personal AP plugged into their desk jack for "better Wi-Fi," or a device an attacker hides in the office. It **extends the internal network over the air**, typically with weak or no security, letting outsiders bypass the firewall entirely.

**Mitigations:** 802.1X/NAC on switch ports (unauthorized devices can't get network access), wireless intrusion prevention systems (WIPS) that detect unknown APs, regular wireless surveys, and disabling unused switch ports.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Symptom sorting — "**some users suddenly have the wrong gateway/DNS or can't reach anything** after a device appeared" = rogue **DHCP**. "**An unauthorized SSID/AP is broadcasting from inside the building on our LAN**" = rogue **AP**.

## Evil Twin

An **evil twin** is a **wireless-specific impersonation attack**: the attacker stands up their own AP broadcasting the **same SSID** (and often spoofed BSSID) as a legitimate network — the coffee-shop Wi-Fi, the corporate SSID — often with a **stronger signal** so devices prefer it, sometimes after deauthenticating clients from the real AP. Victims connect automatically; the attacker now sees all their traffic (on-path) and commonly presents a **fake captive portal** to harvest credentials.

**Evil twin vs. rogue AP:** a rogue AP is *any unauthorized AP attached to your network* (insider convenience or attacker foothold); an evil twin is *an attacker's AP impersonating a legitimate SSID* to lure clients — it usually is **not** connected to your internal network at all, it just mimics it.

**Mitigations/user defenses:** WIPS detection, 802.1X/WPA-Enterprise with server **certificate validation** (the evil twin can't prove the RADIUS server's identity), VPN use on public Wi-Fi, not auto-joining open networks.

## On-Path Attack

An **on-path attack** (formerly **man-in-the-middle, MITM**) is the umbrella term: the attacker secretly **positions themselves between two communicating parties**, relaying traffic so both sides believe they're talking directly to each other. From that position the attacker can **eavesdrop** (confidentiality), **modify data in flight** (integrity), inject content, or hijack sessions.

Many 4.2 attacks are really *techniques to get on-path*:

- **ARP poisoning** → on-path within a LAN
- **Rogue DHCP** (malicious gateway) → on-path for a subnet
- **DNS poisoning/spoofing** → victims connect *through/to* the attacker
- **Evil twin / rogue AP** → on-path for wireless clients

**Mitigations:** end-to-end encryption with authentication (TLS with certificate validation, SSH host-key checking, VPNs), plus the per-technique defenses above (DAI, DHCP snooping, DNSSEC). Encryption doesn't stop interception, but it makes intercepted traffic unreadable and tampering detectable.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** N10-009 uses the term **on-path attack** — older material says man-in-the-middle. If a scenario describes an attacker "intercepting and relaying traffic between a user and a server without either noticing," that's on-path, and the enabling technique named in the stem (forged ARP replies, fake AP, etc.) tells you which specific attack to pick.

## Social Engineering

Social engineering attacks the **human**, not the technology — manipulating people through trust, urgency, fear, or authority into revealing information or performing actions. It's often the cheapest way into a hardened network, and the defense is **security awareness training** plus verification procedures.

### Phishing

Phishing is **fraudulent communication — classically email — impersonating a trusted entity** to trick victims into revealing credentials, clicking malicious links, opening infected attachments, or sending money. Recognize the variants:

| Variant | Channel / targeting | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Phishing** | Mass email, generic lure | "Your account is locked — click here to verify" sent to thousands |
| **Spear phishing** | Email **targeted at a specific person/organization**, personalized with researched details | Email to an engineer referencing their real project and manager |
| **Whaling** | Spear phishing aimed at **executives/high-value targets** ("big fish") | Fake legal subpoena sent to the CFO; CEO-fraud wire requests |
| **Vishing** | **Voice** phishing (phone/VoIP, often spoofed caller ID) | "This is the IT help desk — I need your password to fix your mailbox" |
| **Smishing** | **SMS/text** phishing | "Your package is held — tap this link to pay a fee" |

Red flags: urgency/pressure, mismatched sender domains, unexpected attachments, requests for credentials or payment, look-alike URLs.

### Dumpster diving

Digging through an organization's **trash and recycling** for discarded value: org charts, invoices, sticky-note passwords, network diagrams, customer lists, and old hard drives or backup media. Perfectly effective and low-tech; the harvest often fuels convincing spear phishing. **Defenses: shred documents, sanitize/destroy storage media, secure/locked disposal bins.**

### Shoulder surfing

**Watching someone's screen or keyboard** to capture passwords, PINs, or sensitive data — over a shoulder in the office, at an ATM, on a plane, or via a camera/binoculars from a distance. **Defenses: privacy screen filters, positioning monitors away from sight lines, shielding keypads, screen-lock habits.**

### Tailgating

**Following an authorized person through a secured door** before it closes, without presenting credentials — defeating badge access with nothing but timing and social pressure (carrying boxes, wearing a delivery uniform, "please hold the door"). 

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** **Tailgating vs. piggybacking** — many sources distinguish them: **tailgating** = following through **without the authorized person's knowledge**; **piggybacking** = the authorized person **knowingly consents** (holds the door as a courtesy). CompTIA lists only tailgating; if both appear as choices, consent is the discriminator. **Defenses: access control vestibules (mantraps), turnstiles, security guards, one-badge-one-person policy and training.**

## Malware

Malware ("malicious software") is any software designed to harm, exploit, or gain unauthorized access to systems. Network+ needs the categories and — critically — their **network impact**:

| Type | Defining behavior | Network relevance |
|---|---|---|
| **Virus** | Attaches to files/programs; **requires user action** to execute and spread | Spreads via shares and email attachments |
| **Worm** | **Self-replicates across the network without user action**, exploiting vulnerabilities | Can saturate links and compromise fleets in minutes (scanning + copying traffic) |
| **Trojan** | Disguised as legitimate software; user installs it voluntarily | Commonly opens **backdoors** for remote access (RATs) |
| **Ransomware** | **Encrypts data** and demands payment; may exfiltrate first (double extortion) | Spreads laterally to shares/servers/backups — an **availability** catastrophe |
| **Spyware/keylogger** | Covertly collects data/keystrokes | Exfiltration traffic to attacker servers |
| **Botnet agent** | Enrolls the host into a botnet under C2 control | Your devices become DDoS/spam sources — watch for **unusual outbound traffic** |

**Network impact and detection signals:** worms cause sudden traffic spikes and mass scanning; botnet infections show anomalous outbound connections (C2 beaconing); ransomware shows mass file modification on shares. **Defenses layer together:** patching, endpoint protection, email filtering, segmentation to contain lateral movement, backups (offline/immutable) for ransomware recovery, and egress monitoring.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Discriminators: **needs user action + attaches to files = virus**; **spreads by itself over the network = worm**; **pretends to be a legitimate program = trojan**; **encrypts your files for payment = ransomware**. Botnets (built from malware-infected hosts) are the launch platform for **DDoS** — the two topics link.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] DoS = one source, DDoS = botnet of many; categories: **volumetric** (bandwidth), **protocol** (state, e.g., SYN flood), **application-layer** (L7 resources); **reflection/amplification** uses spoofed source IPs and protocols with big answers (DNS, NTP).
- [ ] VLAN hopping: **switch spoofing** (DTP trunk negotiation) and **double tagging** (native-VLAN abuse). Mitigate: disable DTP, unused native VLAN (never 1), shut unused ports.
- [ ] MAC flooding fills the **CAM table** → switch floods like a hub → sniffing. Mitigate: **port security**.
- [ ] ARP spoofing = forged ARP replies (the act); ARP poisoning = corrupted caches (the result); enables LAN on-path. Mitigate: **Dynamic ARP Inspection**.
- [ ] DNS poisoning corrupts a **resolver's cache** (hits everyone using it); DNS spoofing forges responses. Mitigate: **DNSSEC**.
- [ ] Rogue DHCP hands out malicious gateway/DNS → on-path. Mitigate: **DHCP snooping** (trusted ports).
- [ ] Rogue AP = unauthorized AP **on your network**; evil twin = attacker AP **impersonating your SSID** to lure clients.
- [ ] On-path (MITM) = attacker relaying between two parties; ARP/DHCP/DNS/evil-twin attacks are ways to get there; encryption + authentication blunt it.
- [ ] Phishing family: spear (targeted), whaling (executives), vishing (voice), smishing (SMS).
- [ ] Physical social engineering: dumpster diving (shred!), shoulder surfing (privacy filters), tailgating (mantrap/vestibule; piggybacking = with consent).
- [ ] Malware: virus (user-triggered), worm (self-spreading), trojan (disguised), ransomware (encrypts = availability loss); botnets power DDoS.

## Self-Check Questions

1. An attacker connects a laptop to a meeting-room wall jack, negotiates trunking with the switch, and begins capturing traffic from multiple VLANs. Name the attack and two configuration changes that would have prevented it.
2. Minutes after a strange device was found in a cubicle, users on that subnet began routing all Internet traffic through an unknown local IP address. Their IP settings were assigned automatically. What happened, and which switch feature prevents it?
3. What is the difference in scope between DNS spoofing against a single client and DNS cache poisoning of a company resolver? Which technology defends DNS integrity?
4. At an airport, a traveler's phone auto-connects to "FreeAirportWiFi," which presents a login page asking for their email password. The real airport network has the same name. What attack is this, and how does it differ from a rogue AP?
5. A hospital's file shares suddenly become unreadable and a payment demand appears; meanwhile the security team sees the malicious process copying itself to other machines over the network without any user interaction. Which two malware behaviors are shown, and which CIA principle is primarily violated?
