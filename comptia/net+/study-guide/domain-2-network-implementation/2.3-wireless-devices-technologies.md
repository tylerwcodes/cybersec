# Objective 2.3 — Given a scenario, select and configure wireless devices and technologies

Wireless is where physics meets configuration: channels overlap, radar owns parts of the 5 GHz band, and encryption choices decide whether your Wi-Fi can be cracked from the parking lot. This is a scenario-driven objective — expect questions like "APs on channels 1, 4, and 8 are causing interference — which channels should you use?", "which encryption resists offline dictionary attacks?", and "which antenna fits a point-to-point link between buildings?" You need the 2.4/5/6 GHz trade-offs, non-overlapping channels, channel width, DFS/802.11h, SSID vs. BSSID vs. ESSID, network types, WPA2 vs. WPA3, PSK vs. Enterprise, guest networks/captive portals, antenna types, and autonomous vs. lightweight APs.

## Channels

Each Wi-Fi band is divided into numbered **channels** — slices of frequency an AP transmits on. Neighboring APs must be placed on channels that don't overlap, or their signals interfere (co-channel and adjacent-channel interference), causing retransmissions and throughput collapse.

### Non-overlapping Channels

**2.4 GHz:** channels are spaced only 5 MHz apart but each transmission is ~20 MHz wide (22 MHz for legacy DSSS), so adjacent channels overlap heavily. In the US (FCC), channels **1–11** are available, and only **1, 6, and 11** are non-overlapping. Design rule: tile APs with 1/6/11 in a honeycomb so no two neighboring APs share a channel.

**5 GHz:** channels are 20 MHz apart, so **every standard 20 MHz channel is non-overlapping** — up to 25 of them in the US (36, 40, 44, 48, 52…64, 100…144, 149…165). Far easier to design dense deployments.

**6 GHz:** adds even more non-overlapping spectrum (59 × 20 MHz channels in the US).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Which 2.4 GHz channels should adjacent APs use?" → **1, 6, 11**. Any answer with channels like 3, 4, 8, or 9 causes overlap. A neighboring AP on channel 4 interferes with *both* 1 and 6.

### Channel Width

Channels can be bonded into wider ones: **20, 40, 80, or 160 MHz** (Wi-Fi 7 adds 320 MHz in 6 GHz).

| Width | Throughput | Trade-off |
|---|---|---|
| 20 MHz | Baseline | Most non-overlapping channels; best for dense/2.4 GHz environments |
| 40 MHz | ~2× | Consumes two channels; in 2.4 GHz it swallows most of the band — avoid |
| 80 MHz | ~4× | Common in 5 GHz for high throughput; fewer clean channels available |
| 160 MHz | ~8× | Very few available channels (often requires DFS); practical mainly in 6 GHz |

Wider channels = more speed but fewer non-overlapping channels, more susceptibility to interference, and slightly reduced range (energy spread across more spectrum). Rule of thumb: 20 MHz in 2.4 GHz always; 40/80 MHz in 5 GHz depending on density; go wide in 6 GHz.

### Regulatory Impacts

Radio spectrum is regulated per country (FCC in the US, ETSI in Europe): rules dictate which channels, maximum transmit power (EIRP), and indoor/outdoor use. Examples: 2.4 GHz channels 12–13 are usable in Europe but not for normal US use; 5 GHz UNII-2/2-Extended channels are shared with weather and military **radar**, so Wi-Fi must defer to radar systems. APs have a country/regulatory-domain setting that must be correct — a mis-set domain can be illegal and cause interference.

#### 802.11h

**IEEE 802.11h** ("Spectrum and Transmit Power Management Extensions") was created so 5 GHz Wi-Fi can coexist with radar and satellite systems (originally for European regulations, now applied broadly). Its two mechanisms:

- **DFS (Dynamic Frequency Selection):** the AP monitors its channel for radar pulses; if radar is detected, it must vacate the channel and move the BSS to another channel.
- **TPC (Transmit Power Control):** dynamically limits/reduces transmit power to the minimum needed, reducing interference with satellite services.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "Clients on a 5 GHz DFS channel suddenly drop and the AP changes channels" is *normal DFS behavior* after a radar event — not a failing AP. Also don't confuse 802.11h (DFS/TPC, spectrum management) with 802.11i (security/WPA2) or 802.1X (port authentication).

## Frequency Options

| Band | Channels (US) | Range/Penetration | Congestion | Speed/Capacity | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **2.4 GHz** | 1–11 (only 1/6/11 clean) | Longest range, best wall penetration | Worst — Bluetooth, microwaves, baby monitors, everyone's Wi-Fi | Lowest | Legacy + IoT devices live here |
| **5 GHz** | ~25 non-overlapping 20 MHz | Shorter range | Moderate | High (80/160 MHz possible) | DFS channels shared with radar |
| **6 GHz** | 59 × 20 MHz | Shortest range | Minimal | Highest (wide channels plentiful) | **Wi-Fi 6E/7 only**; no legacy devices; WPA3 required |

- **2.4 GHz:** lower frequency = travels farther and penetrates walls better, but only three usable non-overlapping channels and massive interference from non-Wi-Fi devices (microwave ovens, Bluetooth).
- **5 GHz:** many more channels, higher throughput, less interference; shorter range. Some channels require **DFS** radar detection.
- **6 GHz:** opened for unlicensed use with **Wi-Fi 6E** (802.11ax extended); a huge swath of clean spectrum (5.925–7.125 GHz in the US), no legacy 802.11a/b/g/n/ac devices allowed, WPA3 mandatory. Shortest range of the three.

### Band Steering

**Band steering** is an AP feature that detects dual-band-capable clients and encourages (by delaying/withholding 2.4 GHz probe responses) them to associate on **5 GHz or 6 GHz** instead of crowding onto 2.4 GHz. Result: better performance for capable clients and less 2.4 GHz congestion for legacy/IoT devices that have no choice. Typically used with a single SSID broadcast across bands.

## Service Set Identifier (SSID)

The **SSID** is the human-readable wireless network name (up to 32 bytes) broadcast in beacons. Hiding the SSID is not real security — it's trivially discovered in probe/association frames.

### Basic Service Set Identifier (BSSID)

The **BSSID** uniquely identifies one AP radio's basic service set — it is the **MAC address of the AP's radio** for that SSID. One physical AP broadcasting three SSIDs on two bands presents six BSSIDs. Wi-Fi analyzers list BSSIDs to distinguish individual APs that share an SSID.

### Extended Service Set Identifier (ESSID)

An **ESS (extended service set)** is **multiple APs sharing the same SSID** on a common distribution system (the wired LAN) so clients can **roam** among them without reconfiguring. The ESSID is that shared network name. In practice "SSID" and "ESSID" are used interchangeably; the distinction the exam wants: **BSSID = one AP radio (a MAC), ESSID = the same network name spanning many APs**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Which value identifies the specific AP a client is associated with?" → **BSSID**. "Multiple APs configured with the same network name to allow roaming" → **ESSID/ESS**.

## Network Types

- **Infrastructure:** the normal mode — clients associate to an **AP**, which bridges them to the wired LAN. All traffic flows through the AP (a BSS; multiple APs = ESS).
- **Ad hoc (IBSS):** devices connect **directly to each other, peer-to-peer, with no AP** (Independent Basic Service Set). Quick file sharing between laptops; doesn't scale, no central security. (Wi-Fi Direct is the modern consumer descendant.)
- **Point to point:** two directional antennas/bridges forming a dedicated wireless **link between two locations** — e.g., connecting two buildings across a street where trenching fiber is impractical. Uses high-gain directional antennas, requires line of sight.
- **Mesh:** APs/nodes interconnect **wirelessly with each other**, relaying traffic hop-by-hop back to the wired root; the mesh self-forms and **self-heals** around failed nodes. Great where cabling every AP is impossible (warehouses, outdoor campuses, homes); trade-off is backhaul bandwidth consumed by relaying.

## Encryption

### WPA2 vs. WPA3

| Feature | WPA2 (802.11i, 2004) | WPA3 (2018) |
|---|---|---|
| Encryption | **AES-CCMP** | AES-CCMP / **AES-GCMP** (192-bit suite in Enterprise mode) |
| Personal-mode key exchange | PSK + **4-way handshake** | **SAE** (Simultaneous Authentication of Equals) |
| Offline dictionary attacks | **Vulnerable** — captured handshake can be brute-forced offline | **Resistant** — SAE requires live interaction per guess; forward secrecy |
| Protected Management Frames (802.11w) | Optional | **Required** — blocks deauthentication/disassociation spoofing |
| Open-network privacy | None | OWE "Enhanced Open" (opportunistic encryption) |
| Known attacks | KRACK (key reinstallation), handshake capture + cracking | Early "Dragonblood" implementation flaws (patched) |

- **WPA2** replaced WPA/TKIP with **AES-CCMP** encryption. Its weakness in Personal mode: an attacker who captures the **4-way handshake** can run an **offline dictionary/brute-force attack** against the pre-shared key, and deauth frames (unprotected management frames) make forcing handshakes easy.
- **WPA3** replaces the PSK handshake with **SAE** — a password-authenticated key exchange (a "dragonfly" handshake) where each password guess requires a live exchange with the AP, defeating offline dictionary attacks and providing **forward secrecy** (cracking the password later doesn't decrypt past captures). Protected Management Frames are mandatory. WPA3-Enterprise offers an optional 192-bit security suite.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Keywords map: "AES-CCMP" → WPA2. "SAE / resistant to offline dictionary attacks / forward secrecy / protected management frames" → WPA3. "TKIP/RC4" → legacy WPA (deprecated — never the right choice to deploy).

## Guest Networks

A **guest network** is a separate SSID mapped to an isolated VLAN/subnet that gives visitors internet access while blocking access to internal resources (client isolation is typically enabled too). It's a segmentation control as much as a convenience.

### Captive Portals

A **captive portal** intercepts a guest's first web request and redirects it to a login/terms page — accept the AUP, enter a voucher code, email, or credentials, and only then is traffic released. Seen in hotels, airports, and cafés. Often paired with an open or OWE network; the portal provides *authorization and accountability*, not encryption.

## Authentication

### PSK vs. Enterprise

| | Personal (PSK) | Enterprise (802.1X) |
|---|---|---|
| Credential | One shared passphrase for everyone | **Per-user credentials or certificates** |
| Infrastructure | None — configured on the AP | **RADIUS server** (e.g., with EAP methods; often tied to AD) |
| Accountability | None — can't tell users apart | Full — individual login, logging, and revocation |
| Offboarding | Must change the key everywhere | Disable one account |
| Best fit | Homes, small offices | Corporate networks |

- **PSK (pre-shared key):** everyone uses the same passphrase; the key derives the pairwise keys via the 4-way handshake (WPA2) or SAE (WPA3). Problem: shared secrets leak, and a departing employee means rekeying every device.
- **Enterprise:** uses **IEEE 802.1X** port-based authentication — the client (supplicant) authenticates through the AP (authenticator) to a **RADIUS server** (authentication server) using EAP (e.g., PEAP with username/password, EAP-TLS with certificates). Each user gets unique credentials and unique encryption keys.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "WPA2-Enterprise" doesn't change the *encryption* (still AES-CCMP) — it changes *authentication* (802.1X/RADIUS instead of a shared passphrase). Questions that mention RADIUS, per-user login, or certificates are pointing at Enterprise/802.1X.

## Antennas

### Omnidirectional vs. Directional

- **Omnidirectional:** radiates roughly equally in all horizontal directions — a 360° doughnut-shaped pattern around the antenna. Standard on APs serving clients spread around them (ceiling mounts). Lower gain, broad coverage.
- **Directional:** focuses energy into a narrower beam — higher **gain** (measured in dBi) in one direction at the expense of coverage elsewhere. The narrower the beamwidth, the higher the gain and the longer the reach.
  - **Yagi:** rod-style antenna with multiple elements; medium-to-high gain; medium-distance point-to-point or long corridors/warehouse aisles.
  - **Patch/panel:** flat antenna radiating a wide-ish sector from a wall — cover one room/area from its edge, or short bridge links.
  - **Parabolic (dish/grid):** highest gain, very narrow beam; long-distance **point-to-point building-to-building** links; requires careful aiming and line of sight.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Antenna gain doesn't create power — it *focuses* it. "Connect two buildings 2 km apart" → parabolic (or Yagi for shorter spans). "Cover an open office from the ceiling" → omnidirectional.

## Autonomous vs. Lightweight Access Points

| | Autonomous (standalone/"fat") AP | Lightweight ("thin") AP |
|---|---|---|
| Configuration | Each AP configured and managed individually (its own GUI/CLI) | Centrally configured by a **Wireless LAN Controller (WLC)** |
| Intelligence | All 802.11 functions on the AP | Split-MAC: real-time functions on AP, management/roaming/RF decisions on WLC |
| Protocol | n/a | **CAPWAP** tunnels AP↔WLC (control UDP 5246, data UDP 5247; successor of LWAPP) |
| Scales to | A handful of APs | Hundreds/thousands — zero-touch provisioning, coordinated channels/power, seamless roaming |
| Failure mode | Independent | WLC is a critical dependency (mitigate with redundant controllers or cloud management) |

- **Autonomous APs** suit small deployments; at scale, touching every AP for an SSID or password change is unmanageable.
- **Lightweight APs** download their config from the controller and tunnel traffic/control to it via **CAPWAP**; the WLC coordinates channel and power assignments across all APs, detects rogues, and enables fast roaming. Modern cloud-managed APs (e.g., dashboard-managed) are a variation on centralized control.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] 2.4 GHz US channels 1–11; only **1, 6, 11** are non-overlapping; longest range, most interference.
- [ ] 5 GHz: many non-overlapping channels; DFS channels must vacate for radar. 6 GHz: Wi-Fi 6E+, huge clean spectrum, no legacy clients, WPA3 required.
- [ ] Channel width 20/40/80/160 MHz: wider = faster but fewer clean channels and more interference; keep 2.4 GHz at 20 MHz.
- [ ] Regulatory domains (FCC/ETSI) set legal channels/power; **802.11h = DFS + TPC** for radar coexistence in 5 GHz.
- [ ] Band steering nudges dual-band clients to 5/6 GHz.
- [ ] SSID = network name; **BSSID = AP radio's MAC**; ESSID = same SSID across multiple APs for roaming.
- [ ] Network types: infrastructure (via AP), ad hoc/IBSS (peer-to-peer, no AP), point-to-point (building-to-building bridge), mesh (self-healing wireless backhaul).
- [ ] WPA2 = AES-CCMP + 4-way handshake (offline-crackable PSK); WPA3 = SAE, forward secrecy, protected management frames, dictionary-attack resistant.
- [ ] Guest network = isolated SSID/VLAN; captive portal = web-redirect login/AUP page.
- [ ] PSK = one shared passphrase; Enterprise = 802.1X + RADIUS with per-user credentials.
- [ ] Omnidirectional = 360° coverage; directional (Yagi, patch, parabolic) = focused gain for links/sectors.
- [ ] Autonomous AP = standalone; lightweight AP = WLC-managed via CAPWAP.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A site survey shows three APs in adjacent offices on 2.4 GHz channels 2, 6, and 10, with users complaining of poor performance. What should you change and why?
2. During the afternoon, an AP on 5 GHz channel 120 abruptly moves all clients to channel 44. What feature caused this and what standard defines it?
3. What is the difference between a BSSID and an ESSID?
4. A company wants each employee to log in to Wi-Fi with their own Active Directory credentials, and to be able to revoke one person's access without touching anyone else's device. What should be deployed?
5. Why does WPA3-Personal resist the offline dictionary attacks that threaten WPA2-Personal?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. Move the APs to **channels 1, 6, and 11** — the only non-overlapping 2.4 GHz channels in the US. Channels 2 and 10 overlap with their neighbors' frequencies, causing adjacent-channel interference and retransmissions.
2. **DFS (Dynamic Frequency Selection)** detected radar on the channel and vacated it, as required by **802.11h** (which also defines TPC, Transmit Power Control). This is compliant behavior, not a malfunction.
3. A **BSSID** identifies a single AP radio's basic service set and is the radio's **MAC address**; an **ESSID** is the shared network name used by **multiple APs** in an extended service set so clients can roam among them.
4. **WPA2/WPA3-Enterprise** — 802.1X authentication against a **RADIUS** server (integrated with AD), giving per-user credentials, individual session keys, logging, and one-account revocation. PSK cannot do this.
5. WPA3 replaces the PSK 4-way handshake with **SAE**, a password-authenticated key exchange in which every password guess requires a live exchange with the AP — nothing captured off the air can be brute-forced offline — and it provides forward secrecy for past sessions.
