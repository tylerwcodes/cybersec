# Objective 5.2 — Given a scenario, troubleshoot common cabling and physical interface issues

Layer 1 is where most real-world network failures live: bad cables, wrong cables, botched terminations, dying transceivers, and overloaded PoE budgets. Objective 5.2 is heavily **scenario-based**: the exam describes symptoms (no link light, increasing CRC errors, an AP that keeps rebooting, a fiber link that won't come up) and asks you to name the cause or the fix. To score here you need hard numbers — cable category speeds and frequencies, the 64/1518-byte frame thresholds, and the PoE wattages — plus the diagnostic logic that maps each symptom to its physical cause. Pair this chapter with 5.5 (the tools: cable testers, toners, light meters, visual fault locators are how you *prove* these diagnoses).

## Cable Issues

### Incorrect Cable

Using the wrong cable for the job produces anything from "no link at all" to "link negotiates but performance is terrible." Three classic mismatches:

#### Single-mode vs. multimode fiber

| Property | Single-mode (SMF) | Multimode (MMF) |
|---|---|---|
| Core diameter | ~8–10 µm | 50 or 62.5 µm |
| Light source | Laser | LED or VCSEL (laser at 10G+) |
| Typical wavelengths | 1310 nm, 1550 nm | 850 nm, 1300 nm |
| Distance | Long haul — kilometers (10 km, 40 km, 80 km+) | Short reach — hundreds of meters (e.g., OM3 ≈ 300 m and OM4 ≈ 400 m at 10G) |
| Jacket color (typical) | **Yellow** | **Orange** (OM1/OM2) or **aqua** (OM3/OM4) |
| Cost | Cable cheap, optics expensive | Optics cheaper |

Mixing them fails: an SMF transceiver into MMF cable (or vice versa) means core sizes and wavelengths don't match — the link either never comes up or takes massive dB loss. **Both ends must use the same fiber type and matching transceivers.** Mode conditioning cables/media converters exist for special cases, but on the exam, "single-mode optic plugged into multimode patch cable → no link" is the pattern.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Jacket colors are testable shorthand: **yellow = single-mode, orange/aqua = multimode**. A scenario mentioning a yellow patch cable on one end and aqua on the other is telling you the fiber types are mismatched.

#### Category 5/6/7/8 twisted-pair

| Category | Max data rate | Bandwidth | Distance notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cat 5** | 100 Mbps (Fast Ethernet) | 100 MHz | 100 m; legacy — replace it |
| **Cat 5e** | 1 Gbps | 100 MHz | 100 m; reduced crosstalk vs. Cat 5 |
| **Cat 6** | 1 Gbps @ 100 m; **10 Gbps @ 55 m** | 250 MHz | 10G only on short runs |
| **Cat 6a** | **10 Gbps @ 100 m** | 500 MHz | "a" = augmented; resists alien crosstalk |
| **Cat 7** | 10 Gbps @ 100 m | 600 MHz | Always shielded (S/FTP); GG45/TERA connectors; never TIA-ratified |
| **Cat 8** | **25/40 Gbps @ 30 m** | 2000 MHz (2 GHz) | Data-center switch-to-server runs; shielded |

Symptom pattern: "The link works but only negotiates 100 Mbps" → could be old Cat 5 (or a damaged pair — Fast Ethernet uses 2 pairs, gigabit needs all 4). "10G link errors on a 90 m Cat 6 run" → Cat 6 supports 10G only to ~55 m; upgrade to Cat 6a.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Cat 6 *does* support 10GBASE-T — but **only to about 55 meters**. If the scenario's run is longer than 55 m and needs 10G, the answer is Cat 6a (or fiber), not "Cat 6 is fine."

#### Shielded (STP) vs. unshielded (UTP) twisted pair

- **UTP:** no shielding; cheaper, thinner, easier to terminate; relies on pair twisting alone to reject noise. Fine for typical offices.
- **STP (and screened variants F/UTP, S/FTP):** foil/braid shielding around pairs and/or the whole cable; blocks **EMI/RFI** in electrically noisy environments — factory floors, near motors, elevators, fluorescent lighting banks, radio equipment. Shields must be properly **grounded** (a floating or ground-looped shield can make noise worse), and STP needs shielded connectors/jacks.

Scenario cue: "Cable run through a manufacturing area experiences intermittent errors" → EMI; use STP (or reroute, or switch to fiber, which is immune to EMI).

### Signal Degradation

#### Crosstalk
Signal from one wire pair bleeding into an adjacent pair. Measured as:
- **NEXT (Near-End Crosstalk):** interference measured at the **same end** as the transmitter — most often caused by untwisting too much wire at a termination.
- **FEXT (Far-End Crosstalk):** measured at the far end of the link.
- **Alien crosstalk (AXT):** between *separate cables* bundled together — the reason Cat 6a exists for 10G.

The **twists** in twisted pair are the defense — each pair has a different twist rate to cancel coupling. Mitigation: maintain twists to within ~0.5 in (13 mm) of the termination, use higher-category or shielded cable, don't bundle too tightly. A certifier/cable analyzer measures NEXT/FEXT; a **split pair** (see terminations below) destroys the cancellation and causes severe crosstalk even though a basic wiremap looks correct.

#### Interference (EMI)
**Electromagnetic interference** from external sources: fluorescent/HID light ballasts, electric motors, elevators, HVAC compressors, microwave ovens, power cables run parallel to data cables. Symptoms: intermittent errors (CRC errors climbing), slowdowns, drops that correlate with equipment cycling on ("the link errors every time the elevator runs"). Fixes: **reroute** cabling away from the source, cross power lines at 90° instead of running parallel, use **STP**, or use **fiber** (light is immune to EMI).

#### Attenuation
Loss of signal strength over distance, measured in **dB**. Every medium attenuates: copper Ethernet is specified to **100 meters** (90 m permanent link + 10 m patch cords); beyond that, weak signal causes errors or link failure. Fiber attenuates far less (hence kilometers) but connectors, splices, and bends each add dB loss. Higher frequencies attenuate faster (why Cat 8 is limited to 30 m). Fixes: shorten the run, add a **repeater/switch** mid-span, or move to fiber. On fiber, compare measured loss (**light meter**) against the **link/loss budget**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Symptom "works fine for devices near the closet, but the far office 120 m away has no/flaky link" = **attenuation / exceeded distance limit**. The fix that appears in answer choices: add a switch or repeater mid-run, or use fiber.

### Improper Termination

Termination errors are wiring mistakes at the RJ45 plug/jack or punchdown:

- **T568A vs. T568B:** two pinout standards (they swap the green and orange pairs). Use **the same standard on both ends** for a straight-through cable. One end A and the other end B = a **crossover** cable (TX/RX swapped) — historically deliberate for like-device connections, but a *miswire* when unintended.
- **Open:** a wire not making contact (broken/not punched) — pair fails entirely.
- **Short:** two conductors touching — signal corrupted or no link.
- **Miswire/reversed pairs:** wires landed on wrong pins.
- **Split pair:** wiremap pin-to-pin looks correct, but the pairs are split across different physical twisted pairs — kills crosstalk cancellation; link may come up at low speed with heavy errors. Only a quality tester detects it.
- Untwisting more than ~0.5 in at the jack → crosstalk failure at certification.

**Diagnosis tool:** a **cable tester/wiremap tester** identifies opens, shorts, reversed/miswired pairs, and (better testers) split pairs. A **certifier** validates the run against category specs (NEXT, attenuation, length via TDR).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** A basic continuity/wiremap tester will **pass a split pair** because each pin connects to the right pin — the wires are just twisted with the wrong partners. If the scenario says "wiremap passes but the gigabit link is slow/error-prone," think **split pair** and re-terminate.

### Transmitter (TX)/Receiver (RX) Transposed

One side's transmit must connect to the other side's receive. When TX/RX are **transposed (reversed)**, both ends transmit into each other's transmitters — no link.

- **Copper:** classically fixed with a **crossover cable** (568A one end, 568B the other) between like devices (switch↔switch, PC↔PC). Modern interfaces support **auto-MDI-X**, which detects and electronically swaps TX/RX automatically — so on modern gear, cable choice rarely matters. If auto-MDI-X is disabled/absent (old hardware, speed/duplex hard-coded), the wrong cable type = no link.
- **Fiber:** each strand is one direction, so **polarity** matters. If a duplex fiber link shows no light/no link, the classic fix is to **swap the A/B strands at one end** (flip the connector). Structured fiber uses A/B polarity conventions to keep TX→RX aligned end to end.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "New fiber uplink between two switches won't come up; light is confirmed leaving each transmitter" → **TX/RX transposed — reverse the fiber strands at one end.** This is one of the most-repeated fiber troubleshooting questions.

## Interface Issues

### Increasing Interface Counters

`show interface` counters are your Layer 1/2 telemetry. **A non-zero counter isn't automatically a problem — a steadily *increasing* one is.**

| Counter | Definition | Common causes |
|---|---|---|
| **CRC errors** | Frames failing the frame check sequence (corrupted in transit) | Bad/damaged cable, EMI, failing NIC/transceiver, **duplex mismatch** |
| **Runts** | Frames **smaller than 64 bytes** (minimum legal Ethernet frame) | Collisions (half-duplex/duplex mismatch), faulty NIC |
| **Giants** | Frames **larger than 1518 bytes** (standard max; 1522 with 802.1Q tag) | **MTU/jumbo-frame mismatch** (one side jumbo-enabled, other not), faulty NIC |
| **Drops (discards)** | Frames discarded, typically because buffers/queues are full | **Congestion/oversubscription**, QoS policing, speed mismatch between links |

**Duplex mismatch** is the classic exam scenario behind several counters at once: one side full duplex, the other half duplex (often because one side is hard-coded and the other autonegotiates to half). Symptoms: link works but is *painfully slow*, with **late collisions, runts, and CRC errors** climbing on the half-duplex side. Fix: set both sides to autonegotiate (or hard-code both identically).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Memorize the numbers: **runt < 64 bytes, giant > 1518 bytes**. Giants suggest an MTU/jumbo-frame configuration mismatch, not a bad cable; drops suggest congestion, not corruption. CRC = corruption in transit.

### Port Status

| Status | Meaning | Cause / Fix |
|---|---|---|
| **Error disabled (err-disabled)** | The switch **automatically shut the port down** because a violation/error was detected | Port security violation (unexpected MAC), BPDU guard triggered (a switch/loop on an access port), flapping, storm control. **Fix the underlying cause first**, then re-enable with `shutdown` / `no shutdown` (or errdisable auto-recovery) |
| **Administratively down** | An admin **deliberately** disabled the port (`shutdown` in config) | Not a fault. If the port should be live: `no shutdown` |
| **Suspended** | Port is up but not forwarding — commonly a **LACP/EtherChannel** member whose settings don't match the bundle (or an inactive VLAN) | Correct the port-channel parameters (speed, duplex, VLAN, mode) so the member rejoins |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Distinguish by *who* turned the port off: **err-disabled = the switch did it** (protective, in response to a violation); **administratively down = a human did it** (in config); **suspended = negotiation/consistency check failed** (think LACP). "A port went down right after a user plugged in an unauthorized device" → port security → err-disabled.

## Hardware Issues

### Power over Ethernet (PoE)

PoE delivers DC power over the data cable from **PSE** (Power Sourcing Equipment — the switch or injector) to **PD** (Powered Device — phone, AP, camera).

| Standard | Name | Type | Power at PSE (per port) | Power to PD | Pairs used |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **802.3af** | PoE | Type 1 | **15.4 W** | **12.95 W** | 2 pairs |
| **802.3at** | PoE+ | Type 2 | **30 W** | **25.5 W** | 2 pairs |
| **802.3bt** | PoE++ | Type 3 | **60 W** | 51 W | 4 pairs |
| **802.3bt** | PoE++ | Type 4 | **90 W** | 71.3 W | 4 pairs |

(PD figures are lower than PSE figures because of resistive loss in the cable.)

#### Power budget exceeded
A switch has a **total PoE power budget** (e.g., a 24-port switch with a 370 W budget cannot supply 30 W on all 24 ports at once — that would need 720 W). When the budget is exhausted, new PDs won't power on, and existing devices may **power-cycle randomly or drop** (often lower-priority ports shed first). Symptom cues: "cameras randomly reboot after more APs were added," "the new phone won't power up on that switch but works elsewhere." Diagnose with **`show power inline`** (total budget, used, remaining, per-port draw). Fixes: bigger power supply/switch, spread PDs across switches, use power priority settings, or local power adapters.

#### Incorrect standard
The PD requires more power than the port's standard supplies — e.g., a Wi-Fi 6 AP needing 802.3at (25.5 W) plugged into an 802.3af-only port (12.95 W). Symptoms: device doesn't boot, boots into a degraded/low-power mode (AP disables radios), or reboots under load. Fix: connect to a port/injector supporting the required standard.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The wattage table above is pure memorization gold: **af = 15.4, at (PoE+) = 30, bt Type 3 (PoE++) = 60, bt Type 4 = 90**. Device-side: 12.95 / 25.5 / 51 / 71.3 W. Exam scenarios hinge on "the AP draws 20 W" → needs at least 802.3at.

### Transceivers

Transceivers (SFP, SFP+, QSFP, etc.) are pluggable optics/copper modules.

#### Mismatch
Both ends of a link must agree on:
- **Fiber type:** single-mode optic ↔ single-mode fiber ↔ single-mode optic (same for MM).
- **Wavelength:** e.g., 850 nm (MM) vs. 1310/1550 nm (SM) — both ends must match.
- **Speed/protocol:** 1G SFP ↔ 1G SFP; a 1G SFP and a 10G SFP+ generally won't link. Ethernet vs. Fibre Channel optics are not interchangeable.
- Also: vendor compatibility/coding (some switches reject third-party optics) and connector type (LC/SC).

Symptom: link never comes up, or comes up with errors, despite good fiber. Check both transceiver part numbers/specs.

#### Signal strength
Optical power must land within the receiver's accepted range. Measure with a **light meter/OPM** (or the transceiver's built-in **DDM/DOM** diagnostics: TX/RX power in dBm). Compare measured **dB loss** against the link's **loss budget**.
- **Too weak:** distance too long for the optic, dirty/scratched connectors (clean them — the #1 real-world fiber fix), tight bends (macrobend loss), bad splices, wrong (lower-power) optic.
- **Too strong:** a long-haul high-power optic on a very short run can **overload/saturate** the receiver — insert an **attenuator**.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** More power is not always better. A long-range single-mode transceiver on a 2 m patch in the same rack can overdrive the far receiver, causing errors — the fix is an **attenuator**, not a stronger optic.

## Symptom → Likely Cause → Fix

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| No link on new fiber run; TX light verified at both ends | TX/RX transposed (polarity) | Swap A/B strands at one end |
| Yellow patch cord on one end, aqua on the other; no link | Single-mode/multimode mismatch | Use matching fiber type and transceivers end to end |
| 10G link with errors on a 90 m Cat 6 run | Cat 6 supports 10G only to ~55 m | Re-cable with Cat 6a (or fiber) |
| Errors spike whenever the elevator/motor runs | EMI on UTP | Reroute, use STP, or use fiber |
| Wiremap passes but gigabit link slow with heavy crosstalk | Split pair | Re-terminate with correct pairing |
| Only 100 Mbps negotiated on a gigabit NIC | Damaged pair (only 2 pairs working) or old Cat 5 / bad termination | Test cable; re-terminate or replace |
| Link works but very slow; late collisions, runts, CRC errors | Duplex mismatch | Set both ends to auto (or match manually) |
| Giants incrementing on an interface | MTU/jumbo frame mismatch | Make MTU consistent end to end |
| Drops incrementing, no CRC errors | Congestion / oversubscribed link | Add bandwidth, QoS, fix bottleneck |
| Port went down when user plugged in a personal switch | BPDU guard/port security → err-disabled | Remove offending device, then shut/no shut |
| Port shows administratively down | Deliberately shut in config | `no shutdown` if it should be active |
| LACP member port shows suspended | Channel parameter mismatch | Match speed/duplex/VLAN/mode on all members |
| New phones won't power on; existing cameras reboot randomly | PoE power budget exceeded | Check `show power inline`; add budget/spread load |
| AP powers up but disables radios / underperforms | Incorrect PoE standard (needs at/bt, port gives af) | Use a port/injector with the required standard |
| Fiber link errors; RX power below spec | Attenuation: dirty connectors, bends, distance | Clean/inspect, fix bends, verify loss budget, correct optic |
| Fiber link errors on very short run with long-haul optics | Receiver overload (signal too strong) | Add an inline attenuator |

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Fiber: SM = small core, laser, 1310/1550 nm, yellow, long distance; MM = big core, 850 nm, orange/aqua, short distance. Never mix.
- [ ] Cable categories: Cat 5 = 100 Mbps/100 MHz; Cat 5e = 1G/100 MHz; Cat 6 = 1G (10G @ 55 m)/250 MHz; Cat 6a = 10G @ 100 m/500 MHz; Cat 7 = shielded/600 MHz; Cat 8 = 25/40G @ 30 m/2 GHz.
- [ ] Copper distance limit: 100 m. Attenuation = dB loss over distance; crosstalk = pair-to-pair coupling (NEXT/FEXT); EMI = external noise → STP/fiber/reroute.
- [ ] Termination faults: opens, shorts, miswires, split pairs (wiremap-passing but crosstalk-failing). 568A one end + 568B other = crossover.
- [ ] TX/RX transposed: auto-MDI-X fixes it on copper; on fiber, swap strands at one end.
- [ ] Counters: CRC = corruption; runts < 64 B; giants > 1518 B; drops = congestion. Duplex mismatch → slow link + runts/CRC/late collisions.
- [ ] Port states: err-disabled (switch-triggered violation), administratively down (config), suspended (LACP mismatch).
- [ ] PoE: af/Type 1 = 15.4 W (12.95 W PD); at/PoE+/Type 2 = 30 W (25.5 W); bt/PoE++ Type 3 = 60 W; Type 4 = 90 W. Budget exceeded → random reboots/no power; wrong standard → underpowered device.
- [ ] Transceivers must match wavelength, fiber type, and speed; verify optical signal strength against the loss budget (attenuator if too hot, clean/shorten if too weak).

## Self-Check Questions

1. A technician must run 10 Gbps to a workstation 80 meters from the closet using copper. Which minimum cable category is required?
2. An interface shows rapidly incrementing runts and CRC errors, and users say the connection is extremely slow. What is the most likely cause?
3. A switch port powering a security camera went into err-disabled state. What should the technician do?
4. A newly installed 802.3at access point powers on but disables one radio and logs low-power warnings. What are the two most likely PoE causes?
5. A duplex multimode fiber link between two switches shows no link, but a visual fault locator confirms light passes through both strands. What is the most likely fix?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Cat 6a** — Cat 6 only carries 10GBASE-T to about 55 m; Cat 6a carries it the full 100 m (Cat 8 is limited to 30 m, for data centers).
2. **Duplex mismatch** — one side full, one side half duplex, causing late collisions that appear as runts and CRC errors with severe slowdown.
3. **Identify and fix the underlying violation first** (e.g., port security or BPDU guard trigger), then bring the port back with `shutdown` / `no shutdown` (or errdisable recovery).
4. Either the port supplies the **incorrect standard** (802.3af instead of 802.3at), or the switch's **PoE power budget is exceeded**, so the AP can't draw its full 25.5 W. Check `show power inline`.
5. **Swap the TX/RX strands (fiber polarity) at one end** — the transmitters are pointed at each other's transmitters.
