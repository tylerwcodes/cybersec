# Objective 5.1 — Explain the troubleshooting methodology

Domain 5 (Network Troubleshooting) is the **largest domain on the N10-009 exam at 24%**, and objective 5.1 is its backbone. CompTIA's 7-step troubleshooting methodology is a structured, repeatable process for diagnosing and fixing network problems — and the exam tests it *relentlessly*. The most common question format is: "A technician has just done X. What should the technician do **NEXT**?" To answer these, you must know the steps **in exact order** and know which sub-tasks belong to which step. You will also see scenario questions where a technician skips a step or does steps out of order, and you must identify the mistake. Beyond the exam, this methodology maps directly to real-world help desk and NOC workflows, so internalizing it pays off twice.

**The 7 steps, in exact order (memorize this):**

| # | Step | One-line summary |
|---|------|------------------|
| 1 | **Identify the problem** | Gather information, question users, identify symptoms, determine what changed, duplicate the problem, approach multiple problems individually |
| 2 | **Establish a theory of probable cause** | Question the obvious; consider top-to-bottom/bottom-to-top OSI or divide and conquer |
| 3 | **Test the theory to determine the cause** | Confirmed → determine next steps; not confirmed → new theory or escalate |
| 4 | **Establish a plan of action** to resolve the problem **and identify potential effects** | Plan the fix, schedule change windows, plan rollback |
| 5 | **Implement the solution or escalate** as necessary | Execute the plan (or hand off if beyond your authority/skill) |
| 6 | **Verify full system functionality** and implement **preventive measures** if applicable | Confirm everything works end to end; prevent recurrence |
| 7 | **Document findings, actions, outcomes, and lessons learned** | Update the knowledge base/ticket (documentation happens throughout, formalized last) |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** A popular mnemonic: "**I** **E**at **T**acos **E**very **I**mportant **V**acation **D**ay" → Identify, Establish theory, Test, Establish plan, Implement, Verify, Document. Build your own if that doesn't stick — but you *must* be able to reproduce the order cold.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** There are **two "Establish" steps**: step 2 (establish a *theory*) and step 4 (establish a *plan of action*). Exam answer choices exploit this. Theory comes **before** testing; the plan of action comes **after** the theory is confirmed.

## Step 1 — Identify the Problem

You cannot fix what you have not defined. This step is pure information gathering — **no changes are made yet**.

### Gather information
Collect objective data: error messages, logs (syslog, event viewer, switch logs), monitoring dashboards, interface counters, recent alerts. Note the scope: one user? One department? One VLAN? Everyone? Scope drives the theory later (one user → local issue; whole subnet → gateway/switch/DHCP issue).

### Question users
Interview the people reporting the issue. Ask **open-ended** questions first ("What were you doing when it stopped working?"), then closed questions to narrow down ("Can you print? Can you reach the internet?"). Users report symptoms in non-technical language ("the internet is down") — your job is to translate that into technical facts (DNS failure? Gateway down? One website down?).

### Identify symptoms
Distinguish the *reported* symptom from the *actual* symptoms. "Email is down" might really be "DNS resolution fails for all external names." List every observable symptom — slow logins, timeouts, intermittent drops — because the pattern of symptoms points at the cause.

### Determine if anything has changed
The single highest-yield diagnostic question in networking: **"What changed?"** New firewall rule? Switch firmware update last night? New DHCP scope? Office furniture moved (crushed cable)? Check change management records — a properly run shop logs every change, which is exactly why Domain 3's change management processes exist.

### Duplicate the problem, if possible
If you can reproduce the failure on demand (in production carefully, or in a lab/test environment), you can verify symptoms first-hand and later prove the fix worked. Intermittent problems that cannot be duplicated are the hardest to solve — gather more data (packet captures, monitoring) until a pattern emerges.

### Approach multiple problems individually
If several issues are reported at once, **split them into separate problems** and troubleshoot each one on its own. They may share a root cause (you'll discover that through evidence), but bundling unrelated symptoms leads to false theories. Also: never assume a new report is the same as an existing ticket.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Anything involving *asking, gathering, scoping, or reproducing* belongs to Step 1. If the scenario says "the technician asked the user whether anything changed recently," the technician is in Step 1 — the NEXT step is to establish a theory of probable cause.

## Step 2 — Establish a Theory of Probable Cause

Now form a hypothesis: the most likely explanation that fits all the evidence. Do not commit to it yet — that's Step 3's job.

### Question the obvious
Start with simple, likely causes before exotic ones: Is it plugged in? Is the port enabled? Is the Wi-Fi toggle off? Caps Lock on during login? Correct SSID? A huge fraction of real incidents are "obvious" failures. The exam loves scenarios where the technician overlooks the obvious (e.g., theorizing a routing protocol failure when the patch cable is unplugged).

### Consider multiple approaches
Structured elimination beats guessing. CompTIA names these approaches explicitly:

| Approach | How it works | When it shines |
|----------|--------------|----------------|
| **Bottom-to-top (bottom-up) OSI** | Start at Layer 1 (cables, link lights, transceivers) and work up through Layers 2, 3 … 7 | Physical symptoms: no link light, no connectivity at all |
| **Top-to-bottom (top-down) OSI** | Start at Layer 7 (application) and work down | Application-specific symptoms: one app fails, everything else works |
| **Divide and conquer** | Start at a middle layer (commonly Layer 3 — e.g., `ping`) and move up or down based on the result | Fastest when you have a hint; a successful ping instantly rules out Layers 1–3 |

Example of divide and conquer: `ping 8.8.8.8` succeeds but `ping google.com` fails → Layers 1–3 are fine; the problem is above (DNS). Go up the stack. If the ping had failed, go down.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Don't confuse the OSI approaches (which layer to *start* at) with the theory itself. "Top-to-bottom," "bottom-to-top," and "divide and conquer" are all sub-methods **inside Step 2**.

## Step 3 — Test the Theory to Determine the Cause

Perform a targeted, minimally invasive test that proves or disproves the theory. Testing is *diagnosis*, not *repair* — swap the patch cable to a known-good one, ping the gateway, check the interface counters, try a different port.

### If the theory is confirmed
Determine the **next steps to resolve the problem** — which flows straight into Step 4 (establish a plan of action).

### If the theory is NOT confirmed
Two options: **establish a new theory** (loop back to Step 2 with what you learned) or **escalate** to someone with more expertise, more access, or vendor support. You do not barrel forward with a disproven theory, and you do not skip to implementing random fixes.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Escalation legitimately appears in **two places**: Step 3 (theory can't be confirmed / beyond your ability to diagnose) and Step 5 (solution is beyond your authority or skill to implement). If a question says "the technician cannot determine the cause," the answer is *escalate or form a new theory* — not "establish a plan of action."

## Step 4 — Establish a Plan of Action and Identify Potential Effects

With the cause confirmed, plan the fix **before touching production**:

- **The plan:** exact steps of the fix (replace the cable, correct the VLAN, update firmware, expand the DHCP scope).
- **Potential effects:** what could go wrong or be disrupted? Will a switch reboot drop 200 users? Does the change need a **maintenance window** and **change-management approval**? What is the **rollback plan** if the fix fails?
- Consider alternatives: repair vs. replace vs. workaround, cost and downtime tradeoffs.

This step ties directly to Domain 3's change management: impact assessment, approval, scheduled downtime, and backout plans all live here.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Jumping from "theory confirmed" (Step 3) straight to "implement" skips Step 4. If a scenario says the technician confirmed a faulty switch is the cause, the NEXT step is to **establish a plan of action** (and identify effects) — *not* to swap the switch immediately.

## Step 5 — Implement the Solution or Escalate as Necessary

Execute the plan — during the approved window, following the documented steps, changing **one thing at a time** so you know what actually fixed it. If the fix requires authority, skills, or access you don't have (core router config, ISP-side circuit issue, vendor RMA), **escalate** to the appropriate team and support them as needed.

## Step 6 — Verify Full System Functionality and Implement Preventive Measures

The fix isn't done when the symptom disappears — it's done when the **full system** works:

- Verify from the **user's perspective**: have the original reporter confirm their workflow works end to end.
- Verify you didn't **break something else**: check adjacent systems, monitoring dashboards, error counters.
- **Implement preventive measures** so it doesn't recur: cable management to prevent another crushed cable, UPS for the switch that failed on power blips, monitoring/alerting on the interface, user training, configuration backups, tightening change control.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Have the user confirm they can access the application" and "implement preventive measures" are both Step 6. If a scenario ends with the fix applied and asks what's NEXT, the answer is **verify full functionality** — and only after that, document.

## Step 7 — Document Findings, Actions, Outcomes, and Lessons Learned

Record the problem, root cause, actions taken, outcome, and lessons learned in the ticketing system/knowledge base. Good documentation turns a 4-hour investigation into a 10-minute lookup next time, feeds trend analysis (the same switch failing monthly is a pattern), and supports audits. Note CompTIA's exact wording: document **throughout the process** — you take notes at every step — but formal documentation is finalized as the last step.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Documentation is *always* the last step in ordering questions, but never treat it as optional or skippable. If an answer choice says "close the ticket without documenting," it is wrong.

## Worked Scenario — All 7 Steps End to End

**Situation:** Monday morning, three users in Accounting report they "can't get on the network."

1. **Identify the problem.** You question the users: they all sit in the same corner of the 3rd floor; problems started this morning; nothing changed on their machines. Symptoms: all three get `169.254.x.x` addresses (APIPA — no DHCP). You check the change log: **an electrician installed new outlets in that area over the weekend**. Other 3rd-floor users are fine. You try to duplicate it: plugging a laptop into one of the affected wall jacks also fails to get an address. (Two other unrelated tickets came in — a printer jam and a VPN question — you keep them as separate problems.)
2. **Establish a theory.** Question the obvious first: are the patch cables plugged in? Yes. Scope (three adjacent jacks, weekend electrical work, no link light on one PC) suggests Layer 1 — bottom-to-top approach. Theory: the electrician disturbed or damaged the cable runs serving those jacks.
3. **Test the theory.** In the IDF, you tone the affected jacks and find one punchdown knocked loose; a cable tester on the runs shows an open pair on all three. **Theory confirmed** → determine next steps.
4. **Establish a plan of action / identify effects.** Plan: re-terminate the three runs at the patch panel and retest. Potential effects: brief downtime only for those three jacks; no maintenance window needed, but you note it in the ticket and inform the users. Rollback: not applicable (nothing works now); spare cabling available if re-termination fails.
5. **Implement the solution.** You re-punch the terminations and certify each run with the cable tester (wiremap passes).
6. **Verify full system functionality / preventive measures.** All three PCs now get DHCP leases and can reach file shares, printers, and the internet; users confirm. Preventive measure: you add a policy that facilities work near network cabling requires IT notification, and you label the punchdown block.
7. **Document.** Ticket updated: root cause (terminations disturbed during electrical work), actions (re-terminated, certified), outcome (resolved), lesson learned (coordinate facilities work with IT). Knowledge-base article created.

## Symptom → Likely Cause → Fix (Methodology-Flavored)

| Scenario symptom | Where you are / likely cause | What to do NEXT |
|---|---|---|
| Technician just finished questioning users about what changed | Step 1 complete | Establish a theory of probable cause |
| Technician suspects a bad patch cable | Step 2 complete (theory formed) | Test the theory (swap in a known-good cable) |
| Swapping the cable did NOT fix it | Step 3 — theory not confirmed | Establish a new theory or escalate |
| Cause confirmed: failing switch uplink module | Step 3 confirmed | Establish a plan of action and identify potential effects |
| Plan approved in the change window | Step 4 complete | Implement the solution (or escalate if beyond authority) |
| Replacement implemented, link is up | Step 5 complete | Verify full system functionality; implement preventive measures |
| Users confirm everything works | Step 6 complete | Document findings, actions, outcomes, lessons learned |
| Two unrelated issues reported in one ticket | Step 1 principle | Approach multiple problems individually |
| Fix requires core-router access you don't have | Step 5 branch | Escalate to the appropriate team |

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Know the 7 steps **in exact order**: Identify → Establish theory → Test theory → Establish plan (+ potential effects) → Implement/escalate → Verify (+ preventive measures) → Document.
- [ ] Step 1 sub-tasks: gather info, question users, identify symptoms, determine what changed, duplicate if possible, approach multiple problems individually.
- [ ] Step 2 includes "question the obvious" and the approaches: top-to-bottom OSI, bottom-to-top OSI, divide and conquer.
- [ ] Step 3 branches: confirmed → next steps to resolve; not confirmed → new theory **or escalate**.
- [ ] Escalation can happen at Step 3 (can't determine cause) and Step 5 (can't/shouldn't implement).
- [ ] Step 4 = plan **and** potential effects (change management, rollback); Step 6 = verify full functionality **and** preventive measures.
- [ ] Documentation is the final step but notes are taken **throughout** the process.
- [ ] "What should the technician do NEXT?" questions = identify the current step, answer with the following step.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A technician has confirmed that a misconfigured VLAN is causing the reported outage. According to the troubleshooting methodology, what should the technician do next?
2. Which troubleshooting approach starts testing at Layer 3 (e.g., pinging the default gateway) and then moves up or down the OSI model based on the result?
3. During which step does the technician ask users, "Has anything changed recently?"
4. A technician's theory could not be confirmed after testing. Name the two valid options the methodology allows.
5. After implementing a fix, a technician confirms with the user that email now works, then enables interface monitoring so the failure will be caught early if it recurs. Which step is this, and what remains?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Establish a plan of action to resolve the problem and identify potential effects** (Step 4). Confirming the cause ends Step 3; implementation comes only after planning.
2. **Divide and conquer** — start in the middle of the OSI model and let the test result tell you which direction to go.
3. **Step 1 — Identify the problem.** "Determine if anything has changed" is one of its listed sub-tasks, along with gathering information, questioning users, identifying symptoms, duplicating the problem, and approaching multiple problems individually.
4. **Establish a new theory** or **escalate**. You never proceed to plan/implement on an unconfirmed theory.
5. This is **Step 6 — Verify full system functionality and implement preventive measures**. What remains is **Step 7 — Document findings, actions, outcomes, and lessons learned**.
