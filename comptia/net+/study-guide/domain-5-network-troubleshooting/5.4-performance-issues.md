# Objective 5.4 — Given a scenario, troubleshoot common performance issues

"The network is slow" is the most common complaint a network technician hears — and the least specific. Objective 5.4 gives you the vocabulary and diagnostic logic to decompose "slow" into measurable problems: congestion, bottlenecks, insufficient bandwidth vs. poor throughput, latency, packet loss, and jitter — plus the wireless-specific culprits: interference and channel overlap, signal degradation, coverage holes, client disassociation, and roaming misconfiguration. The exam tests this two ways: definition-level ("which metric describes variation in delay?") and scenario-level ("VoIP calls are choppy but file transfers are fine — what's the cause?"). Know which metric each symptom maps to and which application types are sensitive to which metric (real-time voice/video care about latency, loss, and jitter; bulk transfers care about bandwidth/throughput).

## Congestion/Contention

- **Congestion:** more traffic is offered than the network path can carry — queues fill, then packets are delayed (latency ↑, jitter ↑) and finally dropped (loss ↑). Think rush hour. Typical causes: oversubscribed uplinks/WAN circuits, backup jobs during business hours, broadcast storms, elephant flows.
- **Contention:** multiple devices **competing for the same shared resource/medium**. Classic contexts: shared media (all Wi-Fi is contention-based — CSMA/CA airtime; historically hub-based Ethernet with CSMA/CD), oversubscribed uplinks (a 48-port gigabit switch with a single 1 Gbps uplink has 48:1 contention), and ISP contention ratios on broadband.

Symptoms: slowness that **follows a schedule** (worst at peak hours, fine at night), rising latency and drops on the busy link's interface counters (queue drops), everything on one path degraded at once.
Fixes: add capacity (bigger uplink, link aggregation), **QoS** to prioritize critical traffic, traffic shaping/scheduling (move backups to off-hours), segment the network to reduce shared load.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Performance is terrible every day from 9–11 a.m. and fine after hours" = **congestion/utilization problem**, not a broken cable. Baseline comparisons (Domain 3) and interface utilization graphs are how you prove it.

## Bottlenecking

A **bottleneck** is the single slowest component that caps end-to-end performance — the narrow neck of the bottle. Everything is only as fast as the weakest hop or device: a 100 Mbps switch port between two gigabit segments, an old firewall that can't inspect at line rate, a single overloaded server NIC/disk/CPU, a WAN circuit far smaller than the LAN feeding it.

Troubleshooting is a hunt: **measure each segment/component along the path** (interface utilization/counters, iperf between segments, device CPU/memory) and find where performance collapses. Then fix *that* — upgrading anything else changes nothing.

- Watch for the sneaky ones: a NIC that autonegotiated down to 100 Mbps (or half duplex), an inline appliance (IDS/firewall) at its throughput ceiling, storage or CPU on the server (a *system* bottleneck masquerading as a network problem).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Upgrading the WAN circuit won't help if the bottleneck is the old firewall behind it. Exam scenarios reward identifying the **slowest element in the path**, not the most expensive upgrade.

## Bandwidth

**Bandwidth** is the *theoretical maximum* data rate of a link (the size of the pipe — e.g., 1 Gbps Ethernet, 500 Mbps circuit).

### Throughput capacity
**Throughput** is the *actual measured* rate you achieve through the pipe — always **≤ bandwidth**, reduced by protocol overhead, congestion, errors/retransmissions, latency (TCP window effects), duplex problems, and device limits. **Goodput** goes one step further: the rate of *useful application data* delivered, excluding headers and retransmitted bytes (goodput ≤ throughput ≤ bandwidth).

| Term | Meaning | Analogy |
|---|---|---|
| **Bandwidth** | Theoretical capacity of the link | Width of the highway |
| **Throughput** | Actual data successfully moved per second | Cars actually getting through per hour |
| **Goodput** | Application-useful data per second (minus overhead/retransmits) | Passengers delivered (not counting empty seats) |

Diagnosing "we pay for 500 Mbps but get 80": run a **speed test/iperf** at different points (at the modem/router vs. deep in the LAN) to isolate whether the shortfall is the ISP, the path, or the endpoint; check duplex/speed negotiation; check for high latency (long fat networks throttle single TCP streams); check utilization by other traffic.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Definitions are directly tested: bandwidth = **theoretical** capacity; throughput = **actual** achieved rate. If a question contrasts "the rated speed of the link" with "what users actually experience," it's this pair.

## Latency

**Latency** is delay — how long a packet takes to get from source to destination, usually discussed as **RTT (round-trip time)** as reported by `ping`. Components:
- **Propagation delay:** travel time over distance (speed of light limits — the dominant term on long links). A **geostationary satellite** hop (~35,786 km up) adds roughly 250 ms one way — **~500–600 ms RTT** — which no upgrade can remove; it's physics.
- **Serialization/transmission delay:** time to clock bits onto the wire (worse on slow links).
- **Processing delay:** routers/firewalls/inspection devices thinking about each packet.
- **Queuing delay:** waiting in buffers behind other traffic (the congestion contribution — the variable part that also creates jitter).

**Sensitive applications:** VoIP (one-way delay ideally < 150 ms; noticeable talk-over above that), video conferencing, online gaming, remote desktop, financial trading. Bulk transfers tolerate latency but TCP throughput still drops on high-RTT paths.
**Diagnosis:** `ping` (RTT and consistency), `traceroute` (which hop the delay appears at).
**Fixes:** shorter/better paths, remove or upgrade slow processing devices, QoS priority queuing for real-time traffic, local caching/CDNs, avoid satellite for interactive traffic (use terrestrial/LEO).

## Packet Loss

Packets sent but never delivered. Even 1–2% loss noticeably degrades TCP throughput (retransmissions + congestion-window collapse) and real-time media (gaps, artifacts, robotic audio).

**Causes:** **congestion** (queue overflow — the most common), **faulty hardware** (failing NIC, transceiver, switch port), **cabling/signal errors** (CRC-failed frames are discarded — bad cable, EMI, duplex mismatch), wireless interference/weak signal, misconfigured QoS policers dropping traffic, MTU black holes.
**Diagnosis:** `ping` statistics ("Lost = 3 (3% loss)"), extended/continuous ping while wiggling variables, per-hop loss patterns in traceroute/pathping/mtr, interface counters (drops = congestion; CRC/errors = physical).
**Fixes:** relieve congestion or apply QoS (protect voice), replace bad hardware/cable, fix duplex/MTU, improve wireless signal.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** In traceroute output, `* * *` at a *middle* hop that still shows later hops responding usually means that router **deprioritizes/blocks ICMP replies**, not real loss. Real loss shows up end-to-end. Don't declare an outage from one silent hop.

## Jitter

**Jitter** is the **variation in delay** between packets — packets arriving at uneven intervals even if average latency is fine. Caused by queuing variability, congestion, route changes ("route flapping"), and interface buffering differences.

- **Who cares:** real-time streams. VoIP and video reassemble a steady stream; irregular arrival causes **choppy, garbled audio, gaps, and dropped syllables**. File transfers and web browsing barely notice jitter.
- **Target:** for good VoIP, keep jitter **< 30 ms** (and one-way latency < 150 ms, loss < 1%).
- **Mitigations:** **QoS** (priority queue voice so it never waits behind bulk traffic — e.g., EF/DSCP 46 marking), **jitter buffers** on endpoints (smooth arrival at the cost of a little added delay), adequate bandwidth, stable routing.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The fingerprint: "**VoIP calls are choppy/robotic but downloads and email are fast**" = jitter (or loss/latency) on real-time traffic → the answer is **implement QoS** (and/or jitter buffers). If the metric name is asked: *variation in delay = jitter*.

## Wireless

Wireless adds a hostile physical layer: shared airtime, interference, and attenuation through the building itself. A **site survey** (predictive and/or on-site with a Wi-Fi analyzer) is the master diagnostic for most wireless complaints.

### Interference

#### Channel overlap
- **2.4 GHz** has only **three non-overlapping channels: 1, 6, 11** (each channel is 20+ MHz wide but channels are spaced 5 MHz apart). Neighboring APs must be planned onto 1/6/11 in a honeycomb.
  - **Co-channel interference (CCI):** two APs on the *same* channel — they at least cooperate (CSMA/CA takes turns), so it costs airtime/throughput ("contention").
  - **Adjacent-channel interference (ACI):** APs on *overlapping* channels (e.g., 6 and 8) — they can't coordinate and corrupt each other's frames. **Worse than co-channel.**
- **5 GHz / 6 GHz** offer many more non-overlapping channels (bonded 40/80/160 MHz widths reintroduce overlap — use narrower channels in dense deployments; mind DFS channels near radar).
- **Non-Wi-Fi interference** in 2.4 GHz: microwave ovens, Bluetooth, cordless phones, baby monitors, wireless cameras — shows as noise/high channel utilization on a **spectrum/Wi-Fi analyzer**.

**Symptoms:** low throughput and retransmissions despite strong signal; performance drops when neighbors are busy (or the breakroom microwave runs).
**Fixes:** re-plan channels (1/6/11), reduce channel width, adjust AP power, move to 5/6 GHz, remove/relocate interferers.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** An AP set to channel 3 "to avoid the crowd on 1 and 6" makes things **worse** — it now partially overlaps both. In 2.4 GHz, stick to **1, 6, 11**; adjacent-channel overlap is worse than sharing a channel.

### Signal degradation or loss

RF weakens with distance (free-space path loss) and with every obstacle: drywall a little; **concrete, brick, metal (ducts, mesh, appliances, elevators), low-E glass, water (aquariums, people)** a lot. Signal strength is reported as **RSSI in dBm** — negative numbers, closer to 0 is stronger, and the scale is logarithmic (every −3 dB ≈ half the power):

| RSSI | Quality | Suitable for |
|---|---|---|
| **−30 dBm** | Excellent (practically next to the AP) | Anything |
| **−50 dBm** | Great | Anything |
| **−60 dBm** | Good | Most uses |
| **−67 dBm** | **Minimum for VoIP/video streaming** (common design target) | Real-time apps |
| **−70 dBm** | Fair — light browsing/email | Marginal |
| **−80 dBm** | Poor — unreliable association | Barely usable |
| **−90 dBm** | Unusable — noise floor territory | Nothing |

Weak signal → clients downshift to slower data rates, retransmit more (which eats airtime for everyone), and eventually drop.
**Diagnosis:** Wi-Fi analyzer/site survey heatmap; check client RSSI at the complaint location.
**Fixes:** reposition APs (central, ceiling, away from metal), adjust antenna orientation/type, raise (carefully) or rebalance power, add APs, remove/route around obstructions.

### Insufficient wireless coverage

Dead zones and fringe areas: symptoms are location-specific — "Wi-Fi is fine at my desk but drops in the conference room/stairwell/warehouse corner."
**Diagnosis:** a **site survey** with heatmapping walks the space and maps RSSI per location; it reveals gaps between AP cells.
**Fixes:** add APs where the gaps are, reposition existing APs, correct antenna choice (omni vs. directional/patch for aisles and long halls), modest power increases — but note that cranking AP power doesn't help clients whose weaker radios can't talk *back* (asymmetric power problem).

### Client disassociation issues

Clients that associate, then drop — repeatedly. Causes to check:
- **Interference/weak signal** at the client's location (see above) — the most common.
- **Client power-save/driver issues:** aggressive NIC power management or buggy drivers drop the association; update drivers, disable aggressive power saving.
- **Roaming behavior:** client hopping between APs (see roaming below) appears as brief disconnects.
- **Security/configuration mismatches:** wrong PSK entered, 802.1X/RADIUS timeouts or certificate problems, mismatched security modes — the client authenticates then gets kicked, or cycles.
- **AP-side limits:** max client counts, band-steering quirks, DFS radar events forcing 5 GHz channel changes (everyone drops at once), firmware bugs.
- **Malicious:** **deauthentication attacks** spoof deauth frames to knock clients off (Domain 4 tie-in) — many clients disassociating repeatedly with no RF explanation should raise this flag; 802.11w (PMF) mitigates.

**Diagnosis:** correlate AP/WLC logs (reason codes) with client-side events; Wi-Fi analyzer for RF conditions at the client's spot.

### Roaming misconfiguration

Roaming = a moving client leaving one AP for the next. Design and misconfiguration issues:
- **Insufficient cell overlap:** APs too far apart → dead gap between cells; guideline is roughly **15–20% overlap** between adjacent AP cells so clients transition smoothly.
- **Too much overlap/power too high:** clients hear too many APs, roam constantly or stay confused; co-channel interference rises.
- **Sticky clients:** the *client* decides when to roam, and many cling to the original AP long after a closer one is better — symptom: "full bars near AP-2 but still connected to distant AP-1 with terrible speed." Mitigations: **minimum RSSI / client kick thresholds** on the infrastructure (AP disassociates clients below e.g. −75 dBm), lower AP power so distant clients let go, 802.11k/v assisted roaming.
- **Fast roaming not enabled:** with 802.1X, a full reauthentication at every AP (hundreds of ms) drops VoIP calls mid-walk; **802.11r (Fast BSS Transition)** pre-establishes keys so handoffs are quick. Awareness of 802.11k (neighbor reports) and 802.11v (BSS transition management) helps too.
- **Mismatched SSIDs/security between APs:** clients can't roam seamlessly unless the SSID and security config are identical across APs.

**Symptom fingerprints:** calls drop while walking the hall (no fast roaming / a coverage gap); laptop stays on a far AP with 1 bar (sticky client); disconnect every time users move between floors (insufficient overlap).

## Symptom → Likely Cause → Fix

| Symptom | Likely cause | Fix |
|---|---|---|
| Slow every business morning, fine at night | Congestion/oversubscription on a shared link | Capacity upgrade, QoS, reschedule bulk jobs |
| Whole path capped at ~100 Mbps between gigabit segments | Bottleneck (one 100 Mbps port/device, or autoneg downshift) | Find the slowest element and fix/upgrade it |
| Paying for 500 Mbps, speed tests show 90 Mbps at the desktop but 480 at the router | LAN-side throughput problem (duplex, cabling, Wi-Fi, device limit) | Test segment by segment; fix the failing hop |
| Every connection over the satellite link feels laggy; RTT ~600 ms | Propagation latency (GEO physics) | Can't fix physics — terrestrial/LEO path, caching, latency-tolerant apps |
| `ping` shows 3% loss; TCP transfers crawl | Packet loss from congestion or physical errors | Drops counter → congestion/QoS; CRC counter → cable/EMI/duplex |
| VoIP choppy/robotic; data apps fine | Jitter (delay variation) hitting real-time traffic | QoS priority for voice, jitter buffers; target jitter < 30 ms |
| Strong Wi-Fi signal but low throughput; neighbor APs on channels 4 and 8 | Adjacent-channel interference (2.4 GHz overlap) | Re-plan to channels 1/6/11; consider 5 GHz |
| Wi-Fi dies when the microwave runs | Non-Wi-Fi 2.4 GHz interference | Move to 5 GHz, relocate AP/appliance |
| Good signal at desks, none in far conference room | Insufficient coverage (cell gap) | Site survey; add/reposition APs |
| RSSI −80 dBm at complaint location | Signal degradation (distance/obstacles) | Reposition/add APs; design to −67 dBm for VoIP |
| Laptops randomly drop and rejoin Wi-Fi at one desk cluster | Interference/weak signal, power-save/driver, or deauth attack | Analyze RF, update drivers, check WLC logs/PMF |
| Full bars near new AP but stuck on distant AP, slow | Sticky client / roaming misconfiguration | Min-RSSI kick, lower AP power, 802.11k/v/r |
| VoIP call drops when walking between APs (802.1X WLAN) | Full reauth on roam — no fast roaming | Enable 802.11r fast BSS transition; verify 15–20% overlap |

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Congestion = too much traffic for the path; contention = competing for a shared medium/uplink. Both show time-of-day patterns; fix with capacity + QoS.
- [ ] Bottleneck = the slowest element caps everything; measure hop by hop and fix that element.
- [ ] Bandwidth = theoretical capacity; throughput = actual achieved; goodput = useful data only.
- [ ] Latency = delay (RTT); propagation (distance — GEO satellite ≈ 500+ ms RTT), serialization, processing, queuing. VoIP wants one-way < 150 ms.
- [ ] Packet loss: congestion (drops) vs. physical errors (CRC) vs. wireless; even a few % wrecks TCP and voice.
- [ ] Jitter = variation in delay; kills VoIP/video; target < 30 ms; fix with QoS and jitter buffers.
- [ ] 2.4 GHz: only channels 1/6/11 don't overlap; adjacent-channel interference is worse than co-channel.
- [ ] RSSI: −30 excellent … **−67 dBm design floor for VoIP/video** … −80 poor/unusable. dBm is logarithmic.
- [ ] Coverage gaps → site survey, add/reposition APs; disassociations → RF, drivers/power-save, security mismatch, deauth attack.
- [ ] Roaming: ~15–20% cell overlap, fight sticky clients with min-RSSI/power tuning, 802.11r for fast secure roams.

## Self-Check Questions

1. Users say video calls stutter and freeze while large file downloads remain fast. Which performance metric is most likely the problem, and what is the standard mitigation?
2. What is the difference between bandwidth and throughput, and which one does a speed test measure?
3. An office's 2.4 GHz APs are set to channels 2, 6, and 9. What is wrong and what should the channels be?
4. A remote site connected by geostationary satellite complains that every interactive session feels sluggish, though bulk transfers eventually complete. Why can't more bandwidth fix this?
5. Employees walking from the lobby to the far wing keep their laptops connected to the lobby AP at −78 dBm even though closer APs are available. Name the phenomenon and two infrastructure-side mitigations.

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Jitter** — variation in packet delay disrupts real-time streams but not bulk TCP transfers. Mitigate with **QoS priority queuing for voice/video** and jitter buffers; keep jitter under ~30 ms.
2. **Bandwidth** is the theoretical maximum capacity of the link; **throughput** is the actual rate achieved after overhead, congestion, and errors. A speed test measures **throughput**.
3. Channels 2 and 9 **overlap** adjacent channels (2.4 GHz channels are 5 MHz apart but ~20 MHz wide), causing adjacent-channel interference. Use the only non-overlapping set: **1, 6, 11**.
4. The delay is **propagation latency** — ~35,786 km each way gives roughly 500–600 ms RTT regardless of link capacity. Bandwidth adds lanes, not speed-of-light shortcuts; only a terrestrial/LEO path or latency-tolerant design helps.
5. **Sticky clients** (clients control roaming and cling to the old AP). Mitigations: configure a **minimum RSSI/client-kick threshold**, **reduce AP transmit power** so distant clients release, and enable **802.11k/v (and 802.11r for fast secure roaming)**; also verify ~15–20% cell overlap.
