# Objective 3.3 — Explain disaster recovery (DR) concepts.

Disaster recovery is the discipline of getting the business back online after fires, floods, ransomware, hardware failures, or fat-fingered changes take down production. Objective 3.3 is small but dense, and every part of it is testable: the four DR **metrics** (RPO, RTO, MTTR, MTBF), the three DR **site** types (cold/warm/hot), the two **high-availability approaches** (active-active vs. active-passive), and the two **testing** styles (tabletop vs. validation). Exam questions are almost always definitional traps — "maximum tolerable data loss" vs. "maximum tolerable downtime" — or cost/speed trade-off scenarios ("cheapest site that can be running within a day"). Nail the precise definitions and the comparisons and this objective is free points.

## DR Metrics

DR metrics quantify what the business can tolerate and how reliable systems actually are. The first two (RPO, RTO) are *objectives set by the business*; the second two (MTTR, MTBF) are *measured statistics about equipment and operations*.

### Recovery Point Objective (RPO)

**RPO = the maximum acceptable amount of DATA LOSS, measured in time.** It answers: "how far back in time can we afford to fall when we restore?" If the RPO is 4 hours, the organization can survive losing at most the last 4 hours of data — which means **backups (or replication) must run at least every 4 hours**. RPO directly drives backup frequency and technology:

- RPO of 24 hours → nightly backups suffice.
- RPO of 1 hour → hourly incremental backups or snapshots.
- RPO near zero → continuous/synchronous replication.

The smaller the RPO, the more expensive the solution.

### Recovery Time Objective (RTO)

**RTO = the maximum acceptable DOWNTIME — the target time to restore service** after a disaster or outage. It answers: "how long can the business survive with this system down?" An RTO of 2 hours means service must be running again within 2 hours of the failure. RTO drives the recovery *infrastructure*: a small RTO demands hot sites, automated failover, and rehearsed procedures; a long RTO can tolerate rebuilding servers from scratch.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The single most-tested distinction in this objective: **RPO = how much DATA you can lose (drives backup frequency). RTO = how much TIME you can be down (drives recovery speed/site choice).** Memory hook: R**P**O = **P**oint-in-time of your last good data; R**T**O = **T**ime until you're back up.

### Mean Time To Repair (MTTR)

**MTTR = the average time required to repair a failed component or system and return it to service** — measured from failure to restoration, including diagnosis, fix, and verification. MTTR measures *maintainability and support responsiveness*. Organizations lower MTTR with spare parts on hand, good documentation, monitoring that speeds diagnosis, and vendor support contracts (an SLA may promise a 4-hour MTTR). Compare: RTO is the *goal* you set; MTTR is the *measured average* of how long repairs actually take.

### Mean Time Between Failures (MTBF)

**MTBF = the average time a (repairable) system operates between one failure and the next** — a measure of expected **reliability**. A switch with a 200,000-hour MTBF fails, on average, far less often than one rated 50,000 hours. MTBF guides purchasing decisions (buy more reliable gear for critical roles), spare-parts stocking, and proactive replacement scheduling. Higher MTBF = more reliable; lower MTTR = faster to fix. Availability improves when you raise MTBF and/or shrink MTTR (availability ≈ MTBF ÷ (MTBF + MTTR)).

| Metric | Measures | Question it answers | Drives |
|---|---|---|---|
| **RPO** | Max tolerable data loss (time) | "How much data can we lose?" | Backup/replication frequency |
| **RTO** | Max tolerable downtime | "How fast must we be back up?" | Recovery site/failover investment |
| **MTTR** | Average repair time | "How long does a fix take?" | Spares, support contracts, runbooks |
| **MTBF** | Average uptime between failures | "How reliable is it?" | Purchasing, replacement planning |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** MTTR vs. MTBF: MTTR is about the **repair** (shorter is better); MTBF is about **reliability between failures** (longer is better). And neither is the same as RTO — RTO is a business *objective*, not a measured average.

## DR Sites

A DR site is an alternate facility where operations resume if the primary site is lost. The three types form a cost-versus-recovery-speed spectrum: the faster you need to be running, the more you pay to keep the site ready.

### Cold Site

A **cold site** is essentially empty, ready real estate: building space, power, cooling, and (perhaps) basic network connectivity — but **no installed servers or current data**. After a disaster you must procure/ship hardware, install and configure everything, then restore data from backups. **Recovery time: days to weeks. Cost: lowest.** Suitable when the business can tolerate a long RTO.

### Warm Site

A **warm site** has the facility **plus equipment already installed** — servers, network gear, connectivity — but the **data is not current**: it must be restored or synchronized from recent backups, and final configuration completed, before going live. **Recovery time: hours to days. Cost: moderate.** The classic middle ground.

### Hot Site

A **hot site** is a **fully operational duplicate** of production: hardware installed and running, applications configured, and data kept current through continuous or near-real-time **replication**. Failover can happen in **minutes** (sometimes near-instantly). **Cost: highest** — you are effectively paying for a second data center. Required when RTO/RPO are very small.

| | Cold site | Warm site | Hot site |
|---|---|---|---|
| Facility (space/power/cooling) | ✔ | ✔ | ✔ |
| Hardware installed | ✘ | ✔ | ✔ |
| Data current | ✘ | ✘ (restore from backup) | ✔ (replicated) |
| Typical recovery time | Days–weeks | Hours–days | Minutes |
| Relative cost | $ | $$ | $$$ |
| Fits | Long RTO, tight budget | Moderate RTO | Near-zero RTO/RPO |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Scenario questions give budget + recovery-time requirements and ask you to pick the site. Keywords: "empty facility with power" = **cold**; "equipment in place but data must be restored" = **warm**; "fully mirrored, real-time replication, immediate failover" = **hot**. Cloud DR (DRaaS) blurs the lines by letting you keep warm-site costs with near-hot recovery, but the exam still tests the classic three.

## High-Availability Approaches

High availability (HA) keeps services running through component failures using redundancy. The two canonical arrangements:

### Active-Active

**Both (all) nodes actively handle traffic simultaneously**, typically behind a load balancer or using first-hop redundancy that load-shares. Benefits: full use of all purchased capacity, load distribution, and **near-instantaneous failover** — surviving nodes simply absorb the failed node's share. The design caveat (and a favorite exam scenario): each node must run with enough headroom that the survivors can carry the total load — two active-active nodes each running at 90% cannot absorb each other's traffic; a failure would overload the survivor.

### Active-Passive

**One node handles all traffic while a standby node waits idle**, monitoring the active node (heartbeats) and taking over when it fails. Benefits: simpler (no load-sharing state to synchronize), predictable capacity after failover (the standby carries exactly what the active did). Costs: the passive node is paid-for capacity doing nothing during normal operation, and failover causes a brief interruption while the standby takes over. Firewall HA pairs and FHRPs like HSRP/VRRP are classically active-passive.

| | Active-active | Active-passive |
|---|---|---|
| Normal operation | All nodes serve traffic | One serves; standby waits |
| Failover speed | Near-instant (already serving) | Brief interruption during takeover |
| Hardware utilization | All capacity used | Standby idle ("wasted" until failover) |
| Complexity | Higher (load balancing, state sync) | Lower |
| Watch out for | Surviving nodes must handle total load | Standby must be tested/kept in sync |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "Active-active means I get double capacity." Only until a failure — you must plan capacity so that N-1 nodes can carry the full load, or failover becomes an outage anyway.

## Testing

A DR plan that has never been tested is a hypothesis, not a plan. The exam contrasts two testing approaches:

### Tabletop Exercises

A **tabletop exercise** is a **discussion-based walkthrough**: stakeholders gather (in a conference room or virtually), a facilitator presents a disaster scenario ("ransomware just encrypted the primary SAN — go"), and the team talks through the DR plan step by step — who does what, in what order, with what dependencies. **No systems are touched and production is never at risk.** Tabletops are cheap, low-risk, and excellent at exposing gaps in the *plan itself*: missing contact numbers, unclear responsibilities, outdated assumptions, undocumented dependencies. Their limit: they can't prove the technology actually works.

### Validation Tests

**Validation testing actually exercises the recovery technology**: restoring real backups, failing over to the DR site, running production workloads on standby systems, and measuring the results against **RTO and RPO targets**. Forms range from component tests (restore one database from backup) to parallel tests (bring DR systems up alongside production) to a **full failover/cutover test** (move live operations to the DR site and back). Validation tests are the only way to prove backups are restorable and failover meets its objectives — but they cost time, money, and carry some operational risk, so they're scheduled carefully (often annually, with smaller tests quarterly).

| | Tabletop exercise | Validation test |
|---|---|---|
| Format | Facilitated discussion/walkthrough | Hands-on technical execution |
| Systems touched | None | Real backups, failover, DR systems |
| Risk / cost | Minimal | Higher (time, money, some operational risk) |
| Best at finding | Plan gaps: roles, order, contacts, assumptions | Technical failures: bad backups, broken failover, missed RTO/RPO |
| Frequency | Often (e.g., quarterly/semiannual) | Less often (e.g., annual full test) |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Keyword mapping: "discussion," "walkthrough," "no systems impacted," "review roles and procedures" → **tabletop**. "Actually fail over," "restore from backup," "verify RTO/RPO can be met" → **validation test**. Best practice is both: tabletops to keep the plan sharp, periodic validation to prove it works. Also remember the cruel classic: *a backup that has never been test-restored is not a backup.*

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] RPO = maximum acceptable **data loss** measured in time → dictates backup/replication frequency.
- [ ] RTO = maximum acceptable **downtime** → dictates recovery speed and site investment.
- [ ] MTTR = average time to **repair** and restore a failed component (lower is better).
- [ ] MTBF = average operating time **between failures**; a reliability rating (higher is better).
- [ ] Cold site = space/power only, no hardware, no data → cheapest, days-to-weeks recovery.
- [ ] Warm site = hardware installed, data must be restored → moderate cost, hours-to-days recovery.
- [ ] Hot site = fully mirrored with replicated current data → most expensive, minutes to fail over.
- [ ] Active-active = all nodes serve traffic; instant failover; must keep N-1 capacity headroom.
- [ ] Active-passive = standby waits idle; simpler; brief interruption at failover.
- [ ] Tabletop exercise = discussion-based plan walkthrough; no systems touched; finds plan gaps.
- [ ] Validation test = real restores/failovers that prove RTO and RPO can actually be met.
- [ ] Availability rises as MTBF rises and MTTR falls; site choice must match the RTO/RPO the business set.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A company performs backups nightly at 11 p.m. A server dies at 3 p.m. the next day and is rebuilt from the backup. Roughly 16 hours of data are gone. Which DR metric did this data loss test against?
2. Management declares that the ordering system may never be unavailable for more than 30 minutes. Which metric is this, and which DR site type is most appropriate?
3. What is the difference between MTBF and MTTR, and which one would you examine when choosing the more reliable of two switch models?
4. A firm wants a DR facility with servers and network gear already racked, accepting that after a disaster it will spend several hours restoring data from backups before going live. Which site type is this?
5. The IT director wants to confirm that staff know their roles and the plan's steps are in the right order — without touching any production or DR systems. Later in the year she wants proof the company can actually meet its RTO. Which test type fits each goal?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **RPO (recovery point objective)** — the amount of data lost, measured in time, is bounded by backup frequency. If 16 hours of loss is unacceptable, the RPO must be tightened and backups taken more often.
2. That is the **RTO** (maximum tolerable downtime). A 30-minute RTO effectively requires a **hot site** (or equivalent HA/replication) — warm and cold sites take hours to weeks.
3. **MTBF** is the average operating time between failures (reliability — higher is better); **MTTR** is the average time to repair after a failure (maintainability — lower is better). To compare reliability of two models, compare **MTBF**.
4. A **warm site** — facility and equipment ready, but data is not current and must be restored before operations resume (hours to days).
5. The role-and-procedure walkthrough is a **tabletop exercise** (discussion-based, zero system risk). Proving the RTO can actually be met requires a **validation test** — a real failover/restore measured against the objective.
