# Objective 1.5 — Compare and contrast transmission media and transceivers

Transmission media are the physical (or wireless) pathways that carry bits between devices, and transceivers are the pluggable modules that convert between electrical signals inside a switch/router and the signals that travel over the medium. This objective is one of the most fact-dense on the N10-009 exam: you must memorize 802.11 standards (frequencies, speeds, Wi-Fi generation names), 802.3 Ethernet standards (media, category, distance), fiber types, coax types, transceiver form factors, and connector types. The exam tests it two ways: direct recall ("Which standard operates only at 5 GHz?") and scenario selection ("A technician must connect two buildings 8 km apart — which media and transceiver?"). Comparison tables are your best friend here — study them until you can reproduce them from memory.

## Wireless

Wireless media transmit data using radio frequency (RF) energy instead of a physical cable. The three wireless families the exam names are 802.11 (Wi-Fi), cellular, and satellite.

### 802.11 standards

The IEEE 802.11 family defines wireless LAN (Wi-Fi) communication. Each amendment improves speed, efficiency, or spectrum use. Know this table cold:

| Standard | Wi-Fi Name | Frequency Band(s) | Max Theoretical Data Rate | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 802.11a | — | 5 GHz | 54 Mbps | Early standard; shorter range than b/g due to 5 GHz |
| 802.11b | — | 2.4 GHz | 11 Mbps | Slowest; uses DSSS; long range but crowded band |
| 802.11g | — | 2.4 GHz | 54 Mbps | Backward compatible with 802.11b |
| 802.11n | Wi-Fi 4 | 2.4 GHz and 5 GHz | 600 Mbps | Introduced MIMO and 40 MHz channel bonding |
| 802.11ac | Wi-Fi 5 | 5 GHz only | ~6.9 Gbps | MU-MIMO (downlink), 80/160 MHz channels, 256-QAM |
| 802.11ax | Wi-Fi 6 / Wi-Fi 6E | 2.4 GHz, 5 GHz (+6 GHz for 6E) | ~9.6 Gbps | OFDMA, uplink+downlink MU-MIMO, better efficiency in dense environments |

Key band characteristics:

- **2.4 GHz**: longer range, better wall penetration, but only 3 non-overlapping channels (1, 6, 11 in North America) and heavy interference (Bluetooth, microwaves, baby monitors).
- **5 GHz**: more non-overlapping channels, higher throughput, shorter range.
- **6 GHz (Wi-Fi 6E)**: pristine, uncongested spectrum available only to 802.11ax (Wi-Fi 6E) devices — no legacy clients allowed.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Map the marketing names instantly: 802.11n = Wi-Fi 4, 802.11ac = Wi-Fi 5, 802.11ax = Wi-Fi 6 (and Wi-Fi 6E when it uses the 6 GHz band). If a question says "5 GHz only," think 802.11a (54 Mbps, legacy) or 802.11ac (gigabit-class, modern).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** 802.11a and 802.11g both top out at 54 Mbps — but 802.11a is 5 GHz and 802.11g is 2.4 GHz. Questions love to swap these. Also, 802.11b is 11 Mbps (not 10), an easy figure to misremember.

### Cellular

Cellular networks provide wide-area wireless connectivity through carrier infrastructure (towers/base stations). Relevant generations:

- **4G LTE (Long Term Evolution)**: all-IP packet-switched network; typical real-world downloads in the tens of Mbps, theoretical peaks around 100–300 Mbps (up to ~1 Gbps with LTE-Advanced). Latency roughly 30–50 ms.
- **5G**: theoretical peaks up to 20 Gbps and latency as low as ~1 ms. Uses three spectrum ranges:
  - **Low-band** (< 1 GHz): broad coverage, modest speed gains.
  - **Mid-band** (1–6 GHz, "sub-6"): the sweet spot of speed and coverage.
  - **mmWave** (24 GHz+): multi-gigabit speeds but very short range and poor penetration (blocked by walls, even hands).

Cellular is used for WAN backup links, IoT deployments, branch connectivity where wired service is unavailable, and as one of the transports in SD-WAN designs.

### Satellite

Satellite provides connectivity where terrestrial infrastructure doesn't reach (rural sites, ships, aircraft, disaster recovery).

| Orbit | Altitude | Round-Trip Latency | Examples / Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| GEO (geostationary/geosynchronous) | ~35,786 km | ~500–700 ms | HughesNet, Viasat; one satellite covers a huge fixed footprint; dish points at a fixed spot in the sky |
| LEO (low Earth orbit) | ~340–1,200 km (Starlink ~550 km) | ~20–50 ms | Starlink; large constellations of satellites; latency low enough for VoIP, video calls, and gaming |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The exam angle on satellite is **latency**. GEO latency (~600 ms) breaks real-time applications like VoIP and video conferencing; LEO constellations like Starlink reduce latency to tens of milliseconds because the satellites are ~60× closer to Earth. If a scenario mentions a remote site with laggy voice calls over satellite, the culprit is GEO propagation delay.

## Wired

### 802.3 standards

IEEE 802.3 defines wired Ethernet. Naming convention: `<speed>BASE-<media designator>` — e.g., 1000BASE-T is 1000 Mbps, baseband, over twisted pair (T).

**Copper (twisted pair) Ethernet — all limited to 100 meters:**

| Standard | Speed | Minimum Cable Category | Max Distance |
|---|---|---|---|
| 10BASE-T | 10 Mbps | Cat 3 | 100 m |
| 100BASE-TX (Fast Ethernet) | 100 Mbps | Cat 5 | 100 m |
| 1000BASE-T (Gigabit) | 1 Gbps | Cat 5e | 100 m |
| 10GBASE-T | 10 Gbps | Cat 6 (55 m) / Cat 6a (100 m) | 55 m / 100 m |
| 40GBASE-T | 40 Gbps | Cat 8 | 30 m |

**Fiber Ethernet:**

| Standard | Speed | Fiber Type | Wavelength | Max Distance |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1000BASE-SX | 1 Gbps | Multimode | 850 nm | 220–275 m (OM1) / 550 m (OM2+) |
| 1000BASE-LX | 1 Gbps | Single-mode (or MMF) | 1310 nm | 5–10 km SMF / 550 m MMF |
| 10GBASE-SR | 10 Gbps | Multimode | 850 nm | 300 m (OM3) / 400 m (OM4) |
| 10GBASE-LR | 10 Gbps | Single-mode | 1310 nm | 10 km |

Mnemonic: **S = Short (multimode)**, **L = Long (single-mode)**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The 100-meter limit for twisted-pair Ethernet is the single most-tested number in this objective. A workstation 130 m from the closet that keeps dropping link? Cable length violation — add an intermediate switch/repeater or move to fiber. Also memorize the exception: 10GBASE-T only reaches 55 m on plain Cat 6; you need Cat 6a for the full 100 m.

### Single-mode vs. multimode fiber

Fiber-optic cable carries data as pulses of light through a glass core, immune to EMI and capable of enormous distances and bandwidth.

| Characteristic | Single-Mode Fiber (SMF) | Multimode Fiber (MMF) |
|---|---|---|
| Core diameter | 8–10 microns (µm) | 50 or 62.5 microns |
| Light source | Laser | LED or VCSEL |
| Light path | Single ray straight down the core | Multiple rays (modes) bouncing through the core |
| Distance | Kilometers to tens of km (10 km+ typical) | Hundreds of meters |
| Distance limiter | Attenuation/dispersion over very long runs | Modal dispersion (light modes arrive at different times) |
| Typical jacket color | Yellow | Orange (OM1/OM2), aqua (OM3/OM4), lime green (OM5) |
| Cost | More expensive optics (lasers) | Cheaper optics |
| Typical use | Campus/metro/WAN links, long haul | Inside buildings and data centers |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** It's the *optics* (transceivers) that make single-mode expensive, not the glass itself. And remember which numbers are core sizes: 8–10 µm = single-mode; 50/62.5 µm = multimode. Both typically have 125 µm cladding (written 9/125, 50/125, 62.5/125).

### Direct attach copper (DAC) cable

A **DAC** is a fixed-length cable with transceiver modules permanently attached at both ends (SFP+/QSFP+ ends factory-terminated onto the cable).

#### Twinaxial cable

DACs use **twinaxial (twinax)** cable — two inner conductors sharing a single shield (compare coax, which has one). Characteristics:

- Very short reach: typically 1–7 m (passive), up to ~10–15 m (active with signal conditioning).
- Cheaper and lower power/latency than fiber optics at the same speed.
- Classic use case: **top-of-rack (ToR) switching** — connecting servers to the switch at the top of the same rack, or short switch-to-switch links, at 10/25/40/100 Gbps.
- Related: **AOC (active optical cable)** is the same idea with fiber inside for slightly longer runs.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Scenario: "connect servers to a top-of-rack switch at 10 Gbps as inexpensively as possible" → the answer is DAC/twinax, not fiber.

### Coaxial cable

Coax has a single copper core conductor, dielectric insulator, metallic shield, and outer jacket. Modern networking coax is **75 ohm** and terminates with F-type connectors.

| Type | Impedance | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|
| RG-6 | 75 Ω | Cable TV / cable modem (DOCSIS) broadband, satellite TV; thicker, better shielding, longer runs |
| RG-59 | 75 Ω | Short patch runs, analog CCTV video; higher loss — not for long broadband runs |

DOCSIS (Data Over Cable Service Interface Specification) is the standard that carries broadband internet over coax cable-TV plant.

### Cable speeds

Twisted-pair category summary (know bandwidth and top Ethernet speed):

| Category | Bandwidth | Max Ethernet Speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| Cat 5 | 100 MHz | 100 Mbps | Legacy; deprecated |
| Cat 5e | 100 MHz | 1 Gbps | Reduced crosstalk vs. Cat 5; gigabit baseline |
| Cat 6 | 250 MHz | 1 Gbps (10 Gbps to 55 m) | |
| Cat 6a | 500 MHz | 10 Gbps to 100 m | "a" = augmented |
| Cat 7 | 600 MHz | 10 Gbps | Shielded (S/FTP); uses GG45/TERA connectors; never TIA-ratified |
| Cat 8 | 2000 MHz | 25/40 Gbps to 30 m | Data-center short links only |

### Plenum vs. non-plenum cable

The **plenum** is the air-handling space in a building (above drop ceilings, below raised floors) where HVAC air circulates. Fire codes require special cable there:

- **Plenum-rated cable (CMP)**: jacket made of low-smoke, fire-retardant material (e.g., FEP/Teflon or special PVC) that resists burning and emits minimal toxic smoke — because plenum air spreads smoke throughout the building.
- **Non-plenum / riser (CMR) or general PVC cable**: standard jacket; fine inside walls or vertical risers between floors (riser-rated), but **illegal in plenum spaces** per fire code.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Plenum rating is about the **jacket material and fire safety**, not electrical performance. A plenum Cat 6 cable carries data identically to non-plenum Cat 6 — it just burns more safely.

## Transceivers

A transceiver (transmitter + receiver) is a hot-swappable module that plugs into a switch, router, or NIC port and converts internal electrical signals to the media signal (optical or electrical). Modularity lets one switch support fiber, copper, or DAC per-port.

### Protocol

Transceivers are built for a specific protocol — the module must match the protocol running on the link:

#### Ethernet

The standard LAN/data-center protocol (IEEE 802.3). Ethernet transceivers (1G/10G/40G/100G+) carry ordinary network traffic.

#### Fibre Channel (FC)

A dedicated **storage-area network (SAN)** protocol connecting servers to storage arrays with lossless, low-latency delivery. FC runs at 8/16/32/64 Gbps generations and uses its own FC-specific transceivers. An FC transceiver is not interchangeable with an Ethernet transceiver even if the connector looks identical. (FCoE — Fibre Channel over Ethernet — converges the two onto Ethernet, but pure FC uses its own switches, HBAs, and optics.)

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** If the scenario mentions a SAN or storage array connectivity, think Fibre Channel transceivers; for everything else, Ethernet.

### Form factors

| Form Factor | Full Name | Typical Max Speed | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| SFP | Small form-factor pluggable | 1 Gbps | Replaced older GBIC; LC connector typical |
| SFP+ | Enhanced SFP | 10 Gbps | Same physical size as SFP |
| SFP28 | SFP 28 Gbps | 25 Gbps | Single 25G lane |
| QSFP | Quad SFP | 4 Gbps (4 × 1G) | Four channels in one module |
| QSFP+ | Quad SFP+ | 40 Gbps (4 × 10G) | Can break out to 4 × 10G with fanout cables |
| QSFP28 | Quad SFP28 | 100 Gbps (4 × 25G) | The standard 100G module; breakout to 4 × 25G |

- "Quad" = four lanes/channels bundled in one module.
- Breakout (fanout) cables can split one QSFP+ port into four SFP+ links (1 × 40G → 4 × 10G).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Memorize the speed ladder: SFP = 1G, SFP+ = 10G, SFP28 = 25G, QSFP+ = 40G, QSFP28 = 100G. The exam gives you a required port speed and asks which module to buy.

## Connector types

| Connector | Media | Description | Typical Use |
|---|---|---|---|
| SC (subscriber connector) | Fiber | Square, push-pull snap-in ("Square Connector"/"Stick and Click") | Older fiber patch panels, GBICs, FTTH ONTs |
| LC (local connector) | Fiber | Small square half the size of SC ("Little Connector"), latch clip | The dominant modern fiber connector — SFP/SFP+ modules |
| ST (straight tip) | Fiber | Round, bayonet twist-lock ("Stick and Twist") | Legacy multimode installs |
| MPO (multi-fiber push on) | Fiber | Single rectangular connector holding 12+ fiber strands | 40G/100G parallel optics (QSFP+/QSFP28), high-density trunks |
| RJ11 | Copper (UTP) | 6-position (6P2C/6P4C) small plug | Analog telephone lines, DSL modems |
| RJ45 (8P8C) | Copper (UTP/STP) | 8-position, 8-conductor modular plug | Ethernet twisted-pair — the standard LAN connector |
| F-type | Coax | Threaded screw-on connector | Cable TV, cable modems, satellite receivers (RG-6/RG-59) |
| BNC | Coax | Bayonet quarter-twist lock | Legacy 10BASE2 Ethernet, CCTV video, T3/E3, test equipment |

Memory hooks:

- **SC** = "Square Connector" or "Stick and Click" (push-pull).
- **ST** = "Stick and Twist" (bayonet).
- **LC** = "Little Connector" (small; pairs with SFPs).
- **MPO** = many fibers in one push-on connector (think 40G/100G).
- **F-type screws on** (like your cable-TV wall jack); **BNC twists on** (bayonet).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** RJ45 vs. RJ11 — RJ45 has 8 pins (Ethernet), RJ11 has 6 positions and is narrower (telephone/DSL). An RJ11 plug physically fits (loosely) into an RJ45 jack, which is why miswiring scenarios show up on the exam. And don't confuse the two coax connectors: F-type = threaded (CATV/broadband), BNC = bayonet (legacy Ethernet/CCTV/test gear).

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Reproduce the 802.11 table: a (5 GHz/54 Mbps), b (2.4/11), g (2.4/54), n = Wi-Fi 4 (2.4+5/600 Mbps), ac = Wi-Fi 5 (5 GHz/6.9 Gbps), ax = Wi-Fi 6/6E (2.4+5+6 GHz/9.6 Gbps).
- [ ] 5G adds mmWave (huge speed, tiny range); GEO satellite = ~600 ms latency at 35,786 km; LEO (Starlink, ~550 km) = 20–50 ms.
- [ ] Twisted-pair Ethernet = 100 m limit (10GBASE-T on Cat 6 = 55 m; Cat 6a = 100 m; Cat 8 = 40G at 30 m).
- [ ] Fiber: S = short/multimode (SX, SR @ 850 nm), L = long/single-mode (LX, LR @ 1310 nm, 10 km).
- [ ] SMF core 8–10 µm + laser + km distances; MMF core 50/62.5 µm + LED/VCSEL + hundreds of meters.
- [ ] DAC/twinax = cheap short (< 10 m) high-speed links, classic for top-of-rack; RG-6 = broadband coax, RG-59 = short/CCTV.
- [ ] Plenum cable = fire-safe, low-smoke jacket required in air-handling spaces.
- [ ] Transceiver ladder: SFP 1G → SFP+ 10G → SFP28 25G → QSFP+ 40G → QSFP28 100G; Ethernet vs. Fibre Channel (SAN) protocol modules are not interchangeable.
- [ ] Connectors: LC (modern fiber/SFP), SC (square push-pull), ST (bayonet legacy), MPO (multi-fiber 40G/100G), RJ45 (Ethernet), RJ11 (phone/DSL), F-type (CATV coax), BNC (legacy coax).

## Self-Check Questions

1. A client requires a wireless upgrade to a standard that supports the 6 GHz band. Which standard and generation name should you specify?
2. You must link two campus buildings 9 km apart at 10 Gbps. Which fiber type, Ethernet standard, and connector are appropriate?
3. What is the maximum distance for 10GBASE-T over standard Cat 6, and what cable removes that limitation?
4. A data-center engineer needs the cheapest possible 10 Gbps connection from each server to the switch at the top of the same rack. What cable type is best?
5. Which transceiver form factor supports 100 Gbps, and how does it achieve that rate?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **802.11ax, marketed as Wi-Fi 6E** when operating in the 6 GHz band. Plain Wi-Fi 6 is the same 802.11ax standard on 2.4/5 GHz only.
2. **Single-mode fiber running 10GBASE-LR (1310 nm, 10 km reach), typically terminated with LC connectors** into SFP+ optics. Multimode (10GBASE-SR) tops out at 300–400 m.
3. **55 meters on Cat 6.** Upgrading to **Cat 6a** (500 MHz) restores the full 100 m reach for 10GBASE-T.
4. **A DAC (direct attach copper) twinaxial cable** with integrated SFP+ ends — cheaper and lower power than optical transceivers for runs of a few meters.
5. **QSFP28** — it bundles **four lanes of ~25 Gbps each** (4 × 25G = 100G) in one quad module.

</details>
