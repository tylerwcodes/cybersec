# Objective 2.2 — Explain common threat vectors and attack surfaces

Domain 2 is worth **22% of the SY0-701 exam** — the second-largest domain, behind only Security Operations (28%) — and objective 2.2 is its "how attackers get in" catalog. A **threat vector** (attack vector) is the *path or means* an attacker uses to reach a target; the **attack surface** is the *sum of all points* where an attacker could try — every open port, every user inbox, every vendor connection. Shrinking the attack surface (closing ports, removing unused software, restricting USB) removes vectors before an attack ever starts. The exam tests this objective two ways: **definition matching** ("Which term describes a fraudulent SMS message?") and **scenario selection** ("An executive receives an urgent wire-transfer request from a spoofed CEO mailbox — what attack is this?"). The highest-yield items are the **phishing family (phishing / vishing / smishing) keyword mapping**, **business email compromise (BEC)**, **watering hole vs. typosquatting vs. brand impersonation**, **client-based vs. agentless vulnerable software**, and **supply chain vectors (MSPs / vendors / suppliers)** — expect direct questions on all of them.

## Vector vs. Attack Surface — the Framing

Keep the two exam terms distinct before diving into the list:

| Term | Definition | Example |
|---|---|---|
| **Threat vector** (attack vector) | The **path or method** an attacker uses to gain access | A phishing email, an infected USB drive, an exposed RDP port |
| **Attack surface** | The **total set of points** where an attacker *could* attempt entry | All open ports + all user mailboxes + all installed software + all third-party connections |

Every section below is a vector; together they describe the organization's attack surface. **Attack surface reduction** — closing ports, uninstalling unused software, blocking USB storage, decommissioning old systems — is the recurring "best answer" pattern for prevention questions.

## Message-Based Vectors

Message-based vectors deliver malicious content straight to users through the communication channels they trust and check constantly. They are the delivery mechanism for most social engineering.

| Channel | Typical lure | Why it works | Attack name |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Email** | Malicious link/attachment, spoofed sender, invoice fraud | Universal, cheap, scalable; spoofing is easy without SPF/DKIM/DMARC | Phishing / BEC |
| **SMS** | Short urgent text + shortened URL ("package held," "bank alert") | Feels personal; tiny screens hide URLs; outside email filters | Smishing |
| **IM** | Link/file from a "colleague" or compromised account | Implicit trust inside corporate chat; weak filtering on consumer apps | IM phishing / impersonation |

### Email

Email is the **single most common threat vector** — cheap, scalable to millions of recipients, and able to carry malicious links, malicious attachments (macro-enabled documents, archives, scripts), and pure social-engineering text with no payload at all (as in BEC). Attackers routinely **spoof sender addresses** and compromise legitimate mailboxes to defeat "check the sender" advice. Defenses the exam expects you to associate with email: gateway filtering/sandboxing of attachments, blocking macros, user awareness training, and the email authentication trio **SPF, DKIM, and DMARC** to make spoofing harder.

### Short Message Service (SMS)

SMS delivers attacks to phones as text messages — typically a short lure ("Your package is held — pay the customs fee here") plus a shortened or look-alike URL. SMS is effective because texts feel personal and urgent, mobile screens hide full URLs, and phones often sit outside corporate email filtering. SMS is also the channel for **MFA-code interception lures** ("Reply with the code we just sent you") — a code a user forwards is a compromised account. Attack via SMS = **smishing** (covered under human vectors below).

### Instant messaging (IM)

IM platforms (Teams, Slack, WhatsApp, Discord, social-media DMs) carry the same lures as email — malicious links, files, impersonation — but with **less security tooling and more implicit trust**: a message inside the corporate Slack "feels" internal. Compromised accounts let attackers message colleagues from a trusted identity, and consumer IM apps on work devices bypass corporate inspection entirely.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Channel decides the term. Fraudulent **email** → phishing. Fraudulent **SMS/text** → smishing. Fraudulent **voice call** → vishing. The scenario will name the channel — match it mechanically and don't overthink.

## Image-Based Vectors

Images feel like passive, safe content — which is exactly why they work as a vector. The forms to recognize:

- **Malicious code in image files:** crafted images that exploit flaws in the image-rendering/parsing libraries of browsers, chat apps, and viewers — opening (even previewing) the image triggers the exploit.
- **Steganography:** hiding data or malware **inside an image's pixel data**, invisible to the eye and to casual inspection. Attackers use it both to smuggle payloads *in* past filters and to **exfiltrate stolen data out** disguised as innocent pictures.
- **SVG files:** SVG is an **XML-based** image format that can legitimately contain **embedded script** — an "image" that executes code when rendered in a browser.
- **QR codes:** a malicious URL hidden behind an unreadable square. Users can't hover-preview a QR code the way they can a link, and they scan with personal phones outside corporate filtering — QR-code phishing is sometimes called **"quishing"** (posters, fake parking-payment stickers, codes embedded in phishing emails to dodge link scanners).

Takeaway: security filters that only inspect executables and documents miss image-borne threats entirely.

## File-Based Vectors

File-based vectors hide malicious code inside files a user is persuaded to open or execute. Classic carriers:

- **Executables** (.exe, .msi, .scr) — malware delivered directly, often disguised with misleading names/icons or double extensions ("invoice.pdf.exe").
- **Office documents with macros** — macro-enabled Word/Excel files that run code on open; a long-standing top delivery method for droppers and ransomware.
- **PDFs** — embedded scripts/exploits targeting reader software, or simply embedded phishing links.
- **Archives** (.zip, .rar, .iso) — wrap payloads to evade email scanners; password-protected archives (password in the message body) defeat inspection entirely.
- **Scripts** (.js, .vbs, .ps1, .bat) — small, easily obfuscated loaders.

Delivery is usually via another vector — email attachment, IM file share, drive-by download, or removable media — which is why the exam pairs file-based payloads with message-based delivery in scenarios.

## Voice Call Vectors

Voice calls are a **human-to-human** vector: an attacker phones a target and talks them into revealing credentials, reading out MFA codes, resetting a password, or transferring money — this is **vishing** (voice phishing). What makes the phone effective:

- **Caller ID spoofing:** the call *displays* as the help desk, the bank, or an internal extension — caller ID proves nothing.
- **Cheap VoIP at scale:** robocalls pre-screen for live victims, then hand off to a human operator.
- **Real-time pressure:** unlike email, a live caller can improvise, escalate urgency, and counter the victim's hesitation on the spot.
- **AI voice cloning:** synthesized voices of a specific executive or family member make "I recognize the voice" unreliable.
- **Callback phishing:** an email or text supplies a phone number and the *victim* initiates the call — which builds trust, bypasses email link-scanning entirely, and lands the victim with the attacker's "support desk."

Help desks are targets in both directions: attackers call *pretending to be users* to trigger password/MFA resets, and call *pretending to be the help desk* to harvest credentials from users.

## Removable Device Vectors

Removable media — USB flash drives above all, plus external disks and memory cards — carry threats **physically past network defenses**:

- **Baiting / USB drop attacks:** attacker scatters infected drives in a parking lot or lobby labeled "Payroll"; curiosity does the rest. Plugging one in can auto-execute malware or plant trojanized files.
- **Malicious hardware (e.g., "Rubber Ducky"-style devices):** a device that looks like a flash drive but enumerates as a **keyboard (HID)** and types attacker commands at machine speed — no "file" to scan.
- **Data exfiltration:** an insider copies sensitive data out on a pocket-sized drive.
- **Air-gap jumping:** removable media is the classic way malware crosses into isolated/OT networks (Stuxnet is the canonical example).

Mitigations to recognize: **disable AutoRun/AutoPlay**, block or allowlist USB storage via endpoint policy/DLP, and user training ("never plug in found drives").

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** A keystroke-injection USB device is **not** stopped by antivirus scanning of the drive's files — the OS sees a *keyboard*, not storage. Scenarios about "a found USB device that opened a terminal and typed commands" point to malicious HID hardware, and the control is USB device restriction, not file scanning.

## Vulnerable Software

Every installed application enlarges the attack surface: browsers, plugins, office suites, and server software all ship vulnerabilities that exploits target. SY0-701 splits the *scanning/management* view of vulnerable software into two approaches:

### Client-based vs. agentless

| | **Client-based (agent)** | **Agentless** |
|---|---|---|
| How it works | Software **agent installed on each endpoint** monitors/reports vulnerabilities continuously | **No installed software** — scans remotely over the network, or authenticates temporarily (e.g., via credentials) to assess |
| Visibility | Deep, continuous, works even when the device is off-network (roaming laptops) | Point-in-time snapshots; device must be reachable when the scan runs |
| Overhead | Agent consumes resources; agent itself must be **deployed, updated, and patched** — the agent can be a vulnerability (it *adds* attack surface) | Minimal footprint on targets; nothing extra to maintain per host |
| Best fit | Managed corporate endpoints, remote workers | Devices that **can't take an agent**: IoT, network appliances, printers, unmanaged/guest devices |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Keywords decide it: "**install** software on each endpoint / continuous monitoring / works off-network" → **client-based**. "**No software installed** / scan over the network / devices that can't run an agent" → **agentless**. Also remember the twist: an out-of-date *agent itself* is vulnerable software.

## Unsupported Systems and Applications

Unsupported (end-of-life / end-of-support, "legacy") systems no longer receive **security patches from the vendor** — every vulnerability discovered after the end-of-support date stays exploitable **forever**. Classic examples: retired Windows versions, abandoned applications, old embedded/OT and medical systems that can't be upgraded because a critical business process depends on them. Because the vulnerability *cannot be removed*, the exam's expected answer is **compensating controls**: isolate/segment the system on its own network, restrict access tightly, monitor it closely, and plan replacement. Running unsupported software knowingly is an *accepted risk* that must be documented.

## Unsecure Networks

Networks that lack encryption, authentication, or segmentation let attackers intercept traffic and reach systems directly.

### Wireless

Wireless signals propagate beyond walls, so attackers don't need physical entry. Key wireless vector patterns:

- **Open/public Wi-Fi:** no encryption — anyone nearby can capture traffic.
- **Rogue access point:** an unauthorized AP attached to the network (by an employee for convenience or an attacker for access), creating an unmanaged backdoor.
- **Evil twin:** an attacker's AP broadcasting a **look-alike SSID** ("Airport_Free_WiFi") so victims connect through the attacker, enabling on-path interception and credential harvesting.
- **Weak/legacy protocols:** WEP and WPA are crackable; current expectation is **WPA3** (or WPA2 with strong configuration) and 802.1X/EAP in the enterprise.

### Wired

Wired networks feel safer but are a vector when **physical port access is uncontrolled**: an attacker (or a rogue device) plugs into a live wall jack in a lobby or conference room and is instantly inside the LAN. Wired threats include tapping/sniffing traffic, on-path attacks (e.g., ARP poisoning on the local segment), and unauthorized devices. Mitigations to recognize: **802.1X port-based network access control**, disabling unused switch ports, MAC filtering/port security, and physical security of jacks and wiring closets.

### Bluetooth

Bluetooth's short range does not make it safe — attacks work from across a room or with high-gain antennas:

| Attack | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Bluejacking** | Sends **unsolicited messages** to nearby Bluetooth devices — annoying, mostly harmless |
| **Bluesnarfing** | **Steals data** (contacts, messages, files) from a device over Bluetooth — unauthorized access |
| **Bluetooth impersonation / pairing attacks** (e.g., BlueBorne-class flaws) | Exploit protocol/implementation flaws to hijack or take over devices, sometimes with no pairing at all |

Hygiene: keep firmware patched, turn off discoverable mode (or Bluetooth entirely) when unused, and reject unexpected pairing requests.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Blue**jacking** = sending (junk messages *to* you); blue**snarfing** = stealing (data taken *from* you). Snarfing is the serious one. The exam loves this pair — anchor "snarf = steal."

## Open Service Ports

Every listening port is a **door into a service** — and each service is code that may be vulnerable, misconfigured, or protected by weak credentials. Attackers discover open ports with scanners (e.g., Nmap) as their first reconnaissance step, then probe whatever answers: unpatched web servers, exposed RDP (a top ransomware entry point), databases listening on the internet, forgotten management interfaces. Every unnecessary open port expands the attack surface for no benefit. The mitigation vocabulary: **close/disable unused services**, firewall rules that default-deny, and regular port/vulnerability scanning to find what's exposed before attackers do. If a scenario says "a scan revealed services running that the server doesn't need," the answer is disable them — classic hardening.

## Default Credentials

Devices and applications ship with well-known factory logins (admin/admin, admin/password) — and **published lists of default credentials exist for virtually every product**. Routers, IP cameras, printers, IoT gadgets, and management consoles deployed without changing these are effectively unauthenticated: attackers and botnets (Mirai famously) simply try the defaults at scale. This vector requires **zero exploit skill**. The fix is first-boot hygiene: **change default passwords immediately**, disable or rename default accounts where possible, and include default-credential checks in vulnerability scans. On the exam, "device was deployed with factory settings / manufacturer password unchanged" maps to default credentials every time.

## Supply Chain Vectors

Supply chain attacks compromise you **through a trusted third party** — attackers breach someone you trust and inherit that trust. You can patch your own systems perfectly and still be breached through a partner. SY0-701 names three third parties:

| Third party | Who they are | How they become your vector | Classic example pattern |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Managed service providers (MSPs)** | Outsourced IT/security operations with **remote administrative access** to many client networks | One MSP compromise → attacker uses the MSP's own tools/credentials to reach **every client at once** | Ransomware pushed to hundreds of businesses through a compromised MSP remote-management platform (Kaseya-style) |
| **Vendors** | Companies whose **software/hardware/services** you buy and run | Malicious code inserted into a legitimate, **signed software update**; backdoored or counterfeit hardware | Trojanized update distributed to thousands of customers (SolarWinds-style) |
| **Suppliers** | Sources of **components and materials** further up the chain | Compromised chips/firmware/libraries embedded in products **before you ever receive them** | Tampered component or dependency built into equipment during manufacturing |

Mitigations to recognize: vendor due-diligence/risk assessments, contractual security requirements, least-privilege and monitored access for third parties, and verifying software integrity (signatures, SBOMs).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Distinguish by role: **MSP** = manages your IT (the vector is their *remote access*); **vendor** = sells you the product (the vector is the *software/update/hardware itself*); **supplier** = provides parts/components upstream (the vector is *what's inside* the product before delivery). "Malicious code arrived in a legitimate software update" → vendor supply chain attack.

## Human Vectors / Social Engineering

Humans are the most exploited attack surface: social engineering manipulates people into breaking security procedures using psychological levers — **authority, urgency, fear, scarcity, trust, familiarity** — instead of exploiting code. SY0-701 expects you to name the technique *and* recognize the pressure being applied: "CEO needs this **urgently**, tell no one" = urgency + authority; "caller **claimed to be from IT** with a migration story" = a pretext; "only 2 left — act now" = scarcity. Recognize each named technique and its trigger keywords:

### Phishing

Fraudulent messages — **email** in the base term — impersonating a trusted party to steal credentials, deliver malware, or trigger payments. Variants built on it: **spear phishing** (targeted at a specific person/organization using researched details) and **whaling** (spear phishing aimed at executives/high-value targets). Signature clues in scenarios: generic greetings, urgency, mismatched/spoofed sender domains, links to credential-harvesting pages.

### Vishing

**Voice** phishing — the phone-call form (see Voice Call above). Keywords: *phone call*, *caller ID spoofed*, *caller claimed to be the help desk/bank/IRS*, *asked the user to read back the MFA code*.

### Smishing

**SMS** phishing — the text-message form. Keywords: *text message*, *SMS*, *shortened link*, *package delivery/bank alert text*.

### Misinformation / disinformation

Manipulating what people believe at scale, typically via social media **influence campaigns**:

- **Misinformation** = false information spread **without intent to deceive** (people sharing something wrong in good faith).
- **Disinformation** = false information **deliberately created and spread to deceive** (state-sponsored influence operations, fake narratives to move markets or elections, or to damage a brand).

The differentiator is **intent** — that's the exam question.

### Impersonation

The attacker **pretends to be someone else** — IT support, a delivery driver, an auditor, a new employee, a police officer — in person, by phone, or online, to gain access or information. Uniforms, badges, jargon, and name-dropping make it convincing. Impersonation is the umbrella; many other techniques (vishing, BEC, brand impersonation) are impersonation through specific channels.

### Business email compromise (BEC)

A **targeted, financially motivated** email attack in which the attacker impersonates — or actually **compromises** — an executive, employee, or trusted vendor mailbox, then instructs staff to **wire money, change payment/banking details, or send sensitive data**. Key traits: often **no malware and no link** (pure text, so filters miss it), heavy urgency/authority pressure ("I'm in a meeting, process this transfer now, keep it confidential"), and use of look-alike domains or real hijacked threads. BEC is among the most financially damaging attack types. Countermeasure keyword: **out-of-band verification** of any payment or banking change (call a known number — never contact info from the email itself).

### Pretexting

The attacker invents a **fabricated scenario (the pretext)** — a believable story and identity — to justify their request: "This is Dave from IT; we're migrating mailboxes tonight and I need you to confirm your password," or "I'm the fire inspector, here for the audit." Pretexting is the *storytelling engine* inside most impersonation, vishing, and BEC attacks: the scenario keyword is a **prepared backstory that makes the request seem legitimate**.

### Watering hole

Instead of contacting victims directly, the attacker **compromises a legitimate third-party website the target group is known to visit** (an industry association site, a regional news outlet, a niche supplier portal) and plants malware there — then waits, like a predator at a watering hole, for the intended victims to come to them. Used when targets are too hardened to phish directly. Keywords: *legitimate website frequented by the target organization/industry was compromised; visiting employees were infected*.

### Brand impersonation

The attacker **masquerades as a well-known, trusted brand** — cloned Microsoft/bank/shipping-company login pages, fake support portals, spoofed brand emails complete with authentic logos and formatting — to harvest credentials and payments at scale. It exploits trust in the *brand* rather than in a specific person (contrast with BEC/impersonation of individuals).

### Typosquatting

Registering **look-alike domains based on typing mistakes or visual tricks** — `gooogle.com`, `amaz0n.com`, `micros0ft-support.com` (also called URL hijacking). Users who mistype a URL, or who don't scrutinize a link in a phishing message, land on the attacker's page: credential harvesting, malware, or ad fraud. Typosquatting supplies the *infrastructure* for phishing and brand impersonation. Keyword: **a domain nearly identical to the legitimate one, off by a character**.

### Human-vector keyword map (fast review)

| Technique | Trigger keywords in the scenario |
|---|---|
| **Phishing** | Fraudulent *email*, malicious link/attachment, spoofed sender (spear = targeted; whaling = executives) |
| **Vishing** | *Phone call*, spoofed caller ID, caller requests credentials/MFA code |
| **Smishing** | *Text message/SMS*, shortened link, delivery/bank alert |
| **Misinformation** | False info spread **without** intent to deceive |
| **Disinformation** | False info **deliberately** created/spread to deceive (influence campaign) |
| **Impersonation** | Attacker *poses as* IT, delivery driver, auditor, police — in person or remote |
| **BEC** | Spoofed/compromised *executive or vendor mailbox*, wire transfer, changed banking details, urgency + secrecy |
| **Pretexting** | Invented *backstory/scenario* that justifies the request |
| **Watering hole** | *Legitimate site the target group frequents* was compromised |
| **Brand impersonation** | Cloned *well-known company* login page, fake branded email/support portal |
| **Typosquatting** | *Look-alike/misspelled domain*, off by one character |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Watering hole vs. typosquatting: a **watering hole compromises a real, legitimate site** the targets already visit; **typosquatting registers a fake look-alike domain** and waits for mistyped/misdirected visits. And BEC vs. phishing: mass credential-stealing email = phishing; targeted impersonation of an executive/vendor to move **money** = BEC.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Threat **vector** = the path in; **attack surface** = all possible entry points combined — hardening shrinks the surface by removing vectors.
- [ ] **Email** is the #1 vector (links, attachments, spoofing); SMS and IM carry the same lures with weaker filtering and higher implicit trust.
- [ ] Channel → term: email = **phishing**, SMS = **smishing**, voice call = **vishing**.
- [ ] **Image-based** vectors: malicious/steganographic images, script-bearing SVGs, and QR codes hiding malicious URLs.
- [ ] **File-based** vectors: executables, macro documents, PDFs, scripts, and archives (password-protected ZIPs evade scanners).
- [ ] **Removable devices**: USB drop/baiting, HID keystroke-injection hardware, data exfiltration, and air-gap jumping — disable AutoRun, restrict USB.
- [ ] Vulnerability management: **client-based** = installed agent, continuous, works off-network; **agentless** = no install, network-based, for devices that can't run agents.
- [ ] **Unsupported/EOL systems** never get patches again — segment, isolate, and compensate since the vulnerability can't be removed.
- [ ] Unsecure networks: **evil twin/rogue AP** (wireless), open wall jacks without **802.1X** (wired), **bluejacking = junk messages / bluesnarfing = data theft** (Bluetooth).
- [ ] **Open service ports** invite probing — close unused services, default-deny firewall, scan yourself first.
- [ ] **Default credentials** = zero-skill entry; change factory passwords at deployment (IoT botnets like Mirai depend on them).
- [ ] Supply chain: **MSP** (remote-access to many clients), **vendor** (trojanized software/updates — SolarWinds pattern), **supplier** (tampered upstream components).
- [ ] **BEC** = targeted executive/vendor impersonation to move money — often no malware; verify payment changes **out-of-band**.
- [ ] **Misinformation** = false without intent; **disinformation** = deliberately deceptive — intent is the differentiator.
- [ ] **Watering hole** = compromise a legitimate site targets visit; **typosquatting** = register look-alike domains; **brand impersonation** = fake trusted-company pages/emails.

## Self-Check Questions

1. An accounts-payable clerk receives an email that appears to come from the CFO: "Process this vendor payment to the new account number today — I'm boarding a flight and can't talk. Keep this confidential." There is no attachment and no link. What attack is this, and what is the recommended countermeasure?
2. A security team wants vulnerability visibility on corporate laptops that spend most of their time off the corporate network, and also on 200 IP cameras and printers that cannot run additional software. Which assessment approach fits each group, and why?
3. Employees of a defense contractor are infected with malware after visiting a legitimate aerospace-industry news site that attackers had compromised weeks earlier. Name the attack and explain how it differs from typosquatting.
4. A janitor finds a USB drive labeled "Executive Salaries" in the parking lot and plugs it into a lobby PC; seconds later a terminal window opens and commands type themselves rapidly. Identify the vector and the device type, and explain why antivirus file scanning didn't stop it.
5. A small business outsources all IT administration to a third party with remote-management access. That third party is breached, and ransomware is deployed to the business through the third party's own management tools. Which supply chain vector is this, and how does it differ from a vendor-based supply chain attack?
