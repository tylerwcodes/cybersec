# Objective 2.1 — Compare and contrast common threat actors and motivations

Domain 2 is worth **22% of the SY0-701 exam** — the second-largest domain — and objective 2.1 is its opening move: before you can analyze attacks and mitigations, you must be able to identify **who** is attacking and **why**. The exam tests this material two ways: **definition matching** ("Which threat actor is government-funded and conducts long-term espionage?") and **scenario selection** ("An employee installs an unapproved cloud file-sharing app to work faster — what does this represent?"). Almost every 2.1 question boils down to matching a short scenario to one actor type and/or one motivation using keyword clues. The highest-yield items are the **nation-state (APT) profile**, the **insider threat vs. shadow IT distinction**, the **organized crime = financial gain** pairing, the **hacktivist = philosophical/political** pairing, and the three **actor attributes** (internal/external, resources/funding, sophistication/capability) used to compare them all.

## Threat Actors

A **threat actor** (threat agent) is the person or entity responsible for an event that impacts — or has the potential to impact — the security of an organization. SY0-701 names six actor types. Learn each one's profile, then learn the attribute table that contrasts them, because "compare and contrast" questions ask you to pick the actor whose *combination* of attributes and motivation fits the scenario.

### Nation-state

A **nation-state actor** is a government — or a group sponsored, directed, or funded by a government — conducting cyber operations in support of national interests.

- **Attributes:** external; **highest resources/funding** (effectively unlimited — taxpayer/state budgets); **highest sophistication** (custom malware, zero-day exploits, dedicated teams, patience measured in years).
- **Motivations:** **espionage** (stealing state and trade secrets), **data exfiltration**, **war** (cyberwarfare against another nation's military or critical infrastructure), and **disruption/chaos** (destabilizing a rival's economy or public confidence).
- **Signature behavior:** nation-states run **APTs — Advanced Persistent Threats**: long-term, stealthy campaigns that establish persistent access to a target network and remain undetected for months or years while quietly collecting data. The exam treats "APT" as a near-synonym for nation-state activity.
- **Targets:** governments, militaries, defense contractors, critical infrastructure (power, water, healthcare, finance), and companies holding valuable intellectual property.
- A nation-state may also operate a **false flag** — deliberately imitating another group (e.g., posing as hacktivists or common criminals) to hide attribution.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Scenario keywords **"government-sponsored," "zero-day," "highly sophisticated," "undetected for months/years," "critical infrastructure," "APT"** → **nation-state**. No other actor type combines top-tier funding with top-tier capability and long-dwell-time persistence.

### Unskilled attacker

An **unskilled attacker** (historically called a *script kiddie* — SY0-701 uses the more formal term) has little or no technical ability and relies entirely on **pre-made tools, scripts, and exploits written by others**, often without understanding how they work.

- **Attributes:** external; **low/no funding**; **lowest sophistication**.
- **Motivations:** **disruption/chaos**, bragging rights, curiosity, sometimes petty revenge — rarely a coherent strategic goal.
- **Behavior:** downloads attack tools from forums or uses cheap attack-for-hire services (e.g., DDoS booter/stresser sites); attacks are **noisy, opportunistic, and untargeted** — they hit whatever the tool finds vulnerable.
- **Why they still matter:** modern point-and-click tooling and malware-/ransomware-as-a-service mean even unskilled attackers can cause real damage. Volume compensates for skill — unpatched, internet-facing systems will absolutely be found by them.

### Hacktivist

A **hacktivist** (hacker + activist) attacks organizations to advance a **philosophical or political cause** rather than for money.

- **Attributes:** external; **low-to-moderate funding** (sometimes crowd-sourced); **variable sophistication** — many members are unskilled, but large collectives can include capable individuals.
- **Motivations:** **philosophical/political beliefs** first and foremost; the attacks themselves usually take the form of **service disruption** (DDoS), **website defacement**, and **data exfiltration followed by public leaking** (doxing) to embarrass the target.
- **Targets:** governments, corporations, and organizations the group perceives as opposing its cause — the target is chosen for *what it represents*, not for profit.
- Classic example: the Anonymous collective's DDoS and defacement campaigns.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Hacktivists steal data too — but to **publish it and embarrass the target**, not to sell it. If leaked data appears on a public site with a manifesto, think **hacktivist**; if it's sold on a dark-web market or ransomed back, think **organized crime**.

### Insider threat

An **insider threat** originates from someone **within** the organization — an employee, contractor, partner, or anyone else with **legitimate, authorized access** to systems, facilities, or data.

Insiders come in two flavors, and the exam distinguishes them:

- **Malicious insider:** intentionally abuses access. Motivations: **revenge** (the classic disgruntled or terminated employee), **financial gain** (selling data or access, embezzlement/fraud), **blackmail** (coerced by an outside party), or **espionage** (recruited/planted by a competitor or nation-state).
- **Unintentional/negligent insider:** causes harm through carelessness, not intent — falling for phishing, misconfiguring a cloud bucket, losing an unencrypted laptop, plugging in a found USB drive. Still an insider threat even with zero malice.

- **Attributes:** **internal** (the defining attribute); funding is usually **low/personal**, but that barely matters — their power comes from **already being inside**: they hold valid credentials, know where the valuable data lives, know the security gaps, and bypass most perimeter defenses by default. **Sophistication varies** widely, but insider knowledge substitutes for technical skill.
- **Mitigations to recognize** (previews of later objectives): least privilege, separation of duties, offboarding/deprovisioning, user behavior analytics, DLP.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Don't assume "insider threat" means malicious. A well-meaning employee who emails a customer database to their personal account "to work from home" is an insider threat (unintentional). Intent changes the *motivation*, not the *classification* — anyone with authorized access who endangers the organization is an insider threat.

### Organized crime

**Organized crime** groups are professional criminal enterprises that run cybercrime as a **business** — hierarchical, specialized, and profit-driven.

- **Attributes:** external; **high resources/funding** (funded by prior criminal proceeds); **high sophistication** — often second only to nation-states, with specialists in specific roles (malware development, initial access, money laundering) and a service economy connecting them.
- **Motivation:** **financial gain**, overwhelmingly — everything they do converts to money. Common revenue streams:
  - **Ransomware** — including double extortion (encrypt *and* threaten to leak: financial gain + **blackmail**).
  - **Data exfiltration for sale** — stolen card numbers, credentials, PII, and PHI sold on dark-web markets.
  - **Fraud** — business email compromise (BEC), phishing, identity theft.
  - **Crime-as-a-service** — Ransomware-as-a-Service (RaaS), initial access brokers, botnet/DDoS rental — which is exactly what arms unskilled attackers.
- **Targets:** anyone who will pay — businesses of every size, hospitals, schools, municipalities, individuals. Unlike nation-states, targeting is opportunistic and ROI-driven.
- **Scenario tell:** if the question centers on ransomware, stolen data sold on the dark web, BEC/fraud, or any "as-a-service" criminal offering, the actor is organized crime and the motivation is financial gain — money is always the tell.

### Shadow IT

**Shadow IT** is hardware, software, or cloud services used **inside the organization without the knowledge, approval, or control of the IT department**. It is unique in this list: the "actor" is typically a well-intentioned employee or business unit just trying to get work done.

- Examples: a team adopting an unapproved SaaS file-sharing or messaging app; a department spinning up its own cloud instances on a corporate credit card; an employee plugging a personal Wi-Fi router or NAS into the office network; unauthorized personal devices handling company data.
- **Attributes:** **internal**; funding is whatever the department's budget allows; sophistication is **low** (these are users, not attackers).
- **Motivation:** usually **benign** — convenience, productivity, working around IT processes perceived as slow or restrictive. There is normally **no malicious intent**. (Don't label this the exam's "ethical" motivation — that term is reserved for authorized white-hat testing.)
- **Why it's a threat anyway:** shadow IT sits **outside security management** — unpatched, unmonitored, not backed up, misconfigured, violating data-handling and compliance requirements — creating vulnerabilities and data exposure the security team cannot see or defend.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Shadow IT vs. malicious insider. Both are **internal**, but shadow IT is defined by **unauthorized technology adopted for convenience/productivity**, while a malicious insider **intends harm or personal gain**. "Employee uses an unapproved cloud app to share files with a client faster" = shadow IT — not a malicious insider, and not an external attack.

### Threat actor comparison (memorize this table)

| Actor | Internal/External | Resources/Funding | Sophistication/Capability | Primary motivation(s) | Scenario keywords |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Nation-state** | External | **Very high** (government) | **Very high** (zero-days, custom tools) | Espionage, data exfiltration, war, disruption | APT, government-sponsored, critical infrastructure, long-term stealth |
| **Unskilled attacker** | External | **Very low** | **Very low** (borrowed tools) | Disruption/chaos, notoriety | Script kiddie, downloaded tool, opportunistic, noisy |
| **Hacktivist** | External | Low–moderate | Variable | **Philosophical/political beliefs** | Cause, protest, defacement, leak with manifesto |
| **Insider threat** | **Internal** | Low (but has access) | Variable (insider knowledge) | Revenge, financial gain; or unintentional | Employee/contractor, authorized access, disgruntled, terminated |
| **Organized crime** | External | **High** | **High** (specialized roles) | **Financial gain**, blackmail | Ransomware, dark web sale, fraud, as-a-service |
| **Shadow IT** | **Internal** | Department budget | Low (non-malicious users) | Convenience/productivity (no malice) | Unapproved app/device/cloud, without IT approval |

## Attributes of Actors

The exam's "compare and contrast" framing rests on three attribute axes. Any actor in any scenario can be plotted on all three — and questions often describe an actor *only* by attributes and ask you to name the type.

### Internal/external

- **Internal actors** operate from **inside** the organization with some level of **authorized access**: employees, contractors, vendors/partners with credentials. The two internal types on the objective list are the **insider threat** and **shadow IT**. Internal actors bypass perimeter defenses by default and start with knowledge and trust.
- **External actors** attack from **outside** and must first gain access (breach the perimeter, phish a user, exploit a public-facing flaw): **nation-states, organized crime, hacktivists, unskilled attackers**.
- The boundary can blur — an external actor who compromises (or recruits/bribes) an employee gains insider-grade access — but for classification questions, ask: *did the actor start with authorized access?*

Why the attribute matters defensively: perimeter controls (firewalls, IPS, email filtering) address **external** actors; **internal** actors require least privilege, separation of duties, monitoring/auditing of authorized activity, and disciplined offboarding — you cannot firewall out someone you gave a badge and a login.

### Resources/funding

Resources determine what an actor can afford: tooling, infrastructure, zero-day purchases, personnel, and time.

- **Nation-state:** effectively unlimited — state budgets, dedicated units, ability to develop or buy zero-days.
- **Organized crime:** high — profits from prior crime are reinvested; can buy quality tools, access, and talent.
- **Hacktivist:** low to moderate — volunteer labor, donations, free tools.
- **Insider threat / shadow IT:** personal or departmental funds — but insiders spend access and knowledge, not money.
- **Unskilled attacker:** minimal — free or cheap tools built by others.

### Level of sophistication/capability

Sophistication determines *how* an actor attacks and how hard they are to detect.

- **High sophistication** (nation-state, top organized crime): custom malware, zero-day exploits, novel techniques, careful operational security, long undetected dwell time. Requires advanced defenses to catch.
- **Moderate/variable** (hacktivists, some insiders): capable of real damage with known techniques and public tooling.
- **Low sophistication** (unskilled attackers, negligent insiders, shadow IT users): known exploits, default configurations, and loud, detectable activity — reliably stopped by patching, hardening, and basic controls.

Sophistication also shapes **defensive expectations**: basic hygiene (patching, MFA, backups) defeats low-sophistication actors; detecting a nation-state APT requires threat hunting, behavioral analytics, and the assumption that prevention will sometimes fail. Note that resources and sophistication usually correlate but aren't identical — a lone insider can be technically unsophisticated yet devastating because access substitutes for capability.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Attribute-only questions are common: "Which actor is **external, well-funded, and highly sophisticated**, motivated by espionage?" → nation-state. "External, well-funded, highly sophisticated, motivated by **profit**?" → organized crime. The funding + sophistication pair narrows it to those two; **motivation breaks the tie** (espionage/war → nation-state; money → organized crime).

## Motivations

Motivation is the *why* behind an attack, and SY0-701 lists ten. Questions phrase these almost verbatim, so match the definition and the actor(s) each motivation typically pairs with. Note the distinction the exam draws between an attack's **motivation** (goal) and its **technique** (method) — e.g., a DDoS (technique) can serve hacktivism, extortion, or chaos (motivations).

| Motivation | Definition | Typical actor(s) | Scenario keywords |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data exfiltration** | Unauthorized transfer of data **out of** the organization | Nation-state, organized crime, malicious insider | Data copied/uploaded to external server, stolen database, IP theft |
| **Espionage** | Covertly stealing secrets — government intelligence or corporate/trade secrets | **Nation-state**; insiders recruited by competitors | Spying, state secrets, defense contractor, competitor obtains R&D |
| **Service disruption** | Taking systems/services offline or degrading them | Hacktivists, nation-states, extortionists | DDoS, outage, "website unavailable," sabotage of operations |
| **Blackmail** | Coercing the victim by threatening to release data or sustain an attack unless demands are met | Organized crime | Extortion, "pay or we leak your data," double-extortion ransomware, ransom DDoS |
| **Financial gain** | Attacking to make money | **Organized crime**; some insiders | Ransom payment, selling data/credentials, fraud, BEC |
| **Philosophical/political beliefs** | Advancing an ideology or cause | **Hacktivist** | Protest, activism, manifesto, defacement with a message |
| **Ethical** | Finding/exposing weaknesses to improve security — the "white hat" motivation | Authorized penetration testers, bug bounty researchers | Authorized/permission, responsible disclosure, bug bounty |
| **Revenge** | Retaliating against a perceived wrong | **Disgruntled insider** (current or former employee) | Fired/passed over for promotion, sabotage after termination |
| **Disruption/chaos** | Causing damage or confusion for its own sake — no profit or cause | Unskilled attackers; sometimes nation-states (destabilization) | "For the lulz," vandalism, no demands, random targets |
| **War** | Cyber operations as part of armed conflict — degrading an adversary's military or critical infrastructure | **Nation-state** | Cyberwarfare, attack on power grid/military systems during hostilities |

### Data exfiltration

**Data exfiltration** is the unauthorized transfer of data out of an organization — the data itself is the target. Exfiltrated data may be intellectual property (designs, source code, formulas), personal data (PII/PHI), credentials, or financial records. What happens next reveals the actor: **sold** on dark-web markets → organized crime; **kept secret and analyzed** → nation-state espionage; **published to embarrass** → hacktivist; **carried to a new employer** → malicious insider. Exfiltration is frequently the *payoff stage* of a longer attack — initial access, privilege escalation, and lateral movement all exist to reach the data worth taking.

### Espionage

**Espionage** is covert intelligence gathering — stealing secrets while remaining undetected. Two forms appear on the exam:

- **State espionage:** nation-states stealing government, military, and diplomatic intelligence from rivals.
- **Corporate/industrial espionage:** stealing trade secrets, R&D, bids, and strategy — conducted by nation-states supporting domestic industries, by competitors, or by **insiders recruited or planted** for the purpose.

Espionage overlaps with data exfiltration (the secrets must leave the network) but the emphasis is on **stealth and long-term intelligence value** rather than immediate monetization — which is why espionage scenarios feature APT-style persistence.

### Service disruption

**Service disruption** means degrading or taking down a target's systems or services — websites knocked offline by DDoS, operations halted by sabotage or wiper malware. Disruption serves different masters: hacktivists disrupt to protest, criminals disrupt to extort ("pay us to stop the DDoS"), and nation-states disrupt to weaken adversaries. On the exam, identify disruption by **outage/unavailability language**, then use the surrounding context (demand? manifesto? conflict?) to pin the actor.

### Blackmail

**Blackmail** (extortion) coerces the victim: comply — usually pay — or suffer a threatened consequence. Cyber forms include **double-extortion ransomware** (pay or your stolen data is leaked), **ransom DDoS** (pay or we keep attacking), and threats to expose embarrassing information about individuals or executives. The defining element is the **conditional threat**; the payment demanded makes blackmail a favorite of organized crime, where it compounds with financial gain.

### Financial gain

**Financial gain** — profit — is the single most common motivation in real-world cybercrime and the calling card of **organized crime**. Revenue models: ransomware payments, sale of stolen data and credentials, payment-card fraud, BEC and invoice fraud, cryptojacking, and selling criminal services to other actors. Malicious insiders can share this motivation (selling access or data, embezzlement). When a scenario shows money changing hands or data being *sold*, financial gain is the answer.

### Philosophical/political beliefs

Attacks motivated by **philosophical/political beliefs** advance an ideology, cause, or protest — the definition of **hacktivism**. The target is symbolic (chosen for what it does or represents), the attack is usually public and attributed (the point is the message), and typical techniques are defacement, DDoS, and doxing/leaks accompanied by statements. Contrast with every profit motive: ideologically motivated actors *want* attention; criminals want quiet money.

### Ethical

The **ethical** motivation drives white-hat security work: attacking systems **with authorization** to find weaknesses before adversaries do — penetration tests under a signed scope, red-team engagements, and **bug bounty** research — followed by **responsible disclosure** to the owner. Ethical actors use the same tools and techniques as criminals; permission and disclosure intent are what make them defenders.

### Revenge

**Revenge** is retaliation for a perceived wrong, and on the exam it maps almost exclusively to the **disgruntled insider** — the employee passed over for promotion, disciplined, or terminated, who sabotages systems, deletes data, leaks secrets, or plants logic bombs. Revenge scenarios are why **prompt deprovisioning at offboarding** is stressed everywhere in Security+: a fired admin whose credentials still work is the textbook setup.

### Disruption/chaos

**Disruption/chaos** as a motivation means causing havoc **for its own sake** — no ransom demand, no manifesto, no strategic objective. This is the classic motivation of **unskilled attackers** seeking thrills or bragging rights, though nation-states can also sow chaos deliberately to destabilize a rival society (disinformation, election interference, random-seeming infrastructure attacks).

### War

**War** as a motivation means cyber operations conducted as an instrument of armed conflict — **cyberwarfare**. Nation-states attack an adversary's military systems, command and control, and **critical infrastructure** (power grids, communications, water, transportation, finance) to degrade its ability to fight and function, either alongside kinetic operations or as standalone coercion. Only nation-states (and their sponsored proxies) carry this motivation on the exam.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** **Service disruption** vs. **disruption/chaos** — service disruption is the *outcome* (systems down) pursued as a goal, often with a further purpose (protest, extortion, war); disruption/chaos is the *motivation* of causing havoc for its own sake, with no demands and no cause. Likewise, **blackmail** vs. **financial gain**: blackmail is the *coercion* ("pay or else we leak/attack"); plain theft, fraud, or selling stolen data is financial gain without coercion. Double-extortion ransomware combines both.

### Ethical hackers and authorization

The **ethical** motivation deserves special attention because it flips the frame: the same techniques used by every actor above are legal and beneficial when performed **with authorization**. Ethical (white-hat) hackers — penetration testers under contract and scope agreements, and researchers in **bug bounty** programs — attack systems to find vulnerabilities *before* real adversaries do, then report them responsibly. The single dividing line the exam cares about is **permission**: authorized testing = ethical; the identical act without authorization = a crime, regardless of intent. (Older material calls unauthorized-but-well-intentioned actors "gray hats" — still illegal.)

## Putting It Together — Scenario Mapping

2.1 questions are pattern-matching exercises. Practice reading a one-sentence scenario and extracting **(1) actor, (2) attributes, (3) motivation**:

| Scenario | Actor | Motivation |
|---|---|---|
| Undetected group in a power utility's network for 18 months, using previously unknown exploits | Nation-state (APT) | Espionage / war |
| Teenager launches a DDoS against their school using a rented booter service | Unskilled attacker | Disruption/chaos |
| Group defaces a corporation's homepage with slogans opposing its environmental record | Hacktivist | Philosophical/political beliefs |
| Terminated administrator uses still-active credentials to delete production databases | Insider threat (malicious) | Revenge |
| Company data encrypted; attackers demand cryptocurrency and threaten to publish stolen files | Organized crime | Financial gain + blackmail |
| Marketing team signs up for an unapproved AI transcription SaaS and uploads meeting recordings | Shadow IT | Convenience/productivity (no malice) |
| Researcher reports a flaw through the company's bug bounty program | Ethical hacker | Ethical |

### Elimination strategy for 2.1 questions

When two answer choices seem plausible, apply the attributes in order:

1. **Internal or external?** "Employee/contractor/authorized access" instantly eliminates all four external actors, leaving insider threat vs. shadow IT — then ask whether there was **intent to harm** (insider) or just unapproved convenience (shadow IT).
2. **Funding and sophistication?** "Zero-day / custom malware / long-term stealth" eliminates everyone except nation-state and elite organized crime; "downloaded tool / rented service / noisy" points to an unskilled attacker.
3. **Motivation?** The final discriminator. Money → organized crime. Cause/message → hacktivist. Secrets/war → nation-state. Revenge → disgruntled insider. Chaos with no demands → unskilled attacker. Permission granted → ethical.

Work the axes in that order and most 2.1 questions resolve in seconds.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] A threat actor is the **who** behind a threat; SY0-701 compares actors along **internal/external, resources/funding, and sophistication/capability**.
- [ ] **Nation-state**: external, best-funded, most sophisticated; motivations of espionage, data exfiltration, war; operates **APTs** (long-term stealthy persistence) and may use false flags.
- [ ] **Unskilled attacker** (script kiddie): external, minimal funding/skill, uses others' pre-made tools; motivated by chaos/notoriety; dangerous through volume and easy tooling.
- [ ] **Hacktivist**: external, motivated by **philosophical/political beliefs**; favors DDoS, defacement, and public data leaks to embarrass targets.
- [ ] **Insider threat**: **internal**, has authorized access; can be **malicious** (revenge, financial gain) or **unintentional/negligent** (phishing victim, misconfiguration) — both count.
- [ ] **Organized crime**: external, well-funded, highly sophisticated, business-structured; motivation is **financial gain** (ransomware, data sale, fraud, crime-as-a-service).
- [ ] **Shadow IT**: **internal**, non-malicious — unapproved apps/devices/cloud adopted for convenience — dangerous because it escapes security management and monitoring.
- [ ] Only two objective-list actors are internal: **insider threat** and **shadow IT**; intent to harm distinguishes them.
- [ ] Funding + sophistication narrows to nation-state vs. organized crime; **motivation breaks the tie** (espionage/war vs. money).
- [ ] **Data exfiltration** = data leaving the organization; **espionage** = covert secret-stealing (state or corporate).
- [ ] **Blackmail** = coercive "pay or else"; **financial gain** = profit by any means; double-extortion ransomware is both.
- [ ] **Service disruption** = taking services down as a goal; **disruption/chaos** = havoc for its own sake with no demands.
- [ ] **Ethical** motivation = white-hat testing; **authorization/permission** is the line between ethical hacking and crime.
- [ ] **Revenge** almost always signals a **disgruntled current or former employee** — check offboarding/deprovisioning.
- [ ] **War** = nation-state cyber operations against an adversary's military or critical infrastructure during conflict.

## Self-Check Questions

1. Security analysts discover that attackers have maintained hidden access to a defense contractor's network for over a year, using custom malware and a previously unknown (zero-day) exploit to quietly copy weapons-design documents. Which threat actor is most likely responsible, and what two motivations best fit?
2. An employee in the finance department, frustrated with the approval time for new software, subscribes to an unapproved cloud budgeting app and uploads company financial data to it. Classify this threat and explain why it is *not* a malicious insider threat.
3. A company's public website is knocked offline by a DDoS attack, and a group posts a statement condemning the company's labor practices and claiming responsibility. Identify the actor type, the motivation, and the technique — and explain which of those three the DDoS itself is.
4. A question describes an actor that is external, has substantial funding, employs specialists in distinct roles (malware authors, access brokers, money launderers), and encrypts victims' data while threatening to leak stolen copies unless paid. Name the actor and both motivations in play.
5. Two people run the exact same vulnerability scan and exploit chain against a company's web application. One is prosecuted; the other is paid. What distinguishes them, and what is the paid individual's motivation called on the SY0-701 objective list?
