# Objective 4.6 — Given a scenario, implement and maintain identity and access management

Domain 4 (Security Operations) is the largest domain on SY0-701 at **28% of the exam**, and objective 4.6 is a *scenario* objective: expect questions that describe a situation and ask which IAM mechanism fits. The highest-yield items are **identifying the access control model** from a scenario (MAC vs. DAC vs. role-based vs. rule-based vs. ABAC), **counting authentication factors** (a smart card plus a PIN is two factors; two passwords is still one), telling **SAML vs. OAuth vs. LDAP** apart, the **provisioning/de-provisioning lifecycle** (especially prompt de-provisioning at termination), and the three **privileged access management (PAM) tools**: just-in-time permissions, password vaulting, and ephemeral credentials. The unifying theme: give the right identities the right access to the right resources — and take it away the moment it is no longer needed.

## The Identity Lifecycle

Identity and access management (IAM) follows every account from creation to deletion. The exam names four lifecycle activities: **identity proofing**, **provisioning/de-provisioning**, **permission assignments**, and **attestation**.

### Identity proofing

**Identity proofing** is verifying that a person actually *is* who they claim to be **before** an account or credential is ever issued. Authentication later proves possession of a credential; proofing proves the real-world identity behind it at enrollment time.

Common proofing methods:

- **Document verification** — checking government-issued ID (passport, driver's license), often with a live photo/selfie match for remote onboarding.
- **Knowledge-based verification** — answering questions drawn from records only the true person should know (prior addresses, loan amounts). Weakest method, since breach data can answer these.
- **In-person verification** by HR or a trusted registration authority.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** A scenario about **verifying a new hire's passport before creating their account**, or a help desk **confirming a caller's identity before a password reset**, is **identity proofing**. If proofing is weak, an attacker can socially engineer the help desk into resetting a victim's credentials — proofing failures are how account-takeover attacks begin.

### Provisioning/de-provisioning user accounts

**Provisioning** is the process of creating a user account and granting it the access rights, group memberships, and resources the person's job requires — typically at onboarding. Mature organizations automate provisioning from the HR system so accounts are created consistently and with standard, role-appropriate permissions from day one.

**De-provisioning** is the reverse: disabling or removing the account and revoking *all* its access when the person leaves or no longer needs it — at offboarding, contract end, or role change. De-provisioning must be **prompt and complete**:

- Accounts of terminated employees should be **disabled immediately** (commonly disabled first, then deleted after a retention period so files and audit trails are preserved).
- **Orphaned accounts** — active accounts belonging to people who have left — are a favorite attacker target: valid credentials, real permissions, and nobody watching them.
- De-provisioning covers everything, not just the network login: cloud/SaaS accounts, VPN, badges, tokens, shared-mailbox rights, and API keys.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** A scenario where a **former employee's credentials still work weeks after termination** is a **de-provisioning failure** (an orphaned account). For a hostile termination, the account is disabled *before or at the moment* the employee is notified.

### Permission assignments and implications

**Permission assignment** is deciding and configuring exactly what each account can do. The security implications the exam cares about:

- **Least privilege** (detailed under access controls below): assign only the minimum permissions the job requires — nothing "just in case."
- Assign permissions to **roles/groups**, not individuals, wherever possible; it is consistent, auditable, and makes reviews feasible.
- **Privilege creep** (authorization creep): as users transfer between roles, they *accumulate* permissions from old jobs that are never revoked. Over time a long-tenured employee can access far more than their current role warrants. The cure is revoking old access at every role change plus **periodic access reviews**.
- Excess permissions magnify every other risk: a phished user's account, or malware running as that user, can do exactly what the account can do — no more, no less.

### Attestation

In IAM, **attestation** (access review/recertification) is the formal, periodic process in which a responsible party — typically the user's manager or the resource owner — **reviews and certifies** that each user's access rights are still appropriate. Reviewers confirm ("attest") that each permission is still needed, and flag for removal any that are not. Attestation is the standing countermeasure to privilege creep and orphaned accounts, and regulations/auditors commonly require it on a schedule (e.g., quarterly for privileged access).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Managers must **review and confirm** their employees' access every quarter" → **attestation**. Don't confuse it with identity proofing (verifying *who someone is* at enrollment); attestation verifies *whether existing access is still justified*.

## Federation and Single Sign-On

### Federation

**Federation** extends trust in identities **across organizational boundaries**: users authenticate with their *own* organization's identity provider, and a partner's systems accept that authentication instead of issuing separate credentials. Two key roles:

| Role | Function |
|---|---|
| **Identity provider (IdP)** | Authenticates the user and issues assertions/tokens vouching for them |
| **Service provider (SP) / relying party** | The application or resource that trusts the IdP's assertion and grants access |

Examples: logging in to a SaaS app with your corporate account, or "Sign in with Google" on a third-party site. Benefits: no duplicate credential stores, partners never handle your users' passwords, and de-provisioning at the IdP cuts access everywhere at once.

### Single sign-on (SSO)

**Single sign-on** lets a user authenticate **once** and then access multiple applications without re-entering credentials. SSO reduces password fatigue (fewer weak/reused passwords, fewer reset tickets) and centralizes authentication where strong controls (MFA, conditional access, logging) can be enforced. The trade-off: the SSO identity becomes a single point of compromise, so it must be protected with MFA. Federation is SSO's cross-organization big brother — SSO within an enterprise, federation between enterprises — and both are built on the protocols below.

### Lightweight Directory Access Protocol (LDAP)

**LDAP** is the standard protocol for **querying and modifying directory services** — the central database of users, groups, and resources (Microsoft **Active Directory** is the best-known LDAP-compatible directory). Applications authenticate users and look up group memberships against the directory over LDAP, making one directory the shared identity store behind many apps.

- LDAP uses **TCP/UDP port 389**; unencrypted LDAP sends queries (and simple-bind credentials) in cleartext.
- **LDAPS** (LDAP over SSL/TLS) uses **TCP port 636** and should always be used instead.

### Open authorization (OAuth)

**OAuth** (OAuth 2.0) is an **authorization** framework — it is about *delegated access*, not proving identity. OAuth lets a user grant a third-party application **limited access to resources on another service without sharing their password**: instead, the resource service issues the app a scoped **access token**. Example: granting a scheduling app permission to read (only) your calendar. Because plain OAuth only authorizes, **OpenID Connect (OIDC)** adds an authentication/identity layer on top of OAuth 2.0 and is what modern "log in with…" web/mobile SSO actually uses.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** **OAuth = authorization ("what may this app do?"); SAML and OIDC = authentication ("who is this user?").** If a scenario is a third-party app getting limited access to your data *without your password*, the answer is OAuth. If it's an XML-based assertion from an IdP logging you in to an SP, it's SAML.

### Security Assertions Markup Language (SAML)

**SAML** is an **XML-based** standard for exchanging authentication and authorization data between an **identity provider and a service provider** — the classic protocol for browser-based federated SSO in enterprises. Flow: the user requests the application (SP) → the SP redirects the browser to the IdP → the user authenticates at the IdP (with MFA, ideally) → the IdP sends back a digitally signed **SAML assertion** → the SP validates the signature and grants access. The user's password is only ever entered at the IdP.

### Interoperability

**Interoperability** is the ability of different vendors' identity systems to work together — which is exactly why the ecosystem runs on open standards: **SAML, OAuth, OIDC, LDAP**, and **SCIM** (System for Cross-domain Identity Management, which standardizes automated provisioning/de-provisioning between systems). When selecting an IdP or a SaaS product, verifying it supports these standards ensures it can plug into your existing IAM stack — and into partners' — without proprietary, one-off integrations. Proprietary-only identity products create lock-in and integration gaps that turn into security gaps.

## Access Controls

Access control models define *how* the system decides whether a subject may access an object. Model identification is one of the most-tested items in this objective:

| Model | Who decides | How it works | Classic example |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Mandatory (MAC)** | The **operating system/policy**, not users | Objects carry **classification labels**, subjects hold **clearances**; the OS enforces the policy and even the data owner cannot override it | Military/government systems (Top Secret clearances), SELinux |
| **Discretionary (DAC)** | The **owner** of each object | The resource owner grants or denies access **at their discretion** (e.g., setting permissions on a file they created) | Standard NTFS/Linux file permissions |
| **Role-based (RBAC)** | Admins, via **job roles** | Permissions are attached to roles (e.g., "Accounts Payable clerk"); users get access by being placed in roles/groups | Enterprise group-based access |
| **Rule-based** | Admins, via **system-wide rules** | A predefined rule list is applied to **everyone regardless of identity** | Firewall ACLs; "no logons from outside the country" |
| **Attribute-based (ABAC)** | Policy engine evaluating **attributes** | Decisions combine attributes of the **user** (department, clearance), the **resource** (sensitivity), the **action**, and the **environment** (device health, location, time) | "Managers in Finance, on a compliant device, during business hours, may approve payments" |

Two more named controls:

- **Time-of-day restrictions** limit *when* accounts can log on or access resources (e.g., a cashier's account works only during store hours; batch-job service accounts only at night). They shrink the attack window — a stolen credential is useless at 3 a.m. if the account cannot log on then.
- **Least privilege** is the governing principle across all models: every user, account, and process gets the **minimum access required to do its job, and no more**. It limits the blast radius of any compromise and is the stated rationale behind JIT permissions, role design, and access reviews.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Model-identification keywords — **labels/clearances/military** → MAC; **owner decides** → DAC; **job function/position/group** → role-based; **same rule for everyone, regardless of who** → rule-based; **multiple attributes combined (department + device + location + time)** → ABAC. ABAC is the most fine-grained and context-aware of the models.

## Multifactor Authentication

**Multifactor authentication (MFA)** requires proof from **two or more different factor categories**. Two proofs from the *same* category (a password plus a PIN) is **not** MFA — that's still single-factor.

### Factors

| Factor | Meaning | Examples |
|---|---|---|
| **Something you know** | Knowledge | Password, PIN, security-question answer |
| **Something you have** | Possession | Smart card, hardware token, security key, phone with authenticator app |
| **Something you are** | Inherence (biometrics) | Fingerprint, face, iris, voice |
| **Somewhere you are** | Location | GPS/geolocation, IP address range, geofencing |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Count the **categories**, not the credentials. Smart card + PIN = have + know = **two factors**. Password + fingerprint = know + are = two factors. Password + security questions = know + know = **one factor**. An ATM card + PIN is the classic everyday two-factor example.

### Implementations

- **Biometrics** — measuring a physical characteristic (**something you are**): fingerprint, facial recognition, iris/retina, voice. Accuracy metrics: the **false acceptance rate (FAR)** is the rate at which *unauthorized* users are wrongly accepted (the dangerous error), the **false rejection rate (FRR)** is the rate at which *legitimate* users are wrongly rejected (the annoying error), and the **crossover error rate (CER)** is where FAR = FRR — a **lower CER means a more accurate system**. Tightening sensitivity lowers FAR but raises FRR, and vice versa.
- **Hard authentication tokens** — dedicated **physical devices** that prove possession: key fobs displaying one-time passwords, smart cards. They cannot be remotely phished off a device, but they cost money and can be lost.
- **Soft authentication tokens** — **software-based** tokens, typically an **authenticator app** on a phone generating one-time passwords. **TOTP** (time-based OTP) codes change on a fixed interval (typically every 30 seconds); **HOTP** (HMAC-based OTP) codes advance with a counter on each use. Soft tokens are far stronger than SMS codes, which are vulnerable to SIM-swapping and interception.
- **Security keys** — physical keys (USB/NFC, e.g., YubiKey) implementing **FIDO2/WebAuthn** public-key authentication. The key signs a challenge that is bound to the legitimate site's origin, so a look-alike phishing site gets nothing usable — security keys are the **phishing-resistant** MFA implementation.

## Password Concepts

### Password best practices

| Practice | What it means | Current guidance |
|---|---|---|
| **Length** | Minimum number of characters | The **single most important strength factor** — each added character multiplies the search space; favor long passphrases (12–16+ characters) |
| **Complexity** | Mixing upper/lower case, numbers, symbols | Adds strength, but forced composition rules yield predictable patterns ("P@ssw0rd1!"); modern (NIST) guidance emphasizes **length over mandatory complexity** and screening against known-breached password lists |
| **Reuse** | Using the same password across accounts, or recycling old ones | Prohibit it: reuse is what makes **credential stuffing** work — one breached site unlocks every account sharing the password; password **history** settings block recycling previous passwords |
| **Expiration** | Forcing a change after a maximum age (e.g., 90 days) | Traditional policy; modern NIST guidance drops routine expiration (it breeds "Password1 → Password2") and instead requires a change **whenever compromise is suspected** |
| **Age** | **Minimum** password age — how long a user must keep a new password before changing it again | Prevents users from rapidly cycling through changes to exhaust the history list and return to an old favorite |

### Password managers

A **password manager** is a vault application that **generates, stores, and fills strong, unique passwords** for every account, encrypted under one strong master password (which should itself be protected by MFA). It solves the human limitation that makes reuse inevitable: users need remember only one secret, and every site gets a long random unique password. A compromised website then exposes exactly one credential instead of the user's entire digital life.

### Passwordless

**Passwordless authentication** eliminates the password entirely, replacing it with possession- and inherence-based proofs: **FIDO2 security keys and passkeys**, platform biometrics (Windows Hello, Face ID) unlocking a device-bound private key, or certificates. Under the hood it is public-key cryptography — the service stores a public key, the device signs a challenge with the private key, unlocked locally by biometric or PIN. There is **no shared secret to phish, breach, reuse, or forget**, which is why passwordless (passkeys) is both more secure *and* more convenient than passwords.

## Privileged Access Management Tools

**Privileged access management (PAM)** governs the most powerful accounts — domain admins, root, service and cloud-admin accounts — whose compromise means total compromise. The exam names three PAM tools:

- **Just-in-time (JIT) permissions** — administrators hold **no standing privileges**; elevated rights are granted **on request, for a specific task, for a limited time window**, then automatically revoked. An attacker who compromises an admin's account between tasks gets an account with *no* admin rights. JIT is least privilege applied to the time dimension.
- **Password vaulting** — privileged credentials are locked in a hardened, encrypted **vault**; admins check credentials out (with approval and MFA) and the vault **records who used which credential when** and typically **rotates the password after each check-in**. Nobody "knows" the root password day-to-day, shared-account use becomes individually accountable, and sessions can be brokered/recorded.
- **Ephemeral credentials** — **temporary, short-lived credentials created on demand that expire automatically** after minutes or hours (short-lived cloud tokens, one-time certificates, temporary accounts). Because they self-destruct, stolen ones are quickly useless, and nothing long-lived is left behind to leak or be forgotten.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** All three PAM tools attack **standing privilege**. Keywords: "admin rights granted **only when needed, for a limited time**" → **JIT permissions**; "privileged passwords **checked out** from a central store and rotated" → **password vaulting**; "credentials that **automatically expire** shortly after issuance" → **ephemeral credentials**.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] **Identity proofing** verifies who a person really is *before* issuing credentials; **attestation** periodically re-certifies that existing access is still appropriate.
- [ ] **Provision** accounts with role-appropriate least privilege at onboarding; **de-provision immediately** at termination — orphaned accounts are attacker gold.
- [ ] **Privilege creep** (permissions accumulating across role changes) is countered by revoking access at every transfer plus periodic **access reviews/attestation**.
- [ ] **Federation** = trusting another organization's IdP; **SSO** = authenticate once, access many apps; protect the SSO identity with MFA.
- [ ] **LDAP** queries directory services (port **389**; **LDAPS = 636**); **SAML** = XML-based IdP→SP authentication assertions; **OAuth** = delegated **authorization** via tokens (OIDC adds authentication on top).
- [ ] **Interoperability** comes from open standards (SAML, OAuth, OIDC, LDAP, SCIM) so different vendors' IAM systems work together.
- [ ] Access models: **MAC** = labels/clearances, OS-enforced; **DAC** = owner decides; **role-based** = job function; **rule-based** = same rules for everyone; **ABAC** = decisions from combined user/resource/environment attributes.
- [ ] **Time-of-day restrictions** limit when logons work; **least privilege** = minimum necessary access, always.
- [ ] MFA requires **different factor categories**: know / have / are / somewhere you are. Two of the same category is not MFA.
- [ ] Biometric accuracy: **FAR** (impostor accepted) vs. **FRR** (legit user rejected); **lower CER = better**.
- [ ] **Hard tokens** = physical OTP devices/smart cards; **soft tokens** = authenticator apps (TOTP = time-based, HOTP = counter-based); **security keys** (FIDO2) are the phishing-resistant option.
- [ ] Passwords: **length beats complexity**; block **reuse**; modern guidance replaces routine **expiration** with change-on-compromise; **minimum age** stops history-cycling.
- [ ] **Password managers** make unique, strong passwords practical; **passwordless** (passkeys/FIDO2) removes the shared secret entirely.
- [ ] PAM tools: **JIT permissions** (no standing privilege), **password vaulting** (check-out, audit, rotate), **ephemeral credentials** (auto-expiring).

## Self-Check Questions

1. A user authenticates to the corporate identity provider once each morning and can then open the HR portal, the expense system, and a partner company's supplier portal without logging in again. Name the two IAM concepts at work and the XML-based protocol most likely carrying the authentication assertions to the partner.
2. A file server lets each document's creator decide which colleagues may read or edit it. A classified government network instead assigns clearance levels and lets the OS alone decide access. Name each access control model and the key difference.
3. An auditor finds that a payroll clerk who transferred from the warehouse three years ago can still modify inventory records. Name the problem and the two IAM processes that should have prevented or caught it.
4. A company wants admin rights that exist only while a change ticket is open, privileged passwords that are centrally stored and rotated after each use, and cloud access tokens that expire after 15 minutes. Name the PAM tool matching each requirement.
5. A user logs in with a password and then approves a push notification in an authenticator app on their phone. A second user logs in with a password and then answers three security questions. Which user is performing MFA, and why?
