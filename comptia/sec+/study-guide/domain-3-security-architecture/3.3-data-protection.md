# Objective 3.3 — Compare and contrast concepts and strategies to protect data

Domain 3 is worth **18% of the SY0-701 exam**, and objective 3.3 is its data-protection core: what kinds of data exist, how organizations label them by sensitivity, the three states data can be in, and the toolbox of methods that protect it. The exam tests this material two ways: **definition matching** ("Which term describes replacing sensitive data with a non-sensitive substitute?") and **scenario selection** ("A developer needs realistic test data without exposing real customer records — which method?"). The verb in the objective is *compare and contrast*, so expect questions that put similar-sounding terms side by side and make you pick the precise one. The highest-yield items here are the **three data states (at rest / in transit / in use)** and which protection applies to each, the **masking vs. tokenization vs. encryption vs. hashing** distinction, **data sovereignty** (and how geolocation enforces it), and the **classification labels** — expect direct questions on all four.

## Data Types

Data types describe **what the data is** — its content and the obligations attached to it. A single record can belong to several types at once (a customer's payment record is regulated *and* financial), and the type determines which laws, contracts, and internal rules govern its handling.

### Regulated

Regulated data is any data whose protection is **mandated by law, regulation, or industry standard** — the organization does not get to choose how carefully to handle it. Classic examples:

- **PII (personally identifiable information):** names, Social Security numbers, addresses, birthdates — anything that identifies an individual. Protected by privacy laws such as the EU's **GDPR** and state laws like CCPA.
- **PHI (protected health information):** medical records, diagnoses, insurance details — governed by **HIPAA** in the United States.
- **Payment card data:** cardholder numbers (PANs) — governed by the **PCI DSS** industry standard.

Mishandling regulated data brings **fines, breach-notification duties, and legal liability** on top of the breach itself.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Match the keyword to the regime: **health records → PHI/HIPAA**, **card numbers → PCI DSS**, **EU personal data → GDPR**. If a scenario says data handling is "required by law," the data type is **regulated** — even if the question never names the specific law.

### Trade secret

A trade secret is proprietary business information whose **value depends on staying secret**: formulas, recipes, manufacturing processes, algorithms, customer lists, pricing strategies. Unlike patents, trade secrets are **never publicly disclosed or registered** — protection lasts only as long as the secrecy does. The Coca-Cola formula and Google's ranking algorithm are canonical examples. Loss of a trade secret to a competitor (via insider theft or corporate espionage) can be unrecoverable, because there is no way to make the information secret again.

### Intellectual property

Intellectual property (IP) is the broader category of **creations of the mind that the organization owns**: patents, copyrights, trademarks, source code, designs, and trade secrets (a trade secret is one kind of IP). Patents (and registered trademarks) are *publicly registered*, and copyrights arise automatically on creation — all are legally protected against unauthorized use; trade secrets are protected only by confidentiality. IP theft is a primary motivation for **nation-state and corporate-espionage threat actors**, so IP-heavy organizations (software, pharma, manufacturing) build data-protection programs around it.

### Legal information

Legal information covers data connected to legal matters: contracts, litigation records, attorney-client privileged communications, court documents, and materials under **legal hold** (a preservation order that forbids deleting data relevant to anticipated litigation). Handling requirements include strict confidentiality (privilege can be *lost* if material is disclosed carelessly) and **retention rules** — some legal data must be kept for mandated periods, and data under legal hold is exempt from normal deletion schedules.

### Financial information

Financial information includes bank account and routing numbers, payment card data, payroll, tax records, invoices, and corporate financial statements. It is protected both because it is a **direct target for fraud and theft** and because regulations demand it — **SOX** (Sarbanes-Oxley) for the integrity of public-company financial reporting, **GLBA** for consumer financial data at financial institutions, and **PCI DSS** for card data. Note the overlap: most financial information is *also* regulated data.

### Human- and non-human-readable

This pair contrasts the **format** of data rather than its content:

| | Human-readable | Non-human-readable |
|---|---|---|
| Definition | Directly understandable by a person | Requires a machine or processing to interpret |
| Examples | Documents, spreadsheets, emails, plain-text CSV/JSON/XML | Binary files, machine code, barcodes/QR codes, serialized or encrypted output, database internals |
| Security implication | Immediately useful if stolen or seen on screen | Not secret by itself — trivially converted with the right tool |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Non-human-readable is **not** a security control. A binary or Base64-encoded file is unreadable to the eye but instantly decodable — this is *encoding*, not encryption. Only encryption (a key-based transformation) actually protects confidentiality. Both readable and non-readable data need the same protections.

### Data types at a glance

| Data type | Defining trait | Scenario keywords |
|---|---|---|
| **Regulated** | Protection required by law/standard | PII, PHI, HIPAA, GDPR, PCI DSS, "required by law" |
| **Trade secret** | Value depends on secrecy; never disclosed | Secret formula, proprietary process, competitor advantage |
| **Intellectual property** | Owned creations of the mind | Patents, copyrights, source code, designs |
| **Legal information** | Tied to legal matters and privilege | Contracts, litigation, attorney-client, legal hold |
| **Financial information** | Money-related; fraud target | Account numbers, payroll, tax records, SOX/GLBA |
| **Human-/non-human-readable** | Format, not content | Plain text vs. binary/barcode/machine code |

## Data Classifications

Classification labels describe **how sensitive** data is and therefore how strictly it must be handled — who may access it, how it must be stored and transmitted, and how it is destroyed. Labels drive controls: a "Restricted" label might mandate encryption, need-to-know access, and no removable media, while "Public" needs only integrity protection. SY0-701 lists six labels:

| Classification | Meaning | Typical examples | Impact if disclosed |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Public** | Approved for anyone to see; no confidentiality harm | Marketing material, press releases, public website content | None (integrity/availability still matter) |
| **Private** | Personal or internal data not meant for outsiders | Employee records, internal directories, personal data | Privacy harm, embarrassment, possible legal exposure |
| **Sensitive** | Could cause harm to the organization or individuals if exposed | IP, financial details, security configurations | Moderate to serious harm |
| **Confidential** | Limited to authorized personnel; disclosure causes serious damage | Trade secrets, M&A plans, source code, contracts | Serious competitive/legal damage |
| **Restricted** | Highest sensitivity; strict **need-to-know** access only | Encryption keys, credentials, classified material, PHI in some schemes | Severe damage; often regulatory violation |
| **Critical** | Data the organization **cannot operate without**; availability-focused | Production databases, authentication systems' data, key operational records | Business stoppage — the label drives backup/redundancy priority |

Three points the exam likes:

- **There is no single universal hierarchy.** Organizations define their own ladders (a common commercial one: Public → Private → Sensitive → Confidential → Restricted). Government schemes use their own labels (Unclassified → Confidential → Secret → Top Secret). What matters is that higher labels get stricter controls.
- **Critical is about availability**, not just secrecy. Data can be Critical without being Confidential (e.g., a public transit schedule feed the business depends on). Classification tells you where to spend protection effort — including backups and redundancy, not only encryption.
- **Classification drives the whole lifecycle.** The label assigned at creation determines storage requirements (encrypted volume vs. ordinary share), transmission rules (TLS required, no personal email), retention period, and destruction method (cryptographic erase/shredding vs. simple deletion). Over-classifying everything is also a failure mode — it wastes budget and trains users to ignore labels.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** If a scenario says access is limited to a **need-to-know** basis or names the *most tightly controlled* category, the answer is **Restricted**. If the question stresses that the business **cannot function without** the data, the answer is **Critical**.

## General Data Considerations

### Data states

Every piece of data is always in exactly one of three states, and each state has its own threats and its own protections. This is one of the most-tested concepts in Domain 3.

| State | Definition | Example locations | Primary protections |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Data at rest** | Stored on media, not being accessed | Disks, SSDs, databases, backups, USB drives, cloud storage buckets | **Encryption at rest** (FDE such as BitLocker, database/column encryption, AES-256), access controls, backups |
| **Data in transit** | Moving across a network | Web traffic, email, file transfers, API calls, replication between data centers | **TLS/HTTPS**, **IPSec VPN**, SSH; certificate-based authentication |
| **Data in use** | Actively being processed in memory/CPU | RAM, CPU caches and registers, application variables, screen display | Memory encryption, **secure enclaves/TEE**, hardening against memory-scraping malware, access controls, screen locks |

- **Data at rest** is what a stolen laptop or decommissioned drive exposes — full-disk encryption makes the media unreadable without the key.
- **Data in transit** (also called data in motion) is what eavesdropping and on-path (man-in-the-middle) attacks target — encrypt the channel with TLS or IPSec.
- **Data in use** is the hardest state to protect, because the CPU must generally work on plaintext. Threats include memory-scraping malware (e.g., on point-of-sale systems) and attackers dumping RAM. Modern answers are hardware-based: **secure enclaves** (trusted execution environments) that process data in an isolated, encrypted region of the processor.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Encryption is **state-specific**. Full-disk encryption protects a powered-off stolen laptop, but once the system is booted and unlocked, files read into memory are plaintext (data in use), and a file emailed without TLS crosses the network in cleartext (data in transit). One encrypted state does not protect the other two — scenarios that describe data "already encrypted on disk" being stolen from RAM or off the wire are testing exactly this.

### Data sovereignty

Data sovereignty is the principle that **data is subject to the laws of the country in which it is collected, stored, or processed** — regardless of where the owning company is headquartered. Consequences:

- Some jurisdictions require certain data (citizens' personal data, government data, health records) to **remain physically within national borders** — a *data residency* requirement.
- Cloud computing makes this a live design issue: choosing which **cloud region** hosts data, controlling where **replicas and backups** land, and restricting cross-border transfers (e.g., EU personal data may leave the EU only under approved safeguards).
- A single dataset replicated to three countries can be simultaneously subject to three legal regimes — including foreign government access laws.

Scenario keywords: "data must remain in-country," "subject to local laws," "choose a cloud region for compliance" → **data sovereignty**.

### Geolocation

Geolocation is determining the **physical location** of data, a device, or a user — via GPS, IP address ranges, Wi-Fi positioning, or cell towers. In data protection it plays two roles:

1. **Knowing where data is** — which data center, region, or country holds it — which is the prerequisite for proving sovereignty/residency compliance.
2. **Feeding location-based controls** — conditional access decisions ("block logins from outside the country"), impossible-travel detection, and the geographic restrictions covered below.

Keep the pair straight for contrast questions: **data sovereignty is the legal concept** (data answers to local law), while **geolocation is the technical capability** (knowing where data and users physically are) that lets you *enforce* sovereignty and the geographic restrictions covered next.

## Methods to Secure Data

These eight methods are the "strategies" the objective asks you to compare and contrast. Several of them (encryption, hashing, masking, tokenization, obfuscation) look superficially similar — the exam's favorite trick is describing one and offering the others as distractors, so learn the distinguishing traits and whether each is reversible.

### Geographic restrictions

Geographic restrictions permit or deny access to data **based on physical location**, using geolocation data:

- **Geofencing:** a virtual boundary (GPS/IP/Wi-Fi based) — allow an app or data access only inside a defined area (on campus, in-country), or alert/wipe when a managed device leaves it.
- **Geo-blocking of logins/traffic:** deny authentication attempts or connections originating from countries where the organization has no users.
- **Storage location restrictions:** configure cloud services so data and its replicas are created only in approved regions — the enforcement arm of data sovereignty.

Scenario keywords: "block access from outside the country," "data may not leave the region," "virtual boundary" → geographic restrictions/geofencing.

### Encryption

Encryption transforms readable **plaintext** into unreadable **ciphertext** using an algorithm and a **key**; only holders of the correct key can reverse it. It is the primary confidentiality control across all three data states:

- **At rest:** full-disk encryption (BitLocker, FileVault, self-encrypting drives), file-level, database/column-level encryption — typically symmetric **AES-256**.
- **In transit:** TLS 1.3 for application traffic, IPSec for network tunnels, SSH for administration.
- **In use:** emerging protections — secure enclaves and memory encryption.

Two algorithm families divide the work: **symmetric** encryption (AES) uses one shared key and is fast, so it does the bulk encryption of stored data and traffic; **asymmetric** encryption (RSA, elliptic curve) uses a public/private key pair and handles key exchange and digital signatures. TLS combines them — asymmetric to establish the session, symmetric for the data.

Key properties for the compare-and-contrast questions: encryption is **reversible (with the key)**, protects **confidentiality**, and its security rests entirely on **key management** — lose the key and the data is gone; leak the key and the encryption is worthless.

### Hashing

Hashing runs data through a **one-way function** (SHA-256, SHA-3) that produces a fixed-length digest. It is **not reversible** — you cannot recover the input from the hash — which makes it fundamentally different from encryption:

- **Integrity verification:** compare a file's hash before and after transfer/storage; any change to the data changes the hash. Used in file downloads, digital signatures, and forensic evidence handling.
- **Password storage:** systems store the hash (salted) rather than the password itself, and verify logins by hashing the entered password and comparing. A stolen hash database does not directly reveal passwords.

Hashing provides **integrity** (and safe password verification), never confidentiality of data you need back.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "Encrypt the passwords" is almost always the wrong answer — passwords should be **hashed (and salted)**, not encrypted, because no one should ever need to recover the plaintext. Conversely, hashing data you need to read later (customer records) is wrong because hashing is one-way. **Need the data back → encryption. Only need to verify/compare → hashing.**

### Masking

Masking hides part or all of a data value by **substituting characters** (typically asterisks or Xs), while optionally leaving a portion visible for usability:

- A receipt showing `**** **** **** 4821` masks the card number but keeps the last four digits.
- A support screen showing `***-**-6789` for an SSN.
- **Dynamic masking** hides data on display for unauthorized viewers while the stored value remains intact; **static masking** produces a permanently sanitized copy (e.g., a scrubbed database for developers or testing).

The stored original may remain intact (dynamic) or be irreversibly replaced (static) — but the displayed value itself carries no recoverable secret.

### Tokenization

Tokenization replaces a sensitive value with a **non-sensitive substitute (token) that has no mathematical relationship to the original**. The real value is stored in a separate, highly secured **token vault**, and only the tokenization system can map a token back to the original.

- Classic use: **payment processing** — a merchant stores the token `TKN-83f2…` instead of the card number; mobile payment systems (Apple Pay/Google Pay–style) transmit a device-specific token, so the merchant never touches the PAN.
- Because tokens are mathematically meaningless, a breach of the merchant's database yields nothing usable, and systems that hold only tokens can **fall out of PCI DSS audit scope** — a major compliance driver.
- Contrast with encryption: ciphertext is mathematically **derived** from the plaintext and reversible with a key; a token is a **random stand-in** reversible only via the vault lookup.

### Obfuscation

Obfuscation is the umbrella term for **making data or code intentionally difficult to understand** without necessarily using cryptography. Masking and tokenization are specific obfuscation techniques; other examples:

- **Code obfuscation:** renaming variables to gibberish and mangling program structure so source code is hard to reverse-engineer (protecting IP — and also used by malware authors to evade analysis).
- **Steganography:** hiding data inside another file, such as a message embedded in an image's pixel data — hiding the *existence* of the data rather than scrambling it.

Obfuscation is **security through obscurity**: useful as a supplementary layer (slowing attackers, protecting IP), but never a substitute for encryption and access control, because a determined analyst can reverse it.

### Segmentation

Segmentation isolates data and the systems holding it into **separate network zones** (VLANs, subnets, firewalled segments, or logically separated databases) so that sensitive data stores are not reachable from general-purpose networks:

- Put the **database servers on their own segment**, reachable only from the application tier on required ports — a compromised user workstation cannot touch the data directly.
- **PCI DSS segmentation:** isolate the cardholder data environment (CDE) so the rest of the network is out of audit scope.
- Segmentation **limits lateral movement** and shrinks the blast radius when (not if) something else is breached.

Scenario keywords: "limit lateral movement," "isolate the database/CDE," "separate network for sensitive systems" → segmentation.

### Permission restrictions

Permission restrictions control **who can access data and what they can do with it** — the access-control layer of data protection:

- **Principle of least privilege:** each user/service gets only the minimum access needed for the job.
- **Access control models** implement it: role-based access (permissions assigned to job roles), attribute- or rule-based policies, ACLs on files and database objects granting read/write/delete per identity or group.
- **Need-to-know** for classified/Restricted data: clearance alone is not enough; access requires a job-related reason.
- Reviews and recertification: periodically audit permissions and remove access that accumulated as people changed roles (privilege creep).

Permission restrictions are the control that makes classification labels *mean* something — a "Confidential" label is enforced by permissions that limit the data to authorized personnel.

### Comparing the look-alike methods (memorize this table)

| Method | What it does | Reversible? | Primary goal | Signature scenario |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Encryption** | Scrambles data with an algorithm + key | **Yes — with the key** | Confidentiality (all three states) | Stolen laptop/drive unreadable; TLS on the wire |
| **Hashing** | One-way fixed-length digest | **No** | Integrity; password verification | Verify a file is unchanged; store passwords safely |
| **Masking** | Substitutes characters, may keep part visible | Displayed value: no | Hide values on display/in copies | `****-****-****-4821` on a receipt; scrubbed test data |
| **Tokenization** | Random substitute; original in a secure vault | Only via the **token vault** | Remove sensitive data from systems/scope | Merchant stores tokens, not card numbers; PCI scope reduction |
| **Obfuscation** | Makes data/code hard to understand (umbrella term) | Usually (not cryptographic) | Deter analysis/reverse engineering | Obfuscated source code; steganography |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Decode the scenario language: "**last four digits visible**" → masking. "**No mathematical relationship** / token vault / reduce PCI scope" → tokenization. "**Verify the file wasn't altered**" → hashing. "**Unreadable without the key**" → encryption. "**Hard-to-read source code** / hidden in an image" → obfuscation (steganography for the image). "**Isolate the database network**" → segmentation. "**Only the HR group can open the folder**" → permission restrictions.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] **Regulated data** (PII, PHI, card data) is protected by external mandate: GDPR/privacy laws, HIPAA, PCI DSS.
- [ ] **Trade secrets** derive value from secrecy and are never registered; they are one category of **intellectual property** (with patents, copyrights, trademarks, source code).
- [ ] **Legal information** carries privilege and retention duties — data under **legal hold** must not be deleted.
- [ ] **Non-human-readable** (binary, barcodes, encoded data) is a format, not a protection — encoding is not encryption.
- [ ] Classification ladder (common commercial order): Public → Private → Sensitive → Confidential → Restricted; **Restricted = need-to-know**, **Critical = business cannot operate without it** (availability focus).
- [ ] Three data states: **at rest** (storage → FDE/AES), **in transit** (network → TLS/IPSec/SSH), **in use** (memory/CPU → secure enclaves); each needs its own protection.
- [ ] **Data sovereignty:** data is subject to the laws of the country where it resides — drives cloud region and replication choices.
- [ ] **Geolocation** is the technical capability (GPS/IP/Wi-Fi) that identifies location and enforces sovereignty and geographic restrictions.
- [ ] **Geographic restrictions/geofencing:** allow or block access and storage based on physical location.
- [ ] **Encryption** is reversible with a key and protects confidentiality; its weakest link is key management.
- [ ] **Hashing** is one-way — integrity checks and salted password storage, never data you need back.
- [ ] **Masking** hides characters on display/in copies (last four digits visible); **tokenization** swaps in a vault-mapped random token with no mathematical link to the original.
- [ ] **Tokenization** can pull merchant systems out of PCI DSS scope because they never store real card numbers.
- [ ] **Obfuscation** (including code obfuscation and steganography) deters analysis but is not a substitute for encryption.
- [ ] **Segmentation** isolates sensitive data stores to block lateral movement; **permission restrictions** (least privilege, need-to-know, ACLs) enforce classification labels.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A payment processor wants its merchants' systems to store a substitute value with no mathematical relationship to the real card number, so a merchant database breach exposes nothing usable and audit scope shrinks. Which data-protection method is this, and how does it differ from encryption?
2. A hospital's security officer must protect patient records on backup tapes, records being transmitted to a partner clinic, and records open in a nurse's charting application. Name the three data states involved and one appropriate protection for each.
3. A multinational firm is told that its EU customers' personal data must be stored on servers physically located within the EU, and it configures its cloud provider to keep the data and all replicas in EU regions. Which *concept* imposes the requirement, and which *capability* verifies and enforces where the data actually is?
4. A help-desk application shows customer SSNs as `***-**-6789` to tier-1 staff, while a separate development team receives a database copy in which all SSNs have been permanently replaced with realistic fake values. Which method is in use in each case, and are the two variants of the same technique?
5. An administrator proposes "encrypting" the company's password database so passwords can be recovered if users forget them. Why is this the wrong control, what should be used instead, and what property makes the correct control appropriate?
