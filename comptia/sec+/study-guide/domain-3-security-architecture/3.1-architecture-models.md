# Objective 3.1 — Compare and contrast security implications of different architecture models

Domain 3 (Security Architecture) is worth **18% of the SY0-701 exam**, and objective 3.1 opens it with the big-picture question every architect faces: *where and how should systems run, and what security trade-offs come with each choice?* The exam tests this material two ways: **definition matching** ("Which model runs code without managing servers?") and **scenario selection** ("A utility company needs its control network completely unreachable from the Internet — which architecture?"). The highest-yield items are the **cloud shared responsibility matrix** (who patches what in IaaS/PaaS/SaaS), **air-gapped vs. logical segmentation**, the **ICS/SCADA/embedded "inability to patch"** theme, **IaC and its risks**, and the **considerations list** (availability, resilience, cost, risk transference, etc.) — expect at least one question that hands you a business requirement and asks which architecture consideration it maps to.

## Architecture and Infrastructure Concepts

An architecture model describes where workloads live (cloud, on-premises, hybrid), how they are built (VMs, containers, microservices, serverless), and what special-purpose systems attach to them (IoT, ICS/SCADA, embedded). Each model shifts the attack surface and shifts *who is responsible* for securing each layer — that shift is what 3.1 asks you to compare.

### Cloud

Cloud computing delivers on-demand compute, storage, and services over the Internet from a provider's infrastructure. Security implication: you give up direct control of hardware and (depending on the model) platform layers, in exchange for provider-managed security, elasticity, and geographic redundancy. Misconfiguration by the *customer* — not provider compromise — is the leading cause of cloud breaches (open storage buckets, over-permissive identities).

Vocabulary you're expected to bring into 3.1 questions:

- **Service models:** **IaaS** (provider supplies VMs/storage/network — you manage OS and up), **PaaS** (provider supplies the platform/runtime — you manage applications and data), **SaaS** (provider supplies the finished application — you manage data and user access).
- **Deployment models:** **public** (shared provider infrastructure, multi-tenant), **private** (dedicated to one organization), **hybrid** (public + private connected), **community** (shared by organizations with a common mission/compliance need).
- **Multi-tenancy** is public cloud's core security concern: other customers' workloads run on the same physical hardware, isolated only by the hypervisor and provider controls.

#### Responsibility matrix

The **shared responsibility model** divides security duties between the cloud service provider (CSP) and the customer. The split depends on the service model, and the matrix below is tested almost verbatim:

| Layer | On-premises | IaaS | PaaS | SaaS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Data & access management | Customer | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** |
| Application | Customer | Customer | Customer | Provider |
| Operating system / patching | Customer | **Customer** | Provider | Provider |
| Virtualization / hypervisor | Customer | Provider | Provider | Provider |
| Physical hardware & facility | Customer | Provider | Provider | Provider |

Two rules cover most questions: (1) the **customer is always responsible for their data, identities, and access configuration** — in every model, including SaaS; (2) the **provider is always responsible for physical security and the underlying infrastructure**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Who patches the OS?" is the classic discriminator. **IaaS → the customer** patches guest operating systems; **PaaS/SaaS → the provider** does. If a scenario blames a breach on an unpatched VM in IaaS, the *customer* failed their side of the responsibility matrix — not the CSP.

#### Hybrid considerations

A **hybrid cloud** combines on-premises (or private cloud) infrastructure with public cloud, connected so workloads and data move between them. Security implications the exam highlights:

- **Expanded attack surface:** two environments plus the connection between them (VPN/dedicated link) must all be secured.
- **Consistency:** identity, encryption, logging, and policy must be enforced uniformly across both sides — gaps appear at the seam. Federated identity/SSO across environments is standard.
- **Data in transit** between environments needs encryption; data residency rules may dictate which data can leave the premises.
- **Split visibility:** monitoring tools must cover both environments or attackers will hide in the one you can't see.

#### Third-party vendors

Cloud adoption means depending on third parties — the CSP itself, SaaS vendors, managed service providers. Security implications: you inherit their vulnerabilities (supply chain risk), you must perform **vendor due diligence** (audits, SOC 2 reports, certifications), and obligations must be written into contracts/SLAs. Critically, **you can transfer the operational work but not the accountability** — regulators hold the data owner responsible even when a vendor caused the breach. Vendor lock-in and vendor outage (a third party's downtime becomes your downtime) are additional availability considerations.

### Infrastructure as code (IaC)

IaC defines and provisions infrastructure through machine-readable **definition files/templates** (e.g., Terraform, CloudFormation, Ansible) instead of manual configuration. Benefits: consistent, repeatable, version-controlled deployments that eliminate configuration drift and enable rapid, identical rebuilds (huge for **ease of recovery**). Security implications:

- Templates can be **security-reviewed and scanned before deployment** — security shifts left.
- A **flawed template replicates the same misconfiguration everywhere**, instantly and at scale.
- Templates stored in repositories may leak **hard-coded secrets** (API keys, passwords) — a top IaC finding.
- The code repository and CI/CD pipeline become high-value targets: compromise the template, compromise every environment built from it.

### Serverless

Serverless (Function as a Service, e.g., AWS Lambda, Azure Functions) runs individual functions on demand; the **provider manages all servers, OS, and runtime** — the customer supplies only code and configuration. The functions are **ephemeral**: they spin up per event, execute, and disappear.

Security implications:

- **No OS for the customer to patch or harden** — infrastructure patching responsibility transfers entirely to the provider.
- **No long-lived servers for attackers to persist on** — short-lived execution shrinks the persistence window.
- Cost and scale track usage automatically (pay-per-execution; near-infinite **scalability**), which also means a flood of malicious invocations can inflate the bill (economic/denial-of-wallet angle).
- Remaining customer responsibilities: **secure code, per-function permissions (least privilege on each function's role), secrets management, and API/event input validation** — injection through event data is the classic serverless attack path.
- Risks: heavy provider dependence (**vendor lock-in**, provider outages become your outages) and reduced visibility/monitoring into the underlying stack.

### Microservices

A microservice architecture decomposes an application into many **small, independent services** that communicate over APIs — versus a **monolithic** application where everything runs as one unit.

| | Monolithic | Microservices |
|---|---|---|
| Deployment | One large unit | Many small, independently deployable services |
| Failure impact | One flaw can take down the whole app | A failed service can be isolated (**resilience**) |
| Scaling | Scale the entire app | Scale only the busy service (**scalability**, efficient compute) |
| Attack surface | Fewer, larger interfaces | **Many APIs** — each needs authentication, authorization, encryption |
| Patching | Big, risky updates | Patch one service without redeploying everything |

Security implications: each service and API is an entry point, so **service-to-service authentication**, TLS between services, and per-service least privilege are required; the increased complexity also complicates monitoring.

### Network infrastructure

How the network itself is partitioned is an architecture decision with direct security consequences.

#### Physical isolation

Physical isolation separates systems with **physically distinct hardware and cabling** — separate switches, separate NICs, no shared media.

- **Air-gapped:** the extreme form — the network has **no physical or wireless connection to any other network**, especially the Internet. Used for classified systems, critical ICS/SCADA, and backup vaults. An air gap defeats remote network attacks entirely, but is defeated by **removable media (USB), rogue/transient devices, and insiders** — Stuxnet famously jumped an air gap via USB. Air gaps also make patching and data transfer slow and manual (a cost/operations trade-off).

#### Logical segmentation

Logical segmentation divides one physical network into isolated zones using **VLANs, subnets, and firewall rules** — no separate cabling required. It is cheaper and more flexible than physical isolation and is the standard way to limit **lateral movement**, contain untrusted device classes (IoT, guest), and satisfy compliance scoping. The trade-off: it depends on correct configuration — a misconfigured switch or firewall rule (e.g., VLAN hopping exposure) can bridge segments, whereas an air gap cannot be misconfigured open.

#### Software-defined networking (SDN)

SDN separates the network's **control plane** (decision-making) from the **data plane** (packet forwarding) and centralizes control in a software **SDN controller**. (A third layer, the **application/management plane**, is where policy is defined.) Security implications:

- **Pro:** network-wide policy from one place, dynamic **microsegmentation**, programmatic response to attacks (e.g., automatically isolating a compromised host), and infrastructure that can be managed as code.
- **Con:** the **controller is a single point of failure and a high-value target** — compromising it means controlling the entire network. Harden it, restrict its APIs, and make it redundant.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Match the isolation keyword. "**No connection whatsoever** / must survive nation-state remote attack" → **air-gapped (physical isolation)**. "Separate untrusted devices **without new cabling/hardware**" → **logical segmentation (VLANs)**. "**Centralized, programmable** network control / separate control and data planes" → **SDN**.

### On-premises

On-premises means the organization **owns and operates its infrastructure in its own facilities**. Security implications: **full control** over hardware, data location, and every security layer — attractive for strict regulatory or data-sovereignty requirements — but full control means **full responsibility**: the organization funds and staffs physical security, redundancy, patching, and hardware refresh itself.

| | On-premises | Cloud |
|---|---|---|
| Control | Full — hardware, data location, every layer | Shared — provider controls layers below your service model |
| Cost model | **CapEx** (buy hardware up front) + ongoing facilities | **OpEx** (pay-as-you-go) |
| Scalability | Slow — purchase, rack, and configure hardware | Elastic — provision in minutes |
| Responsibility | Entirely yours | Split per the **responsibility matrix** |
| Availability | Limited by your own **power**, cooling, connectivity, staffing | Provider zones/regions offer built-in geographic redundancy |
| Best fit | Data sovereignty, strict regulation, full-control mandates | Variable demand, limited capital, rapid deployment |

### Centralized vs. decentralized

| | Centralized | Decentralized |
|---|---|---|
| Structure | Resources/decisions in one place (data center, central IT, central identity) | Distributed across sites, teams, or nodes |
| Management & policy | Uniform, easier to secure/monitor/audit consistently | Harder to enforce consistently; visibility gaps |
| Failure model | **Single point of failure** — central outage or compromise affects everyone | One node's failure/compromise has limited blast radius (**resilience**) |
| Attack value | One high-value target | Many smaller targets; larger total attack surface |
| Examples | Central AD/identity provider, single HQ data center | Branch-office servers, edge computing, blockchain |

The exam wants the trade-off stated both ways: centralization buys **consistency and control** at the cost of a single point of failure; decentralization buys **resilience and local autonomy** at the cost of consistent policy enforcement.

### Containerization

Containers (e.g., Docker) package an application with its dependencies and run on a **shared host operating system kernel** — no guest OS per instance. They are lightweight, start in seconds, and deploy identically everywhere (they underpin microservices). Security implications: containers provide **weaker isolation than VMs** because all containers share the host kernel — a kernel exploit or **container escape** can expose every container on the host. Additional risks: vulnerable or malicious **base images** pulled from public registries (scan images before use), secrets baked into images, and over-privileged containers (never run privileged/root without cause). The container orchestrator (e.g., Kubernetes) is itself a high-value target.

### Virtualization

Virtualization runs multiple complete **virtual machines** — each with its own guest OS — on one physical host, managed by a **hypervisor** (Type 1/bare-metal or Type 2/hosted). Benefits: hardware consolidation (cost, power), snapshots and easy rollback (**ease of recovery**), and strong isolation between guests. Security implications and vocabulary:

- **VM escape:** an attacker breaks out of a guest VM to attack the hypervisor or other VMs — the worst-case virtualization attack; keep hypervisors patched.
- **VM sprawl:** unmanaged proliferation of VMs (so easy to create, nobody tracks them) leaves forgotten, unpatched machines running — inventory and lifecycle management are the fix.
- **Resource reuse:** memory/storage reallocated from one VM to another can leak residual data if not sanitized.
- The **hypervisor is the critical attack surface** — compromise it and every guest falls.

| | Virtual machines | Containers |
|---|---|---|
| OS | Full guest OS per VM | Share the **host OS kernel** |
| Isolation | **Strong** (hypervisor-enforced) | Weaker (kernel-level, namespace-based) |
| Overhead / density | Heavier, fewer per host | Lightweight, high density, fast startup |
| Key attack | VM escape (hypervisor) | Container escape (shared kernel), poisoned images |
| Patching | Patch each guest OS | Rebuild/redeploy from a patched image |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Don't say containers are "more secure because they're smaller." Containers **share the host kernel**, so isolation is *weaker* than VM isolation. VMs isolate at the hypervisor level; containers isolate at the OS level. If a scenario demands the strongest isolation between tenant workloads, VMs (or separate hosts) beat containers.

### IoT (Internet of Things)

IoT devices — smart cameras, sensors, thermostats, wearables, smart TVs, building controls — embed network connectivity in everyday objects. Security implications: **weak or default credentials, infrequent or nonexistent patches, minimal onboard security** (no room for endpoint agents), long service lives, and enormous numbers. Compromised IoT devices become **botnet** members (Mirai) or pivot points into the internal network. Mitigations to know: change default credentials, **segment IoT onto its own VLAN/network**, and keep firmware updated where possible.

### Industrial control systems (ICS)/supervisory control and data acquisition (SCADA)

ICS is the category of systems controlling **physical industrial processes** (manufacturing, energy, water); **SCADA** is the ICS architecture that monitors and controls **large-scale, geographically dispersed** operations (power grids, pipelines, water treatment) from central supervisory stations, using components such as PLCs (programmable logic controllers), RTUs, and HMIs (human-machine interfaces). Security implications:

- Designed for **reliability and safety, not security** — legacy protocols (e.g., Modbus) often lack authentication and encryption.
- **Cannot tolerate downtime**, so patching windows are rare or nonexistent (**inability to patch** in practice, even when a patch exists).
- Compromise causes **physical damage and safety incidents** — not just data loss (availability and safety outrank confidentiality here, inverting the usual IT priority).
- Standard defense: **strict segmentation or air-gapping from IT networks** with tightly controlled gateways, plus compensating controls (monitoring, allow-listing) where patching is impossible.

### Real-time operating system (RTOS)

An RTOS is an operating system that guarantees processing within **strict, deterministic time constraints** — used where a late response is a failure: vehicle systems (braking, engine control), medical devices, avionics, industrial controllers. Security implications: RTOSes are **minimal by design**, often lacking room for authentication, encryption, or security agents; anything that introduces delay (including some security controls and even scanning) can break real-time guarantees; and updates are rare because recertification/revalidation is costly. Protect RTOS devices with **network isolation and strict input control** rather than host-based security.

### Embedded systems

An embedded system is a computer **built into a larger device to perform a dedicated function** — firmware in medical devices, printers, vehicles, ATMs, smart appliances, PLCs. (IoT devices, RTOS devices, and ICS components are largely embedded systems — these categories overlap.) Security implications: fixed, constrained hardware that **can't be upgraded or run security software**; vendors may never release patches or require lengthy regulatory recertification (medical devices); very **long lifespans** mean devices outlive vendor support; and default/hard-coded credentials are common. Compensating controls — **segmentation, restricted network access, monitoring** — substitute for the hardening you can't do on the device.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** IoT, embedded systems, RTOS, and ICS/SCADA questions almost never have "install antivirus/EDR on the device" or "patch it immediately" as the right answer — these devices frequently **can't run agents and can't be patched** (no patch exists, or downtime/recertification forbids it). The correct answer pattern is **compensating controls: network segmentation/isolation, changed default credentials, and monitoring**.

### High availability

High availability (HA) designs systems to **remain operational despite component failures**, minimizing downtime through **redundancy and failover**: clustered servers, load balancers, redundant power/network paths, and replicated data across zones or regions. Building blocks to recognize:

- **Redundancy:** duplicate components (power supplies, NICs, links, servers, sites) so no single failure stops service — eliminating **single points of failure** is the design goal.
- **Failover:** automatic switchover to a standby when the primary fails. **Active-active** clusters share load across all nodes (capacity + availability); **active-passive** keeps a standby idle until failover.
- **Load balancing:** distributes traffic across servers, masking individual server failures and absorbing traffic surges.
- Availability targets are expressed in "nines": 99.9% ≈ 8.8 hours of downtime per year; **99.999% ("five nines") ≈ about 5 minutes per year**.

Security relevance: availability is one third of the CIA triad — HA is the architectural answer to DoS, hardware failure, and site loss. Trade-off: every redundant component adds **cost** and complexity (more systems to secure and patch), so the required availability level should be driven by business impact, not maximalism.

## Considerations

The second half of 3.1 is a checklist of factors used to compare the models above. The exam presents a business statement and asks which consideration it represents — learn the keyword for each. Two clusters deserve special attention:

- **Availability / resilience / ease of recovery** are related but distinct: availability is *being up*, resilience is *withstanding failure and continuing*, ease of recovery is *how fast you restore* after failure. A single scenario can invoke all three.
- **Patch availability vs. inability to patch** is the embedded/ICS theme in consideration form: first ask *does a patch exist and can it be applied easily* (cloud/SaaS = automatic; mainstream OS = routine), then ask *can this system be patched at all* (legacy SCADA, certified medical devices = no → compensating controls).

| Consideration | What it means | Scenario keywords / example |
|---|---|---|
| **Availability** | The system is up and accessible when needed | "Must be reachable 24/7," uptime SLA, nines |
| **Resilience** | Ability to withstand *and recover from* failures/attacks and keep functioning | "Continue operating even if a component/site fails" |
| **Cost** | CapEx vs. OpEx; cloud shifts capital purchases to pay-as-you-go operational spend | "Reduce upfront hardware investment" → cloud |
| **Responsiveness** | How quickly the system reacts to requests/events (latency) | "Users complain of lag"; RTOS determinism; edge computing for low latency |
| **Scalability** | Ability to grow (or shrink) resources with demand | "Handle seasonal traffic spikes" → cloud elasticity/serverless |
| **Ease of deployment** | How quickly/simply new systems can be stood up | IaC templates, containers, SaaS = fast; on-prem hardware = slow |
| **Risk transference** | Shifting risk to a third party (CSP responsibilities, cybersecurity insurance) | "Provider is contractually responsible for infrastructure security" |
| **Ease of recovery** | How quickly normal operation is restored after failure | Cloud snapshots, IaC rebuilds, VM restore vs. rebuilding physical servers |
| **Patch availability** | Whether vendors supply updates at all, and how easily they're applied | Cloud/SaaS auto-patched; mainstream OS = regular patches |
| **Inability to patch** | Systems that *cannot* be updated — no vendor patch, downtime forbidden, recertification required | Legacy ICS/SCADA, embedded/medical devices → **compensating controls + segmentation** |
| **Power** | Electrical supply and its redundancy (UPS, generators, dual feeds); a constraint for on-prem and embedded devices | "Data center outage from utility failure"; battery-limited IoT sensors |
| **Compute** | Available processing capacity | Embedded/IoT too weak for encryption or agents; cloud offers elastic compute |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** **Resilience vs. availability** trips people up: *availability* is the outcome (it's up), *resilience* is the property that produces it under stress (it absorbs failures and recovers). And **risk transference** in this objective usually means using a **cloud provider or third party** so that part of the security burden — and some of the risk — contractually shifts to them. Remember: responsibility for tasks transfers; **accountability for the data does not**.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Shared responsibility matrix: customer always owns **data and access**; provider always owns **physical/infrastructure**; OS patching = customer in **IaaS**, provider in **PaaS/SaaS**.
- [ ] Hybrid cloud's weak spot is the **seam**: inconsistent policy, unencrypted links, and split visibility between environments.
- [ ] Third-party vendors: due diligence + contracts/SLAs; you can transfer work and risk, **never accountability**.
- [ ] **IaC** gives consistent, version-controlled, rapidly rebuildable infrastructure — but one bad template replicates a misconfiguration everywhere, and repos can leak secrets.
- [ ] **Serverless**: provider manages everything below the code; customer still secures code, permissions, and inputs.
- [ ] **Microservices** = many small services and many APIs — isolate failures and scale independently, but authenticate and encrypt every service-to-service call.
- [ ] **Air-gapped** = no network connection at all; defeated by USB/removable media and insiders, not by remote attack.
- [ ] **Logical segmentation** (VLANs/firewalls) isolates without new hardware; **SDN** centralizes control but the controller is a single point of failure.
- [ ] **On-premises** = full control and full responsibility (CapEx); cloud trades control for elasticity and OpEx.
- [ ] **Centralized** = consistent control, single point of failure; **decentralized** = resilient, harder to govern uniformly.
- [ ] **VMs** isolate strongly via hypervisor (watch VM escape/sprawl/resource reuse); **containers** share the host kernel — weaker isolation, scan base images.
- [ ] IoT, embedded, RTOS, ICS/SCADA share one answer pattern: **can't patch, can't run agents → segment, change defaults, monitor** (compensating controls).
- [ ] ICS/SCADA prioritize **availability and safety** over confidentiality; compromise causes physical harm.
- [ ] **High availability** = redundancy + failover measured in "nines"; more nines = more cost.
- [ ] Considerations mapping: elasticity → scalability; keeps working through failure → resilience; provider assumes duties → risk transference; snapshots/IaC rebuild → ease of recovery.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A company hosts virtual machines in an IaaS environment. After a breach, investigators find the guest OS was missing eighteen months of security patches. The company argues the cloud provider was responsible. Who was actually responsible, and why?
2. A power utility runs 20-year-old SCADA equipment whose vendor no longer exists, so no patches will ever be released. Downtime is unacceptable. What is the appropriate security approach, and which two "considerations" from objective 3.1 does this scenario illustrate?
3. An architect must choose between deploying tenant workloads in containers on shared hosts or in separate virtual machines. The requirement is the strongest possible isolation between tenants. Which option fits, and what is the key technical reason?
4. A retailer's traffic spikes 20x every holiday season, and leadership wants to avoid buying hardware that sits idle the rest of the year, while also making the infrastructure provider contractually responsible for securing the underlying platform. Which architecture fits, and which two considerations does the request map to?
5. A security team wants network policy managed programmatically from a central controller, with the ability to microsegment and automatically isolate compromised hosts. What technology is described, what new single point of failure does it introduce, and how does it differ from an air gap?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **The customer.** Under the shared responsibility matrix, in **IaaS** the provider secures the physical facility, hardware, and hypervisor, but the **customer is responsible for the guest operating system, its patching, the applications, and data/access configuration**. Only in PaaS/SaaS does OS patching shift to the provider. (And in *every* model, data and identity/access remain the customer's responsibility.)
2. Because the systems exhibit **inability to patch** (no vendor patches will ever exist) and downtime is forbidden, the answer is **compensating controls**: strictly **segment or air-gap** the SCADA network from IT and the Internet with tightly controlled gateways, restrict and monitor all access, and use allow-listing/anomaly detection. The scenario illustrates **inability to patch** and **availability** (the process cannot tolerate downtime — ICS prioritizes availability and safety over confidentiality). *(Patch availability is also acceptable as the flip side of the same point.)*
3. **Separate virtual machines.** VMs are isolated by the **hypervisor**, each with its own guest OS, so a compromise in one tenant must achieve a full **VM escape** to reach another. Containers all **share the host OS kernel**, so a single kernel exploit or container escape can expose every tenant on that host — container isolation is inherently weaker than hypervisor isolation.
4. **Public cloud** (e.g., IaaS or serverless/auto-scaling services). "Handle 20x seasonal spikes without idle hardware" maps to **scalability** (elasticity, and OpEx over CapEx — also a **cost** point), and "provider is contractually responsible for securing the underlying platform" maps to **risk transference** — part of the security burden shifts to the CSP under the shared responsibility model, though accountability for the data stays with the retailer.
5. **Software-defined networking (SDN)** — the control plane is separated from the data plane and centralized in an SDN controller, enabling programmatic policy, microsegmentation, and automated quarantine of compromised hosts. The new risk: the **SDN controller is a single point of failure and a high-value target** — compromising it yields control of the entire network, so it must be hardened, its APIs restricted, and it should be deployed redundantly. An **air gap**, by contrast, is *physical* isolation — no connectivity at all, nothing to configure or program; SDN isolation is *logical* and only as strong as the controller and its configuration.
