# Objective 5.5 — Explain types and purposes of audits and assessments

Domain 5 (Security Program Management and Oversight) is **20% of the exam**, and objective 5.5 covers how organizations *verify* that their security program actually works: audits, assessments, and penetration tests. The exam tests this material almost entirely by **definition matching and scenario selection** — "an unaffiliated firm reviews the controls and issues a report customers can rely on: which audit type?" The highest-yield items are the **internal vs. external split** (who performs the review), **attestation**, the **audit committee's** oversight role, the **known / partially known / unknown environment** trio for penetration testing, the **offensive (red) / defensive (blue) / integrated (purple)** team model, and the **passive vs. active reconnaissance** distinction. If you can answer "who performs it, is it required, and how much do the testers know?", you can answer nearly every question this objective produces.

## Attestation

**Attestation** is a formal declaration by a responsible party that something is true — that controls exist, are accurate, and are working as intended. In practice it appears in two forms:

- **Management attestation:** an executive (often the CEO or CFO) formally signs a statement taking responsibility for the accuracy of a report or the effectiveness of controls. Sarbanes-Oxley (SOX), for example, requires executives to personally attest to the accuracy of financial reporting and the internal controls behind it.
- **Auditor attestation:** an independent auditor examines evidence and issues a written opinion that an organization's claims about its controls are fairly stated. A **SOC 2** report is an attestation engagement — a licensed CPA firm, following AICPA standards, attests that the service organization's controls meet the relevant trust criteria.

The purpose of attestation is **accountability and assurance**: someone with authority puts their name (and liability) behind the claim, which is what lets customers, regulators, and partners rely on it.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Attestation = a *formal signed statement* that controls or reports are accurate. Look for verbs like "certifies," "signs off," "formally declares." The person attesting takes responsibility for the claim.

## Internal Audits and Assessments

Internal reviews are performed by the organization's **own personnel** — internal auditors, compliance staff, or the teams themselves. They are cheaper, faster, and can run continuously, but they lack the independence of an external review: an internal team may (consciously or not) go easy on its own organization.

### Compliance

Internal **compliance** reviews verify that the organization is following its **own policies and procedures as well as external legal, regulatory, and contractual requirements** — password policies actually enforced, access reviews actually performed, required training actually completed. A dedicated compliance function typically monitors this continuously rather than once a year, catching gaps *before* an external auditor or regulator does.

### Audit committee

The **audit committee** is a subgroup of the **board of directors** responsible for **oversight** of the organization's audit and compliance activities. It does not perform audits itself — it commissions them, reviews their results, oversees the internal audit function, selects and communicates with external auditors, and ensures that findings are actually remediated. Because internal auditors report to the audit committee (not to the managers whose work they audit), the committee is what gives the internal audit function its independence.

### Self-assessments

A **self-assessment** is a review an organization (or an individual team) performs on **itself**, typically against a checklist, framework, or standard — e.g., a team walks through the CIS Controls or a PCI DSS Self-Assessment Questionnaire (SAQ) and scores its own compliance. Self-assessments are inexpensive, build internal awareness, and are excellent *preparation* for a formal audit — but because the assessors are grading their own work, the results carry the **least independence** of any review type and are rarely accepted as proof by outside parties.

## External Audits and Assessments

External reviews are performed by parties **outside the organization** — regulators, examiners, consultants, or independent audit firms. They cost more, but their independence is exactly what makes their results credible to customers, partners, and authorities.

### Regulatory

A **regulatory** audit is **required by law or regulation** and verifies compliance with that specific requirement — HIPAA for healthcare data, SOX for financial reporting at public companies, GLBA for financial institutions. (PCI DSS assessments work the same way but are technically a *contractual* obligation imposed by the card brands rather than a law.) Regulatory audits are not optional, are conducted by or on behalf of the regulating authority, and failing one can bring **fines, sanctions, or loss of the ability to operate**.

### Examinations

An **examination** is a formal inspection conducted by an external authority — the classic example is a **bank examiner** from a government agency reviewing a financial institution's practices, records, and controls. Examinations are typically performed by (or for) regulators, can be scheduled or unannounced, and tend to be broader and more formal than a focused audit. The organization being examined has little say in scope or timing.

### Assessment

An external **assessment** is an in-depth analysis by an outside party aimed at **identifying gaps and recommending improvements** rather than issuing a formal pass/fail opinion. Think of a consulting firm evaluating your security program against NIST CSF and delivering a roadmap. Assessments are usually **voluntary** and improvement-oriented; audits are evidence-driven and verdict-oriented. Organizations often run an assessment first to find and fix gaps, *then* undergo the formal audit.

### Independent third-party audit

An **independent third-party audit** is performed by an **unaffiliated outside firm** with no stake in the outcome — the auditors are not employees, do not report to management, and follow professional audit standards. Examples: a SOC 2 audit by a CPA firm, or an ISO/IEC 27001 certification audit by an accredited certification body. Because of that independence, the resulting report is the form of assurance that **customers, partners, and regulators trust most** — it is how a cloud provider proves its security to thousands of customers without letting each one audit it individually.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Sort by two questions. **Who performs it?** Own staff → internal; outsiders → external. **Is it required?** Mandated by law → *regulatory* (or an *examination* by the authority itself); voluntary and advisory → *assessment*; voluntary but formal, independent, and relied on by others → *independent third-party audit*. And the **audit committee** never performs audits — it *oversees* them on behalf of the board.

## Penetration Testing

A **penetration test** is an **authorized, simulated attack** against an organization's systems, people, or facilities. Unlike a vulnerability scan, which only *identifies* potential weaknesses, a pen test **actively exploits** them to prove what an attacker could really accomplish. Every pen test starts with defined **rules of engagement** — written scope, targets, timing, and permissions — because without authorization the same activity is simply a crime.

### Physical

**Physical** penetration testing targets the facility rather than the network: testers attempt **tailgating** through secured doors, **badge cloning**, **lock picking**, defeating cameras and sensors, **dumpster diving** for sensitive documents, or simply talking their way past reception to plant a rogue device. It answers the question every network-only test skips: can an attacker just walk in?

### Offensive

**Offensive** testing is attack-side work — the **red team**. Operators emulate real adversaries: reconnaissance, exploitation, persistence, lateral movement, and data exfiltration, often over an extended campaign. The purpose is to find what a determined attacker would find, and to test whether anyone notices.

### Defensive

**Defensive** testing exercises the protection side — the **blue team**. The focus is on how well the organization **detects, responds to, and contains** attacks: monitoring, alerting, log analysis, incident response. A defensive exercise measures whether the alarms actually ring and whether the responders actually respond.

### Integrated

**Integrated** testing combines offense and defense in a cooperative exercise — commonly called **purple teaming**. Instead of the red team reporting results weeks later, attackers and defenders work together in real time: red runs a technique, blue checks whether it was detected, gaps are fixed, and the technique is run again. The purpose is a continuous feedback loop that improves detection, not just a list of findings.

### Test environments: how much do the testers know?

| Environment | Legacy name | Testers are given | Simulates |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Known environment** | White box | Full knowledge — architecture diagrams, documentation, source code, credentials | A malicious insider; maximizes depth and coverage per hour of testing |
| **Partially known environment** | Gray box | Some knowledge — e.g., network ranges or a standard user account, but not full internals | An attacker with limited inside information (or a compromised user) |
| **Unknown environment** | Black box | Nothing — testers start from scratch like a real outsider | A real external attacker; most realistic, but slowest and may miss deep flaws |

### Reconnaissance

Reconnaissance is the information-gathering phase of a test (and of a real attack). The exam draws one bright line through it:

- **Passive reconnaissance** gathers information **without directly interacting with the target's systems**: OSINT sources such as WHOIS records, public DNS data, the company website, social media, job postings (which reveal the technology stack), press releases, and breach dumps. Because no packets are sent to the target, passive recon is **essentially undetectable**.
- **Active reconnaissance** **directly probes the target**: ping sweeps, **port scanning** (e.g., Nmap), banner grabbing, vulnerability scanning, DNS zone-transfer attempts. It yields far more detail — live hosts, open ports, service versions — but every probe touches the target's systems and therefore **can be detected and logged**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The recon question is almost always "which one touches the target?" Reading LinkedIn and job postings → **passive**. Running Nmap → **active**. And map the team colors: **red = offensive, blue = defensive, purple = integrated** — while **known / partially known / unknown** map to the older **white / gray / black box** terms.

## Quick Reference

| Type | Performed by | Required? | Purpose |
|---|---|---|---|
| Attestation | Management or independent auditor | Sometimes (e.g., SOX) | Formal signed declaration that controls/reports are accurate |
| Internal — Compliance | Own compliance staff | Internal policy | Verify adherence to policies, laws, and contracts |
| Internal — Audit committee | Board subgroup | Governance | Oversee audit activity; receive results; ensure remediation |
| Internal — Self-assessment | The team itself | Voluntary | Checklist-based self-review; audit preparation; least independent |
| External — Regulatory | Regulator or its agent | Mandatory (law) | Verify legal compliance; noncompliance brings fines/sanctions |
| External — Examination | External authority (e.g., bank examiner) | Mandatory | Formal, broad inspection of practices and records |
| External — Assessment | Outside consultant | Voluntary | Identify gaps, recommend improvements; no pass/fail verdict |
| External — Independent third-party audit | Unaffiliated audit firm | Voluntary/contractual | Trusted formal opinion (SOC 2, ISO 27001) for customers and partners |
| Penetration testing | Authorized testers | Voluntary/contractual (e.g., PCI DSS) | Actively exploit weaknesses to prove real-world impact |
