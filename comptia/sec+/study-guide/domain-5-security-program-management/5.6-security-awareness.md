# Objective 5.6 — Given a scenario, implement security awareness practices

Domain 5 (Security Program Management and Oversight) is **20% of the exam**, and objective 5.6 is the *human* layer of security: training people to recognize attacks, behave safely, and report problems. The exam tests it with scenario questions — "an employee does X: which behavior category is this?" or "a user reports a suspicious email: what does the security team do first?" The highest-yield items are the **phishing simulation campaign** and the **response workflow for reported messages**, the three **anomalous behavior categories (risky / unexpected / unintentional)**, the individual **user guidance topics** (passwords, removable media and cables, OPSEC, hybrid/remote work), and the awareness **program lifecycle: development → execution → initial and recurring reporting/monitoring**. If you can classify a behavior and sequence the program lifecycle, you can answer nearly everything this objective produces.

## Phishing

Phishing remains the most common initial attack vector, so it anchors every awareness program.

### Campaigns

A **phishing campaign** (phishing simulation) is a controlled exercise in which the security team — or a vendor platform — sends **simulated phishing emails to the organization's own users**. Users who click, open the attachment, or enter credentials are directed to **immediate, short remedial training** ("you just clicked a simulated phish — here's what you missed"). The campaign measures the organization's real-world susceptibility with metrics such as **click rate** (percentage who fell for it) and **report rate** (percentage who reported it), and repeated campaigns show whether training is actually working. The goal is measurement and education, **not punishment** — shaming or disciplining clickers discourages the reporting culture the program depends on.

### Recognizing a phishing attempt

Training teaches users the classic red flags:

- **Mismatched or spoofed sender addresses** — display name says "Microsoft Support," actual address is `support@micros0ft-security.com`
- **Urgency, fear, or threats** — "your account will be closed in 24 hours"
- **Generic greetings** ("Dear Customer"), spelling and grammar errors
- **Suspicious links** — hovering over the link reveals a URL different from the displayed text or an unrelated domain
- **Unexpected attachments** or requests to enable macros
- **Requests for credentials, payment changes, or sensitive data** — especially impersonating executives or vendors (business email compromise)
- **Offers too good to be true**

The behavioral rule that ties it together: **verify unexpected requests through a separate, known-good channel** (call the person at their known number) instead of replying or clicking.

### Responding to reported suspicious messages

A mature program makes reporting effortless — typically a **"report phishing" button** in the mail client or a dedicated abuse mailbox — and defines what happens next:

1. The user **reports** the suspicious message (and does not click, reply, or forward it to coworkers).
2. The security team **triages and analyzes** it (headers, links, attachments in a sandbox) to determine whether it is malicious.
3. If confirmed malicious, the team **contains** it: remove all copies from other users' mailboxes, block the sender/domain/URLs, and add indicators to email filters.
4. The team **identifies anyone who clicked or entered credentials**, resets affected credentials, and checks for compromise.
5. The reporter receives **feedback and thanks** — positive reinforcement is what keeps reports coming.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** After a user reports a message, the security team's *first* action is to **analyze it** — not mass-delete, not reset every password. And phishing simulations exist to **measure and train, not to punish**; punishing clickers kills the reporting culture.

## Anomalous Behavior Recognition

Awareness programs train both users and security staff to recognize three categories of anomalous behavior. The exam gives you a scenario and asks which bucket it falls in.

- **Risky:** the user **deliberately does something dangerous**, though usually not maliciously — disabling antivirus or other security tools, installing unauthorized software (shadow IT), plugging in personal USB drives, sharing passwords, visiting risky websites, or bypassing security controls "to get work done faster."
- **Unexpected:** the behavior is **out of pattern** for that user or account — logons at 3 a.m., logons from an unfamiliar country, **impossible travel** (logons from two distant locations too close together in time), accessing systems or data the user has never touched, or abnormally large downloads. Unexpected behavior may signal a compromised account or an insider problem and warrants investigation.
- **Unintentional:** an **honest mistake** — emailing a sensitive file to the wrong recipient, misconfiguring a share, losing a laptop or phone, clicking a phishing link without realizing it. The response is correction and training, not blame.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Classify by intent and pattern. Deliberate-but-not-malicious shortcut → **risky**. Out-of-pattern for that account (odd hour, odd place, odd system) → **unexpected**. Honest mistake → **unintentional**.

## User Guidance and Training

The training curriculum itself. Security+ names eight topics; know what each covers and be able to pick it out of a scenario.

### Policy/handbooks

Security expectations must be **written down and accessible**: the **employee handbook**, the **acceptable use policy (AUP)**, and related security policies. New hires review and acknowledge them during **onboarding**, and training references them so users know both the rules and where to find them.

### Situational awareness

Staying **alert to your surroundings**, physical and digital: noticing someone **tailgating** through a badge-controlled door, a stranger without a badge in a secure area, **shoulder surfing** on a train, a USB drive left in the lobby, or a system behaving oddly. Situational awareness is the habit of noticing — and reporting — things that are out of place.

### Insider threat

Users are trained to recognize and report **indicators of a potentially malicious insider**: hoarding or exfiltrating data unrelated to the job, attempting access beyond their role, repeatedly bypassing controls, working unusual hours without cause, or expressed disgruntlement combined with the above. Employees who notice indicators should report them through defined channels (manager, security, or an anonymous hotline) rather than confront the person.

### Password management

Modern guidance (aligned with **NIST SP 800-63B**): use **long passphrases** — length beats complexity; use a **unique password for every account**, stored in a **password manager**; enable **multifactor authentication (MFA)** wherever possible; never share or reuse passwords; and change a password **when there is evidence of compromise** rather than on an arbitrary calendar schedule.

### Removable media and cables

- **Found or gifted USB drives are never plugged in** — drive-drop **baiting** is a standard attack; hand found media to security.
- **Untrusted cables and chargers are also attack hardware** — malicious cables (e.g., the O.MG cable) look identical to normal ones but contain implants that inject keystrokes or exfiltrate data.
- **Juice jacking:** public USB charging ports can attempt data theft or malware installation; use an AC wall adapter, a battery pack, or a USB **data blocker** instead.
- Organizations commonly enforce this with policy and technical controls that block unauthorized removable media.

### Social engineering

Recognizing manipulation in all channels: **phishing** (email), **vishing** (voice calls), **smishing** (SMS), **pretexting** (invented scenarios), impersonation of IT staff or executives, and **business email compromise (BEC)** payment fraud. The universal defense taught to users: slow down, be suspicious of urgency and authority pressure, and **verify requests out-of-band** through a known-good contact method before acting. IT will never ask for your password.

### Operational security

**Operational security (OPSEC)** means viewing day-to-day activity **from an adversary's perspective** and protecting the small, seemingly harmless pieces of information an attacker could aggregate: don't overshare on social media (badge photos, whiteboards in selfies, job postings that reveal the internal stack), follow a **clean desk policy**, shred sensitive documents, and don't discuss sensitive work in public places.

### Hybrid/remote work environments

Remote and hybrid workers get additional guidance: secure the **home network** (change the router's default credentials, use WPA3 — or at minimum WPA2 — encryption, keep firmware updated); use the **company VPN** on untrusted networks and avoid public Wi-Fi for sensitive work without it; maintain **physical security** at home (lock screens, keep family members off work devices, privacy screens in public); keep work and personal use separated; and stay mindful of what's visible or audible on video calls.

## Reporting and Monitoring

An awareness program must prove it works, which means measuring behavior and reporting the results to management.

- **Initial:** the **first measurement — the baseline**. The first phishing campaign's click rate, the initial training completion rate, the first survey of security knowledge. Initial reporting establishes the starting point against which all future results are compared.
- **Recurring:** **ongoing, repeated measurement** — quarterly phishing simulations, annual refresher-training completion, monthly metrics dashboards to leadership. Recurring monitoring reveals **trends** (is the click rate falling? is the report rate rising?) and shows whether the program is improving behavior over time.

## Development

**Development** is building the program: define its **scope and objectives**, identify the **audiences** and tailor content to roles (developers, finance, executives, and privileged IT staff face different threats), select topics based on the organization's actual threat landscape and past incidents, choose delivery methods and metrics, and secure **executive support**. Guidance such as **NIST SP 800-50** (building security awareness and training programs) is commonly used as a framework.

## Execution

**Execution** is running the program: deliver training at **onboarding** and through **recurring refreshers**, run phishing simulation campaigns, reinforce continuously with newsletters, posters, and awareness events, **track completion and results**, and feed the metrics from reporting and monitoring back into the next development cycle so content stays current with new threats.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The lifecycle sequence is **development → execution → initial report (baseline) → recurring reporting/monitoring**, then update and repeat. *Development* = design the program; *execution* = deliver it; *initial* = first baseline measurement; *recurring* = ongoing trend measurement.
