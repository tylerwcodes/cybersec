# Objective 1.1 — Compare and contrast various types of security controls

Domain 1 is worth **12% of the SY0-701 exam**, and objective 1.1 is the very first thing the objectives list — because the entire exam is, at its core, about selecting the right control for a situation. This objective gives you the classification grid used everywhere else: every control belongs to a **category** (who implements it and how: technical, managerial, operational, or physical) and serves one or more **types** (what it accomplishes: preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, or directive). The exam tests this two ways: **definition matching** ("Which control type discourages an attacker without physically stopping them?") and **scenario selection** ("A legacy system cannot be patched — which control type is the firewall rule protecting it?"). The highest-yield items are the **preventive vs. deterrent distinction**, **compensating controls** (almost always paired with a "cannot patch/cannot implement the primary control" scenario), **detective vs. corrective** (before-vs-after the incident), the **managerial vs. operational split** (policy on paper vs. people performing procedures), and the fact that **one control can be classified in multiple ways** — a category *and* one or more types at once.

## Security Control Fundamentals

A **security control** is any safeguard or countermeasure that reduces risk — it protects the confidentiality, integrity, or availability of systems and data by preventing incidents, reducing their impact, or detecting them so you can respond. Controls exist because risk can never be eliminated, only managed: you deploy layers of controls (**defense in depth**) so that the failure of any single control does not equal a breach.

SY0-701 classifies every control along two independent axes:

| Axis | Question it answers | Values |
|---|---|---|
| **Category** | *Who implements it, and with what?* | Technical, Managerial, Operational, Physical |
| **Type** | *What does it do relative to an incident?* | Preventive, Deterrent, Detective, Corrective, Compensating, Directive |

These axes combine: a firewall is a **technical preventive** control; a security guard is an **operational** control that is simultaneously **preventive**, **deterrent**, and **detective**. Exam questions ask you to classify from either axis, so learn them separately, then practice combining them.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Category and type are **independent** classifications. When a question asks "what *category* is this control?" the answer set is technical/managerial/operational/physical. When it asks "what *type*?" the answer set is preventive/deterrent/detective/corrective/compensating/directive. Misreading which axis the question wants is the most common way to lose easy points on 1.1.

## Control Categories

Categories describe the *mechanism* of the control — is it enforced by systems, defined by management, carried out by people, or built into the physical world?

### Technical controls

Technical controls (also called logical controls) are implemented **by systems** — hardware, software, and firmware enforce them automatically, without a human in the loop at enforcement time.

Examples:

- **Firewalls and ACLs** — filter traffic based on rules.
- **Encryption** — protects data at rest and in transit.
- **Antivirus/EDR** — detects and blocks malware on endpoints.
- **Authentication systems and MFA** — verify identity before granting access.
- **IDS/IPS** — detect (and for IPS, block) intrusion attempts.
- **Operating system permissions / file ACLs** — enforce who can read or modify data.

Keyword mapping: if the control is *configured once and enforced by a device or software*, it's technical.

### Managerial controls

Managerial controls (also called administrative controls) are the **oversight layer**: policies, procedures, standards, and risk-management decisions that govern how security is designed and directed. They are documents and decisions, not devices.

Examples:

- **Security policies** (acceptable use policy, password policy, data classification policy).
- **Risk assessments and risk registers** — identifying and prioritizing what to protect.
- **Vulnerability management program** design — deciding scan frequency and patching SLAs.
- **Compliance and audit planning**, security awareness *program* design.
- **Vendor/third-party risk reviews** and security requirements in contracts.

Keyword mapping: *policy, plan, assessment, review, governance, "management decides"* → managerial.

### Operational controls

Operational controls are implemented and executed **by people** performing day-to-day procedures — the human activity that puts managerial intent into practice.

Examples:

- **Security guards** performing patrols and checking badges.
- **Security awareness training** sessions delivered to users.
- **Change management procedures** being followed for a maintenance window.
- **Incident response exercises**, log review performed by analysts.
- **Account provisioning/deprovisioning procedures** carried out by IT staff.
- **Backup verification and media handling** performed by operators.

Keyword mapping: *a person performs a procedure, patrol, training session, manual review* → operational.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Managerial vs. operational is the most-confused pair. The **policy document requiring annual awareness training is managerial**; the **training class actually being delivered is operational**. Managerial = the plan/policy on paper; operational = people executing procedures. Similarly, the change-management *policy* is managerial, while following the change procedure during a deployment is operational.

### Physical controls

Physical controls protect **facilities, equipment, and people** in the real world — they limit or monitor physical access to tangible assets.

Examples:

- **Locks, badge readers, and access control vestibules** (mantraps) — restrict entry and stop tailgating.
- **Fences, bollards, and barricades** — perimeter protection; bollards stop vehicles.
- **Security cameras (CCTV)** — record activity for detection and evidence.
- **Lighting and warning signs** — make intrusion visible and discourage attempts.
- **Security guards** (also operational — a person executing a procedure — depending on how the question frames it).
- **Fire suppression, HVAC, cable locks, locked server racks.**

Keyword mapping: *doors, buildings, hardware, perimeter, "walk up to"* → physical.

### Category comparison table

| Category | Implemented by | Enforced through | Classic examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | Systems (hardware/software) | Automated enforcement | Firewall, encryption, MFA, antivirus, IPS, ACLs |
| **Managerial** | Management/governance | Policies, plans, assessments | Security policy, risk assessment, vulnerability management program |
| **Operational** | People | Day-to-day procedures | Guard patrols, awareness training delivery, change/incident procedures, log review |
| **Physical** | Facilities/structures | Tangible barriers and monitoring | Locks, fences, bollards, vestibules, cameras, lighting, guards |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** SY0-701 uses exactly these four category names — **technical, managerial, operational, physical**. Older exams and other frameworks say "administrative" instead of managerial and fold operational into it; if you see "administrative" in a practice question, translate it to **managerial**. On the real exam, answer with the SY0-701 terms.

## Control Types

Types describe the control's *function relative to an incident timeline*: stop it before it happens, discourage the attempt, notice it happening, fix it afterward, stand in for a missing control, or tell people what to do.

### Preventive

A preventive control **blocks the unwanted action from occurring at all** — access is denied, traffic is dropped, the door will not open.

- Firewall rules dropping unauthorized traffic (technical preventive).
- Full-disk encryption making a stolen drive unreadable (technical preventive).
- A locked door or access control vestibule (physical preventive).
- An onboarding process that denies access until background checks complete (operational preventive).
- Least privilege / account permissions preventing unauthorized file access (technical preventive).

Keyword mapping: *block, deny, stop, prevent, restrict* → preventive.

### Deterrent

A deterrent control **discourages the attempt** — it changes the attacker's decision by raising perceived risk or effort, but it does not physically or logically stop the action.

- **Warning signs** ("Premises under 24-hour video surveillance," "Authorized personnel only").
- **Visible cameras and bright lighting** — the *visibility* deters, whether or not recording occurs.
- **Login warning banners** ("Unauthorized access is prosecuted").
- **A guard's visible presence**, guard dogs, the *published existence* of sanctions in policy.

Keyword mapping: *discourage, warning, sign, banner, visible presence, "think twice"* → deterrent.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Preventive vs. deterrent is the classic 1.1 trap. **Preventive physically or logically stops the action; deterrent only discourages it.** A locked door is preventive — even a fearless attacker cannot walk through it. A "beware of dog" sign is deterrent — it stops nobody who ignores it. A camera is a great discriminator: the **visible camera housing is a deterrent**, the **recorded footage is detective**, and the camera is **never preventive** — it cannot stop anyone.

### Detective

A detective control **identifies and records that an incident is occurring or has occurred**. It doesn't stop the event — it makes you aware of it and produces evidence.

- **IDS** (intrusion detection system) alerting on malicious traffic — note: an **IPS** that blocks is preventive.
- **Log monitoring / SIEM alerts**, file integrity monitoring.
- **CCTV footage review**, motion sensors, alarms.
- **Audits and periodic access reviews** discovering violations after the fact.
- **Honeypots** — any interaction reveals attacker presence.

Keyword mapping: *alert, log, monitor, discover, identify, record, audit trail* → detective.

### Corrective

A corrective control **restores systems or reverses damage after an incident has occurred** — it minimizes impact and returns operations to normal.

- **Restoring from backup** after ransomware or data loss.
- **Patching** a vulnerability after it was exploited (patching *before* exploitation is preventive — timing/context decides).
- **Antivirus quarantining/removing** discovered malware.
- **Incident response actions**: re-imaging an infected host, revoking compromised credentials.
- **Fire suppression discharging** to stop a fire in progress.

Keyword mapping: *restore, recover, repair, remediate, "after the incident"* → corrective.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Detective vs. corrective is a timeline question. **Detective tells you it happened** (the SIEM alert, the audit finding); **corrective fixes it** (the backup restore, the re-image). If the scenario's control produces *awareness*, it's detective; if it produces *recovery*, it's corrective. A backup *plan* sitting on the shelf is often argued as preventive/managerial, but on the exam, **restoring from backup = corrective** every time.

### Compensating

A compensating control is an **alternative control that provides equivalent (or acceptable) protection when the primary control is not feasible** — too expensive, technically impossible, or business-prohibited.

- A **legacy system cannot be patched**, so it is **isolated on its own network segment** with strict firewall rules — segmentation compensates for the missing patch.
- MFA hardware tokens cannot be deployed in time, so **time-based one-time passwords via an app** are used instead.
- An application does not support encryption, so the organization **wraps its traffic in a VPN tunnel**.
- Separation of duties is impossible in a two-person office, so **increased logging and management review** compensate.

Keyword mapping: *cannot patch, legacy/end-of-life system, primary control not feasible, alternative measure, "instead"* → compensating. This is one of the most reliably tested terms in Domain 1 — compensating-control questions almost always feature an **unpatchable or constraint-bound system**.

### Directive

A directive control **directs, mandates, or guides behavior** — it tells people what they must (or must not) do, typically through policy, procedure, or posted instruction.

- **Acceptable use policy (AUP)** signed at hire.
- **Standard operating procedures** telling staff how to handle sensitive data.
- **Posted signs directing behavior**: "All visitors must sign in," "Authorized personnel only."
- **Mandatory training requirements** communicated to employees.

Keyword mapping: *policy tells users to..., procedure requires..., sign instructing...* → directive. Directive controls rely on compliance — they don't technically enforce anything.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Directive vs. deterrent for signs and policies: a **directive** control *instructs* ("Visitors must sign in at the front desk"); a **deterrent** control *threatens or discourages* ("Trespassers will be prosecuted"). Likewise an AUP is directive because it tells users the rules — but the *sanctions clause* within it acts as a deterrent. Read whether the wording **guides behavior** (directive) or **discourages an attack/violation** (deterrent).

### Control type comparison table (memorize this)

| Type | Function | Timing relative to incident | Classic examples |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Preventive** | Blocks the action outright | Before | Firewall, locks, encryption, least privilege, vestibule |
| **Deterrent** | Discourages the attempt | Before | Warning signs, login banners, visible cameras, lighting |
| **Detective** | Identifies/records the event | During/after | IDS, SIEM alerts, CCTV footage, audits, motion sensors |
| **Corrective** | Restores/repairs after the event | After | Backup restore, re-imaging, quarantine, patching post-exploit |
| **Compensating** | Substitutes for an infeasible primary control | Any | Segmentation for an unpatchable system, VPN wrap, extra logging |
| **Directive** | Mandates/guides behavior | Before | AUP, SOPs, "visitors must sign in" signage, mandatory training |

## Combining Categories and Types

Every real control sits at an intersection of one category and one or more types — and the exam loves controls that occupy **multiple cells depending on context**. Practice with this matrix:

| | Preventive | Deterrent | Detective | Corrective | Compensating | Directive |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Technical** | Firewall, MFA, encryption | Login warning banner | IDS, SIEM, honeypot | Backup restore, AV quarantine | Segmentation for legacy system | Group Policy notice/prompts |
| **Managerial** | Hiring/screening policy | Published sanction policy | Scheduled audits, access reviews | Incident response plan (invoked) | Policy requiring extra reviews when SoD impossible | AUP, security policies |
| **Operational** | Guard denying entry, provisioning checks | Guard's visible patrol | Log review by analysts, guard noticing tailgating | IR team containment/re-image | Manual review replacing an automated check | Delivering procedure training |
| **Physical** | Locks, vestibule, bollards | Signs, lighting, visible camera housing | CCTV recording, motion alarm | Fire suppression discharge | Guard posted where badge system failed | "Authorized personnel only" sign |

Two rules that resolve most ambiguous questions:

1. **The same control can be multiple types.** A security guard prevents (blocks entry), deters (visible presence), detects (notices tailgating), and can correct (escorts intruder out). A camera deters (visible) and detects (records). The question's *wording* tells you which function it's testing — answer for the function described in the scenario.
2. **Context changes the type.** Patching before exploitation = preventive; patching to remediate an active compromise = corrective. Segmentation as a design choice = preventive; segmentation because you *can't* patch = compensating.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** When a scenario names a control and asks for its type, ignore what the control *could* do and classify what it is *doing in the scenario*. "Cameras were reviewed to determine how the intruder entered" → detective. "Cameras were installed prominently at the entrance to discourage break-ins" → deterrent. Same device, different answer.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] A security control reduces risk to confidentiality, integrity, or availability; layered controls = defense in depth.
- [ ] SY0-701 classifies controls on two independent axes: **category** (technical, managerial, operational, physical) and **type** (preventive, deterrent, detective, corrective, compensating, directive).
- [ ] **Technical** = enforced automatically by systems (firewall, encryption, MFA, IPS, ACLs).
- [ ] **Managerial** = governance and documents (policies, risk assessments, program design) — called "administrative" in older material.
- [ ] **Operational** = people performing day-to-day procedures (guard patrols, delivering training, log review, change procedures).
- [ ] **Physical** = tangible protections (locks, fences, bollards, vestibules, cameras, lighting).
- [ ] Policy requiring training is **managerial**; the training session being delivered is **operational**.
- [ ] **Preventive** blocks the action (locked door); **deterrent** only discourages it (warning sign) — a deterrent stops no one who ignores it.
- [ ] **Detective** identifies/records incidents (IDS, SIEM, CCTV footage, audits); **corrective** restores afterward (backup restore, re-image, quarantine).
- [ ] IDS = detective; IPS = preventive (it blocks). Restoring from backup = corrective, always.
- [ ] **Compensating** = alternative control when the primary is infeasible — trigger phrase: "legacy system cannot be patched," answer: isolate/segment plus strict firewall rules.
- [ ] **Directive** mandates or guides behavior (AUP, SOPs, "visitors must sign in") but does not technically enforce it.
- [ ] One control can be several types at once (guard = preventive + deterrent + detective); classify by the function described in the scenario.
- [ ] Context changes type: patching pre-incident is preventive, post-incident is corrective.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A company posts large signs reading "This facility is monitored by 24-hour video surveillance — trespassers will be prosecuted" but has not yet installed any cameras. What control type and category are the signs?
2. An organization runs a business-critical legacy application on an operating system the vendor no longer patches. The security team places the server on an isolated VLAN reachable only through a tightly restricted firewall. What control type is the segmentation, and why?
3. Classify each of the following by **category**: (a) a written password policy, (b) a badge reader on the server-room door, (c) an analyst reviewing SIEM logs each morning, (d) full-disk encryption on laptops.
4. After a ransomware infection is discovered, administrators wipe the affected hosts and restore data from last night's backups. Which control type does the restore represent, and which type discovered the infection if a SIEM alert first flagged it?
5. A security guard stands at the lobby entrance, checks badges before allowing entry, and reports anyone attempting to tailgate. Which control category does the guard belong to, and which control types is the guard providing in this scenario?

<details>
<summary>Answers</summary>

1. **Deterrent** (type), **physical** (category). The signs discourage intrusion by raising the perceived risk of being caught, but they cannot stop or record anything — with no cameras installed there is no detective function at all. This illustrates that deterrence works on the attacker's *decision*, not on the action itself.
2. **Compensating.** The primary control (patching the OS) is not feasible because the vendor no longer provides patches, so an alternative control — network isolation with strict firewall rules — is deployed to provide equivalent risk reduction. "Legacy/unpatchable system + alternative safeguard" is the signature compensating-control scenario. (The segmentation itself is enforced technically, but the *type* being tested is compensating.)
3. (a) **Managerial** — a policy document is governance, not enforcement. (b) **Physical** — it restricts physical entry to a room. (c) **Operational** — a person performing a day-to-day security procedure. (d) **Technical** — software/hardware enforcing protection automatically.
4. The backup restore is **corrective** — it repairs damage and returns systems to normal operation after the incident. The SIEM alert that flagged the infection is **detective** — it identified that the incident was occurring. Sequence on the exam timeline: detective controls find the event, corrective controls recover from it.
5. Category: **operational** (a person executing security procedures; guards can also be argued physical, but a guard actively performing badge checks is the textbook operational example). Types: **preventive** (physically denying entry to anyone without a valid badge), **deterrent** (visible presence discourages attempts), and **detective** (observing and reporting tailgating attempts). This is the classic example of a single control fulfilling multiple types simultaneously.
