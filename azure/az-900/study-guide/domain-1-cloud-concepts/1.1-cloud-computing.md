# Objective 1.1 — Describe cloud computing

This is the foundation objective of AZ-900: everything else in the exam (Azure services, architecture, cost management) assumes you can define what cloud computing *is* and who is responsible for *what*. Questions here are usually definitional ("Which cloud model dedicates infrastructure to a single organization?") or scenario-based ("A company wants to keep regulated data on-premises but burst its web tier to Azure — which model?"). The exam (passing score 700 on a 1–1000 scale) leans hard on three recurring patterns: the shared responsibility model chart (who patches the OS in IaaS vs. PaaS?), the public/private/hybrid decision, and CapEx-vs-OpEx economics. Learn each concept, its keyword triggers, and the one attribute that separates it from its neighbor (hybrid vs. multi-cloud, pay-as-you-go vs. reserved vs. spot, PaaS vs. serverless).

## Define cloud computing

- **What it is:** The **delivery of computing services over the internet** — compute (VMs, containers), storage, databases, networking, software, analytics, and AI — provided by someone else's datacenter and consumed on a **pay-as-you-go** basis.
- **Key traits:** No need to buy or maintain physical hardware; you rent exactly what you need, when you need it, and give it back when you're done. Capacity that once took weeks of procurement is provisioned in minutes.
- **Why it matters:** Cloud computing is an *operating model*, not a place — it trades large upfront hardware investments for flexible, metered consumption, and it makes capabilities like global scale, elasticity, and high availability accessible to any organization.
- **Common services delivered:** virtual machines, storage, databases, networking, and increasingly higher-level services (IoT, machine learning, serverless functions).
- **What cloud is NOT (frequent distractors):**
  - *Just virtualization* — running VMs in your own datacenter is virtualization, not cloud; you still bought and maintain the hardware.
  - *Colocation* — renting rack space for **your own** servers in someone else's building still leaves the hardware yours.
  - *Outsourcing staff* — a managed service provider running your on-prem servers changes who does the work, not the economic/delivery model.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The two phrases that define cloud computing on the exam: **"delivery of computing services over the internet"** and **"pay only for what you use."** If a question asks for the fundamental definition, look for the answer containing both ideas — not answers about specific vendors or virtualization alone.

## The shared responsibility model

- **What it is:** The division of security and management duties between the **cloud provider** and the **customer**. Which side owns a given layer depends on the service model (IaaS → PaaS → SaaS), but some responsibilities **never move**.
- **Always the customer's:** **information and data**, **devices** (mobile and PCs), and **accounts and identities**. No cloud model ever takes these off your plate — you classify your data, secure your endpoints, and manage who can sign in (in Azure, via **Microsoft Entra ID**, formerly Azure Active Directory).
- **Always the provider's:** the **physical hosts, physical network, and physical datacenter**. You will never badge into a Microsoft datacenter to swap a drive.
- **Shifts with the service model:** operating system, network controls, applications, and identity/directory infrastructure move from customer toward provider as you go IaaS → PaaS → SaaS.

Microsoft's shared responsibility chart, as the exam expects it:

| Responsibility | On-premises | IaaS | PaaS | SaaS |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Information and data | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** |
| Devices (mobile and PCs) | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** |
| Accounts and identities | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** | **Customer** |
| Identity and directory infrastructure | Customer | Customer | *Shared* | *Shared* |
| Applications | Customer | Customer | *Shared* | Provider |
| Network controls | Customer | Customer | *Shared* | Provider |
| Operating system | Customer | Customer | Provider | Provider |
| Physical hosts / network / datacenter | Customer | **Provider** | **Provider** | **Provider** |

- **Why it matters:** Real exam questions hide here. "A VM was compromised through a missing OS patch — whose fault?" In IaaS, **yours**. "Who ensures the physical server hosting your PaaS database has power and cooling?" **Microsoft's.**

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Memorize the three rows that never move in either direction: **data, devices, and accounts/identities are ALWAYS the customer's**; **physical hosts, network, and datacenter are ALWAYS the provider's**. Any answer choice that hands one of those to the other party is wrong — no matter how the scenario is dressed up.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Don't assume SaaS means "the provider handles everything." Even in SaaS, the customer **always** retains responsibility for data, devices, and accounts/identities — enabling MFA, granting access rights, and classifying data are on you, in every model.

## Cloud models: public, private, and hybrid

- **Public cloud:** Services offered over the public internet on infrastructure **owned and operated by a third-party provider** (e.g., Microsoft Azure) and **shared by many organizations** (multitenant). No capital expenditure to scale; resources available on demand; least direct control over the underlying hardware.
- **Private cloud:** Cloud infrastructure used **exclusively by a single organization**. It can run in your own datacenter **or be hosted by a third party** — what makes it "private" is dedicated single-organization use, not physical location. Maximum control and customization; you carry the cost and maintenance burden; self-service and scale are limited by what you bought.
- **Hybrid cloud:** An environment that **connects and integrates** a private cloud (or on-premises datacenter) with a public cloud, letting workloads and data move between them. You choose where each workload runs — keep sensitive or legacy systems private, use public cloud for burst capacity, new apps, or disaster recovery.
- **Multi-cloud (related concept):** Using **two or more public cloud providers** (e.g., Azure *and* AWS) — whether to avoid lock-in, use best-of-breed features, or because of mergers. You manage resources and security in multiple environments.
- **Azure Arc:** Microsoft's tool for **managing resources across all of these environments** — Azure, on-premises, hybrid, and even other clouds — from a single Azure control plane.
- **Azure VMware Solution:** Lets an organization **run its existing VMware workloads in Azure** with seamless integration — the fast path for hybrid deployments and migrations when you're already invested in VMware.

| | Public cloud | Private cloud | Hybrid cloud |
|---|---|---|---|
| Who uses it | Many organizations (multitenant) | One organization exclusively | One organization, spanning both |
| Where it runs | Provider's datacenters | On-prem **or** third-party hosted | Both private and public |
| Upfront cost | None (OpEx) | High (you buy/maintain hardware) | Mixed |
| Control | Least over hardware | Maximum | You choose per workload |
| Scale | Near-unlimited, on demand | Limited to owned capacity | Burst into public as needed |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "Private cloud" does **not** mean "in your building," and "hosted by a third party" does **not** automatically mean public. A third party can host a private cloud dedicated entirely to you. Tenancy — who shares it — defines the model, not geography. Also don't confuse **hybrid** (private + public, integrated) with **multi-cloud** (multiple *public* providers).

## Use cases for each cloud model

- **Public cloud fits:** startups and new projects with no hardware budget, unpredictable or fast-growing demand, workloads needing global reach, anyone who wants zero hardware maintenance and pure pay-as-you-go economics.
- **Private cloud fits:** organizations with **strict regulatory/compliance or data-sovereignty requirements**, need for complete control over hardware and security configuration, or legacy applications that can't move — accepting higher cost and their own maintenance responsibility.
- **Hybrid cloud fits:** phased cloud migrations, keeping regulated data on-premises while running the web front end in Azure, **cloud bursting** (overflowing into public capacity during demand spikes), using public cloud for backup/disaster recovery of on-prem systems, and extending the life of existing datacenter investments.
- **Multi-cloud fits:** avoiding dependence on a single vendor, regulatory diversification, or organizations inheriting different providers through acquisitions — managed centrally with **Azure Arc**.

| Scenario in the question | Best-fit model |
|---|---|
| Startup, zero hardware budget, unpredictable growth | Public |
| Regulator requires dedicated infrastructure and full control | Private |
| Regulated data stays local; customer-facing web tier scales in Azure | Hybrid |
| Normal load on-prem, holiday spikes overflow to Azure (cloud bursting) | Hybrid |
| Company acquired a business running on AWS; both clouds stay | Multi-cloud |
| One dashboard to govern servers on-prem, in Azure, and in AWS | Azure Arc |
| Lift a VMware estate into Azure without re-architecting | Azure VMware Solution |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Scenario keywords map cleanly: "no upfront investment / pay only for use" = public; "complete control / dedicated hardware / strict compliance" = private; "keep some workloads on-premises AND use Azure" = hybrid; "Azure **and** another public provider" = multi-cloud; "manage servers everywhere from one place" = Azure Arc; "move VMware workloads to Azure without re-architecting" = Azure VMware Solution.

## The consumption-based model

- **What it is:** You are charged **only for the resources you actually use** — no upfront purchase, no need to buy and manage infrastructure that might sit idle, and you **stop paying when you stop using**. Usage is metered (per second of compute, per GB stored, per execution).
- **CapEx vs. OpEx:** Traditional IT is **capital expenditure (CapEx)** — big upfront spending on servers and datacenters that depreciates over years, sized by *guessing* future demand. Cloud is **operational expenditure (OpEx)** — an ongoing metered cost that tracks *actual* demand.
- **Why it matters:** No wasted capacity (underprovisioning *and* overprovisioning both disappear), better cost prediction per unit of work, and the ability to try things cheaply and shut them down for free.
- **Tracking it:** In Azure, **Microsoft Cost Management** provides the tools to monitor, analyze, alert on, and optimize this metered spending.

| | CapEx (traditional) | OpEx (consumption-based cloud) |
|---|---|---|
| When you pay | Large payment **up front** | As you go, based on metered usage |
| What you pay for | Capacity you *predict* you'll need | Resources you *actually* use |
| Idle resources | Still paid for (sunk cost) | Deallocate them — billing stops |
| Demand mismatch risk | Over- or under-provisioning | Scale with demand; pay accordingly |
| Accounting treatment | Asset, depreciated over time | Ongoing operating expense |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "No upfront costs, pay only for what you use, stop paying when you stop using" = **consumption-based model**, and it is the textbook example of shifting from **CapEx to OpEx**. If a question stresses "avoid buying hardware that may go unused," this is the answer.

## Comparing cloud pricing models

Azure offers several ways to pay for the same resources — the exam tests when each makes sense:

| | Pay-as-you-go | Reserved instances | Spot pricing |
|---|---|---|---|
| Commitment | None — start/stop anytime | **1- or 3-year** commitment | None |
| Price | Standard metered rate | **Significant discount** (up to ~72%) vs. pay-as-you-go | Deepest discount (up to ~90%) |
| Catch | Most expensive per hour long-term | You pay for the commitment even if usage drops | Workload can be **evicted** when Azure needs the capacity back |
| Best for | Variable, unpredictable, or short-lived workloads | **Steady, predictable** workloads running long-term (e.g., an always-on production database) | **Interruptible** workloads — batch jobs, rendering, dev/test — that can tolerate being stopped |

Real environments **mix** these: reserve the steady baseline, run the variable layer pay-as-you-go, and push interruptible batch work to spot. The exam decides between them on two axes — *can you commit?* (reserved) and *can you be interrupted?* (spot); if neither, pay-as-you-go.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Match the stem's stress word: "**predictable / long-term / committed**" → reserved instances; "**can be interrupted / restarted / batch**" → spot; "**unpredictable / short-term / no commitment**" → pay-as-you-go.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Spot pricing is the cheapest but **never** appropriate for workloads that must not be interrupted — Azure can reclaim (evict) spot capacity with little notice. "Batch processing that can be restarted" = spot; "production system running 24/7 for the next three years" = reserved instances; "unpredictable startup traffic" = pay-as-you-go.

## Serverless

- **What it is:** A cloud execution model in which the **provider handles all the infrastructure** — servers, OS, runtime, scaling, capacity planning are fully abstracted away. You supply **code (or workflow logic)** that runs **on demand in response to events**.
- **How billing works:** You are charged **per execution/consumption** — and when nothing runs, serverless **scales to zero** and costs nothing. This is the consumption-based model at its purest.
- **Azure examples:** **Azure Functions** (event-driven code) and **Azure Logic Apps** (low/no-code automated workflows).
- **Serverless vs. PaaS:** Both hide the OS, but PaaS typically keeps an app *running* on provisioned capacity you pay for continuously; serverless spins resources up per event and disappears between them.
- **Why "serverless" if there are servers?** There absolutely are servers — you just never see, provision, patch, or manage them. The name describes *your* experience, not physics.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Keyword triggers for serverless: "run code **only when an event occurs**," "no infrastructure to manage at all," "billed per execution," "scales automatically **including down to zero**." Named services: Azure Functions (code), Logic Apps (workflows).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "Serverless" does not mean servers are absent, and it is not the same as PaaS. If the workload runs **continuously** on capacity you provisioned, it's PaaS/IaaS; if it **appears per event and costs nothing while idle**, it's serverless.

## How It Fits Together (mental model)

Picture one company's journey through every bullet of this objective: it decides to consume **cloud computing** — compute, storage, and networking delivered over the internet instead of from its own racks — moving spending from **CapEx to OpEx** under the **consumption-based model** (metered usage, no upfront cost, watched in **Microsoft Cost Management**). Regulated customer records stay in its **private cloud**, the web tier runs in Azure's **public cloud**, and the two are integrated into a **hybrid** design (with **Azure VMware Solution** carrying over its VMware estate and **Azure Arc** managing servers in both worlds — plus a few AWS resources, making it technically **multi-cloud**). For each Azure workload it picks a **pricing model**: the always-on database on **3-year reserved instances**, the unpredictable web tier on **pay-as-you-go**, nightly batch analytics on evictable **spot** VMs, and the order-notification code as **serverless** Azure Functions that scale to zero between orders. Across all of it, the **shared responsibility model** governs: Microsoft runs the physical datacenters, responsibility for the OS and applications shifts with each service model, and the company always owns its **data, devices, and identities** in Microsoft Entra ID.

## Quick Contrast Drills

Four pairings the exam loves to blur — rehearse the one-line distinction:

1. **Hybrid vs. multi-cloud:** hybrid integrates **private + public**; multi-cloud uses **two or more public providers**. A company on Azure and AWS with no on-prem footprint is multi-cloud, not hybrid.
2. **CapEx vs. OpEx:** CapEx is **upfront payment for predicted capacity** (own the asset); OpEx is **ongoing payment for actual usage** (rent the service). The consumption-based model is OpEx.
3. **Reserved vs. spot:** both are discounts, but reserved trades a **1/3-year commitment** for savings on *steady* workloads, while spot trades **evictability** for the deepest savings on *interruptible* workloads.
4. **PaaS vs. serverless:** both hide the OS, but PaaS runs on **continuously provisioned capacity you pay for**, while serverless is **event-driven, scales to zero, and bills per execution**.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Cloud computing = computing services (compute, storage, networking, and more) delivered over the internet on a pay-as-you-go basis — an operating model, not a location.
- [ ] Shared responsibility: the customer **always** owns information/data, devices, and accounts/identities; the provider **always** owns physical hosts, network, and datacenter; OS, network controls, applications, and identity infrastructure shift with IaaS → PaaS → SaaS.
- [ ] In IaaS the customer patches the OS; in PaaS and SaaS the provider does. Memorize the chart.
- [ ] Public = multitenant provider infrastructure; private = single organization (any location); hybrid = integrated private + public; multi-cloud = multiple public providers.
- [ ] Use cases: public for no-upfront-cost scale; private for control/compliance; hybrid for phased migration, cloud bursting, and keeping regulated data local; Azure Arc manages across all environments; Azure VMware Solution moves VMware estates into Azure.
- [ ] Consumption-based model = OpEx, not CapEx: no upfront cost, pay only for what you use, stop paying when you stop; tracked with Microsoft Cost Management.
- [ ] Pricing models: pay-as-you-go (flexible, priciest), reserved instances (1/3-year commitment, big discount, steady workloads), spot (cheapest, evictable, interruptible workloads).
- [ ] Serverless (Azure Functions, Logic Apps): provider manages all infrastructure, code runs on demand, scales to zero, billed per execution.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A company's Azure IaaS virtual machine is breached through an operating-system vulnerability that had a patch available for months. Under the shared responsibility model, who was responsible for applying that patch — and would the answer change for a PaaS database?
2. A regional bank must keep customer data on hardware dedicated solely to it for compliance, but wants a third party to host and run that hardware. Which cloud model is this, and why?
3. A media company runs steady production servers 24/7, plus overnight video-encoding jobs that can be safely restarted if interrupted. Which Azure pricing model fits each workload, and why?
4. A startup wants order-confirmation code to run only when a customer places an order, pay nothing during idle periods, and never manage servers. Which cloud approach and which two Azure services fit?
5. An enterprise runs workloads in its own datacenter, in Azure, and in AWS, and wants to manage and govern all of them from a single control plane. Which terms describe this environment, and which Azure service enables the management?

<summary>Answers</summary>

1. The **customer** — in IaaS, everything from the operating system up (patching, middleware, apps, data) is the customer's responsibility. For a **PaaS** database, the **provider** manages and patches the OS and runtime, though the customer still owns the data and access to it.
2. A **private cloud** — the defining trait is exclusive use by a single organization, not physical location. A third party can host and operate infrastructure that remains dedicated to one customer, and it is still private.
3. The steady 24/7 servers fit **reserved instances** — a 1- or 3-year commitment earns a significant discount for predictable long-running workloads. 4. **Serverless** — the provider manages all infrastructure, the code runs on demand per event, scales to zero when idle, and is billed per execution. The Azure services are **Azure Functions** (event-driven code) and **Azure Logic Apps** (automated workflows).
5. On-prem + Azure is **hybrid cloud**; adding AWS makes it **multi-cloud**. **Azure Arc** extends the Azure control plane to manage resources across on-premises, Azure, and other public clouds from one place.
