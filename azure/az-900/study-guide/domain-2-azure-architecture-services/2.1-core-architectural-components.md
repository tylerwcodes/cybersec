# Objective 2.1 — Describe the core architectural components of Azure

This objective is the backbone of Domain 2 and one of the most heavily tested areas on AZ-900. The exam wants you to understand Azure's physical layer (datacenters, availability zones, regions, region pairs, sovereign regions) and its logical/organizational layer (resources, resource groups, subscriptions, management groups) — and, crucially, how the two layers relate. Questions are usually definitional ("What is the minimum number of availability zones in a zone-enabled region?") or scenario-based ("A company must keep data in Germany and needs policies applied to 40 subscriptions at once — what should it use?"). Learn each component's one-line definition, its position in the hierarchy, and the failure or governance problem it solves: zones protect against **datacenter** failure, region pairs protect against **regional** disaster, resource groups organize by **lifecycle**, subscriptions draw **billing and access boundaries**, and management groups apply **governance at scale**.

## Azure datacenters

- **What it is:** The **physical buildings** — facilities filled with racks of servers, storage, and networking gear, each with its own power, cooling, and network infrastructure. They are the lowest physical layer of Azure.
- **Why it matters:** Everything in Azure ultimately runs in a datacenter, but datacenters are **not directly exposed to customers**. You never choose "Datacenter 3 in Virginia" — you deploy to a **region** (and optionally an availability zone), and Azure places your workload in a datacenter for you.
- **Grouping:** Datacenters are grouped into **availability zones**, and zones/datacenters are grouped into **regions**.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** If an answer choice says "deploy the VM to a specific Azure datacenter," it's wrong. The datacenter is an abstraction below the customer-facing surface — the smallest placement decisions you make are region and (for supported services) availability zone.

## Azure regions

- **What it is:** A **geographical area containing one or more datacenters** that are nearby and networked together within a **latency-defined perimeter** through a dedicated, low-latency network. Examples: East US, West Europe, Japan East.
- **Why it matters:** The region is your **primary deployment decision**. Nearly every resource you create asks "which region?" first.
- **How to choose a region:**
  - **Proximity to users** — closer region = lower latency.
  - **Data residency and compliance** — some laws require data to stay within a country/geography (e.g., GDPR-driven residency in Europe).
  - **Service availability** — not every service or VM size is available in every region; new services often roll out to select regions first.
  - **Pricing** — costs vary between regions.
- **Geographies:** Regions are organized into **geographies** (e.g., United States, Europe, Asia Pacific) — discrete markets that preserve **data residency and compliance boundaries**. Data replicated for resiliency typically stays within its geography.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Customers in Australia complain about latency" or "data must remain within the country's borders" = choose an appropriate **region**. Region selection is the answer to both performance-proximity and data-residency scenarios.

## Region pairs

- **What it is:** Most Azure regions are **paired with another region within the same geography**, typically **at least 300 miles apart** (e.g., East US ↔ West US, North Europe ↔ West Europe).
- **Why it matters — the three classic benefits:**
  - **Sequential updates:** Planned Azure platform updates are rolled out to paired regions one at a time, so both members of a pair are never taken down by the same update.
  - **Prioritized recovery:** In a broad outage affecting multiple regions, one region out of every pair is **prioritized for restoration**.
  - **Replication target:** Geo-redundant storage (GRS) automatically replicates data to the paired region, giving you a recovery site hundreds of miles away.
- **Caveats:** Some services are automatically region-pair-aware (like GRS); others require you to configure cross-region replication yourself. A **few regions have no pair** (some newer regions instead rely on availability zones plus customer-configured replication).

| | Availability zones | Region pairs |
|---|---|---|
| Protects against | **Datacenter-level failure** (power, cooling, network in one facility) | **Region-wide disaster** (hurricane, earthquake, flood) |
| Distance | Separate facilities **within one region** | **≥300 miles apart**, same geography |
| Latency between members | Very low (synchronous replication feasible) | Higher (typically asynchronous replication) |
| Example use | Zone-redundant VM set, ZRS storage | GRS storage, cross-region disaster recovery |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Survive the loss of an entire region" = **region pair / geo-redundancy**. "Survive the loss of a datacenter but stay in the same region" = **availability zones**. The distance and the scope of disaster are your keywords.

## Sovereign regions

- **What it is:** **Physically and logically isolated instances of Azure** — separate clouds for compliance or legal reasons, not reachable from the global (public) Azure cloud.
- **The two to know:**
  - **Azure Government (US):** dedicated regions for US federal, state, and local government agencies and their partners; operated by **screened US persons**; meets requirements like FedRAMP High and DoD impact levels.
  - **Azure China:** operated not by Microsoft directly but by **21Vianet**, a local partner, to comply with Chinese regulations. Microsoft does not directly maintain the datacenters.
- **Why it matters:** A sovereign region has its **own portal endpoints, its own compliance certifications, and no connectivity to global Azure**. A US defense contractor or a company operating in mainland China can't just pick a normal region — they need the sovereign cloud.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Sovereign regions are not just "regions with extra compliance settings" — they are **separate, isolated cloud instances**. And remember the operator detail the exam loves: **Azure China is operated by 21Vianet**, not Microsoft.

## Availability zones

- **What it is:** **Physically separate datacenters within a single Azure region.** Each zone is made up of one or more datacenters with **independent power, cooling, and networking**, connected to the other zones by a private high-speed fiber network.
- **The magic number:** Regions that support availability zones have a **minimum of three** zones.
- **Why it matters:** A zone is an **isolation boundary** — if one zone (datacenter) fails from a power or cooling event, the other zones keep running, and low inter-zone latency allows **synchronous replication** between them.
- **How services use zones:**
  - **Zonal services** — you pin a resource to a *specific* zone (e.g., a VM, a managed disk, a standard public IP). For resilience, you deploy duplicates into other zones yourself.
  - **Zone-redundant services** — the platform **automatically replicates across zones** (e.g., zone-redundant storage/ZRS, SQL Database). A zone failure is transparent to you.
  - (Some global/non-regional services — like Microsoft Entra ID or Azure DNS — are always-available and not tied to a region at all.)
- **Limits:** Zones protect against datacenter failure, **not** against a disaster large enough to take out the whole region — that's what region pairs and geo-redundancy are for. Also, **not every region supports availability zones**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Independent power, cooling, and networking" is the fingerprint phrase for **availability zones**, and "minimum of three" is a favorite factoid. "Zonal = you pick the zone; zone-redundant = Azure spreads it across zones automatically."

## Azure resources and resource groups

- **Resource — what it is:** **Any manageable item in Azure** — a virtual machine, a disk, a virtual network, a storage account, a database, a web app. If you can create, update, or delete it through Azure, it's a resource.
- **Resource group — what it is:** A **logical container for resources**. Every resource must live in exactly one resource group; groups exist purely for organization, lifecycle management, and access control — they are **not** a physical location.
- **The rules the exam tests:**
  - A resource belongs to **exactly ONE resource group** at a time (it can be *moved* to another group, but never in two at once).
  - A resource group **can contain resources from different regions** — the group itself has a location (where its metadata is stored), but its members don't have to match it.
  - **Deleting a resource group deletes everything inside it** — powerful for tearing down environments, dangerous if you forget what's in there.
  - Resource groups **cannot be nested** — no groups inside groups.
  - Applying **RBAC roles or policies to a group** applies them to every resource in the group.
- **Best practice:** Group resources that **share a lifecycle** — deployed, updated, and deleted together (e.g., everything belonging to one application's test environment) — so a single delete cleanly retires the whole set.

| | Resource group | Management group |
|---|---|---|
| Contains | **Resources** (VMs, VNets, disks...) | **Subscriptions** (and other management groups) |
| Nesting | **Not allowed** | Up to **six levels** deep |
| Membership rule | Resource in exactly one group | Subscription/group has exactly one parent |
| Purpose | Lifecycle + organization of resources | Governance at scale across subscriptions |

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Two traps in one sentence: resource groups **cannot be nested**, and a resource **cannot be in two resource groups simultaneously**. Also remember a resource group is a *logical* container — putting a VM in a group named "WestEurope-RG" does not place the VM in West Europe; the VM's own region setting does that.

## Subscriptions

- **What it is:** A **unit of billing and a boundary for access control and scale**, linked to a **Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure Active Directory) tenant** for authentication. Every resource group (and therefore every resource) belongs to exactly one subscription.
- **The two boundary roles:**
  - **Billing boundary:** each subscription generates its own invoice/billing reports — the natural way to separate costs by department, project, or environment.
  - **Access-control boundary:** policies and role assignments applied at subscription scope govern everything inside it, so separate subscriptions cleanly separate environments (e.g., a locked-down production subscription vs. a permissive dev subscription).
- **Why organizations use multiple subscriptions:** separate **environments** (dev/test/prod), separate **departments or teams** (IT, HR, Marketing), separate **billing** (chargeback per cost center), or to work within per-subscription **quotas/limits**.
- **Relationship to the tenant:** One Entra tenant can trust **many subscriptions**, but each subscription trusts exactly **one tenant**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The scenario "each department must receive its own invoice" or "development and production must be strictly separated for billing and access" = **create separate subscriptions**. A resource group can't do this — it has no billing boundary of its own.

## Management groups

- **What it is:** A container **above subscriptions** used to organize subscriptions into a hierarchy for **unified policy and access management**. Apply Azure Policy or an RBAC role once at the management-group level and it is **inherited by every subscription (and every resource) underneath**.
- **The facts the exam checks:**
  - A single directory supports up to **10,000 management groups**.
  - The tree supports up to **six levels of depth** (not counting the root management group or the subscription level).
  - Each management group and subscription has **exactly one parent**; a management group can have many children.
  - Every directory has a single top-level **root management group**, under which all other management groups and subscriptions ultimately sit.
- **Classic use case:** Put all production subscriptions under a "Production" management group and assign a policy such as "limit deployments to specific regions" or "require MFA" once — every current *and future* subscription placed in that group inherits it automatically.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Management groups contain **subscriptions and other management groups — never resources or resource groups directly**. If the question asks how to apply a policy across *many subscriptions* with one assignment, the answer is a management group, not a resource group.

## The hierarchy: management groups → subscriptions → resource groups → resources

Azure's organizational structure is a strict four-level tree, and **governance flows downhill**:

1. **Management groups** — organize subscriptions; policies/RBAC assigned here are inherited by everything below.
2. **Subscriptions** — billing and access boundaries; inherit from their management group; contain resource groups.
3. **Resource groups** — logical lifecycle containers; inherit from their subscription; contain resources.
4. **Resources** — the actual services (VMs, VNets, databases); inherit everything assigned above them.

Assignments made at a higher scope **cascade down** to every child scope and cannot be simply "opted out of" at a lower level — which is exactly why the hierarchy is the mechanism for governance at scale. The general rule: assign broad guardrails high (management group), grant working access low (resource group), and use subscriptions as the clean billing/environment seams in the middle.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Memorize the top-down order cold: **Management group → Subscription → Resource group → Resource.** Ordering questions and "which scope should the policy be assigned to?" questions both come straight from this list.

## How It Fits Together (mental model)

Picture a multinational company building in Azure. The physical foundation is Microsoft's **datacenters** — buildings the company never touches directly. Those datacenters are grouped into **availability zones** (minimum three per zone-enabled region, each with independent power, cooling, and networking), and zones make up a **region** like West Europe, chosen for user proximity and EU data residency. West Europe's **region pair**, North Europe, sits hundreds of miles away receiving GRS replicas and staggered platform updates in case a regional disaster strikes; the company's US-government subsidiary, meanwhile, must use the isolated **sovereign** Azure Government cloud. On the logical side, every VM, disk, and VNet the company creates is a **resource**, organized into **resource groups** by shared lifecycle so a test environment can be deleted in one shot. Resource groups live inside **subscriptions** — one per department and environment for clean invoices and access boundaries, all trusting the company's Microsoft Entra ID tenant. The subscriptions are arranged under **management groups** ("Prod," "Non-Prod") so a single policy assignment cascades down the **hierarchy** — management group to subscription to resource group to resource — governing everything with one stroke.

## Quick Contrast Drills

Four pairings the exam loves to blur — rehearse the one-line distinction:

1. **Availability zones vs. region pairs:** both add resiliency, but zones are separate datacenters **inside one region** (protect against datacenter failure, synchronous replication), while region pairs are **two regions ≥300 miles apart** (protect against regional disaster, geo-replication).
2. **Resource group vs. management group:** both are containers, but a resource group holds **resources** and cannot be nested, while a management group holds **subscriptions** (and other management groups) and nests up to six levels for policy inheritance.
3. **Subscription vs. resource group:** the subscription is a **billing and access boundary** tied to an Entra tenant; the resource group is a **logical lifecycle container** with no billing identity of its own. "Separate invoices" always means separate subscriptions.
4. **Region vs. datacenter:** you deploy to a **region** (a customer-facing choice); the **datacenter** is the physical building Azure selects for you — never a deployment target on the exam.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Datacenters are the physical buildings; customers deploy to regions and zones, never to a datacenter directly.
- [ ] A region = one or more datacenters within a latency-defined perimeter on a low-latency network; choose regions for user proximity, data residency/compliance, service availability, and price.
- [ ] Most regions have a pair ≥300 miles away in the same geography — benefits: sequential platform updates, prioritized recovery, and automatic GRS replication target; a few regions have no pair.
- [ ] Sovereign regions (Azure Government for US government; Azure China operated by 21Vianet) are physically and logically **isolated** cloud instances, separate from global Azure.
- [ ] Availability zones = physically separate datacenters within a region, each with independent power, cooling, and networking; **minimum three** in supporting regions; zonal (you pick) vs. zone-redundant (Azure replicates) deployments.
- [ ] Zones defend against datacenter failure; region pairs/geo-redundancy defend against regional disaster.
- [ ] A resource is any manageable Azure item; a resource group is its logical container — one group per resource, mixed regions allowed, no nesting, delete-the-group-deletes-the-contents, group by shared lifecycle.
- [ ] A subscription is both a billing boundary and an access-control boundary, linked to a Microsoft Entra ID (formerly Azure Active Directory) tenant; organizations use multiple subscriptions to separate environments, departments, and invoices.
- [ ] Management groups organize subscriptions for governance at scale: up to 10,000 per directory, six levels of nesting depth (excluding root and subscription), one parent each; policy and RBAC assigned there are inherited downward.
- [ ] The hierarchy — management group → subscription → resource group → resource — with policy and RBAC inheritance flowing top-down.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A financial services firm must guarantee its customer data never leaves Germany, and its application must survive the failure of a single datacenter without failing over to another country. What two architectural choices satisfy both requirements?
2. A platform update from Microsoft is being rolled out. Why can an organization using East US and its pair West US be confident both regions won't be updated simultaneously?
3. An administrator deletes the resource group "rg-test-app" containing a VM in East US and a storage account in West Europe. What happens, and what does this reveal about resource groups and regions?
4. Contoso has 45 subscriptions and must enforce a policy restricting all deployments to European regions, including for subscriptions created next year. What should Contoso use, and why not assign the policy per subscription?
5. Arrange these from the top of the Azure hierarchy to the bottom, and state which direction policy inheritance flows: resource, subscription, management group, resource group.

Choose a **German region** (satisfies data residency/compliance) and deploy the application across **availability zones** within that region — physically separate datacenters with independent power, cooling, and networking, so a single datacenter failure is survived without data ever leaving the country.
2. Because they are a **region pair**: Azure rolls out planned platform updates to paired regions **sequentially, not at the same time**, so at least one member of the pair remains unaffected. Pairs also get prioritized recovery in broad outages and serve as GRS replication targets.
3. **Both resources are deleted** — deleting a resource group deletes everything it contains. It also shows that a resource group is a **logical** container that can hold resources from **different regions**; the group is about lifecycle and organization, not physical location.

Inheritance pushes it to every subscription below — including **future subscriptions** added to the group — whereas per-subscription assignment means 45 separate assignments plus manual work for each new subscription.
