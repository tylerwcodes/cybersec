# Objective 3.2 — Describe features and tools in Azure for governance and compliance

Governance is how an organization keeps its cloud estate compliant, consistent, and safe from accidents — without slowing teams down. This objective has only three official bullets, but they appear constantly on the exam because each maps to a crisp scenario: "we need to find and classify sensitive data everywhere" (Microsoft Purview), "we need to enforce rules about what resources can be created and how" (Azure Policy), and "we need to make sure nobody accidentally deletes this" (resource locks). Questions are almost always scenario-based, and the favorite trap is blurring these tools with role-based access control (RBAC): remember that RBAC governs **who can do what**, while the tools in this objective govern **what resources and data may look like and whether they can be changed at all**. Learn each tool's one-line purpose, its scopes and inheritance behavior, and the keywords that select it.

## Microsoft Purview

- **What it is:** A **unified data-governance and compliance** family of solutions that helps you **discover, classify, and track sensitive data across your entire data estate** — Azure services, on-premises systems, Microsoft 365, and even other clouds like AWS and Google Cloud.
- **What it produces:** an up-to-date **map of your data estate** — a single pane showing what data exists, where it lives, how sensitive it is, and how it flows between systems.
- **Why it matters:** You cannot protect, govern, or stay compliant with data you don't know you have. Purview answers "*what* data do we have, *where* is it, *how sensitive* is it, and *where did it come from*?" — the prerequisite for meeting regulations such as GDPR.

### Three capabilities to memorize

- **Automated data discovery:** Purview scans registered data sources (databases, data lakes, file shares, SaaS apps) on a schedule and catalogs what it finds — no manual inventorying.
- **Sensitive-data classification:** built-in and custom classifiers label discovered data (credit card numbers, national ID numbers, health records) so you know exactly where regulated data resides.
- **End-to-end data lineage:** Purview traces where data originated and how it moves and transforms across systems — from source database through a pipeline into a report — so auditors can follow the trail.

### Two solution areas

| | Risk & compliance solutions | Unified data governance |
|---|---|---|
| Focus | Protect sensitive data, meet legal/regulatory requirements | Map and manage data across the whole estate |
| Anchored in | Microsoft 365 data (email, Teams, SharePoint) | Azure, on-premises, other clouds, SaaS |
| Example task | Identify data covered by GDPR and respond to data-privacy regulations | Automated discovery, classification, and lineage for a data lake |

### Exam-style scenarios that map to Purview

- "We must locate every place **credit-card numbers** are stored across Azure and our on-premises file servers" → Purview **classification**.
- "Auditors need to see how figures in a report **trace back to the source database**" → Purview **lineage**.
- "We need one **catalog of all data assets** across Azure, AWS, and Microsoft 365" → Purview **discovery / data map**.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** The Purview trigger words are **data**-centric: "discover and **classify sensitive data**," "**data lineage**," "map the **data estate**," "**on-premises, multicloud, and SaaS** data." If the scenario is about governing *data* (not resources), the answer is **Microsoft Purview**.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Purview is **not** limited to Azure. Its whole point is spanning **Azure + on-premises + Microsoft 365 + other clouds**. A distractor claiming it "only classifies data stored in Azure" is wrong. Also don't confuse it with Azure Policy — Purview governs **data**, Policy governs **resources**.

## Azure Policy

- **What it is:** A service for **creating, assigning, and managing rules (policies) that enforce standards on Azure resources** — allowed regions, required tags, permitted VM sizes, mandatory encryption settings, and so on.
- **Why it matters:** It turns organizational standards ("all resources in East US," "every resource carries a costCenter tag") from written guidelines into automatically enforced guardrails that apply to *everyone*, including administrators.

### Policies and initiatives

- **Policy (definition):** one rule — e.g., "allowed locations = East US" or "resources must have a costCenter tag."
- **Initiative:** a **group of related policies** defined, assigned, and tracked for compliance **as a single unit** — e.g., 20 policies that together implement a regulatory standard. Assign the initiative once instead of twenty policies separately.
- **Assignment:** you assign a policy or initiative at a **scope** — a **management group, subscription, or resource group** — and the assignment **inherits downward** to everything beneath it.

| Assignment scope | The rule then covers... |
|---|---|
| Management group | Every subscription, resource group, and resource beneath it |
| Subscription | Every resource group and resource in that subscription |
| Resource group | Every resource in that group |

### What Policy does with violations

Azure Policy continuously **evaluates resources for compliance** and **highlights non-compliant resources** in a compliance dashboard. Depending on the policy's effect it can also act:

| Effect | What happens |
|---|---|
| **Deny** | Non-compliant resource creation/update is **blocked** — it never gets created |
| **Audit** | Resource is allowed but **flagged as non-compliant** for reporting |
| **Append / Modify** | Azure **automatically remediates** — e.g., adds the missing required tag |

- **Existing resources:** resources that already existed before the policy (or that slipped through under audit) are **marked non-compliant** on the dashboard — Policy does **not** delete them.

### Azure Policy vs. RBAC — the exam's favorite contrast

| | Azure Policy | Role-based access control (RBAC) |
|---|---|---|
| Governs | **WHAT** resources can be created / how they must be configured | **WHO** can perform which actions on resources |
| Example | "VMs may only be created in East US" | "Alice may create VMs in this resource group" |
| Violation handling | Deny, audit, or remediate the **resource** | Deny the **user's action** for lack of permission |
| A user with full permissions | Is **still blocked** by a deny policy | Is limited only by role assignments |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Classic stems: "ensure all resources are created **only in the East US region**" and "**require a costCenter tag** on every new resource" — both are **Azure Policy**. If the requirement applies to *every* resource regardless of *who* creates it, it's Policy, not RBAC.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** An **initiative** is a **group of related policies** managed as one unit — it is not a special policy type, a monitoring tool, or a management group. And remember what Policy does to *existing* violators: it **flags** (or remediates) them; it does **not** delete resources that already exist.

## Resource locks

- **What it is:** A safeguard you place on a **subscription, resource group, or individual resource** to **prevent accidental deletion or modification** of critical assets — a production database, an ExpressRoute circuit, a hub virtual network.
- **Inheritance:** locks applied at a higher scope are **inherited** by everything within it — lock a resource group and every resource inside is locked too.
- **Why it matters:** RBAC alone can't stop an authorized administrator from making an honest mistake. A lock forces a deliberate pause before destructive actions.

### The two lock types

| | **CanNotDelete** (Delete lock) | **ReadOnly** lock |
|---|---|---|
| Read the resource | ✅ Allowed | ✅ Allowed |
| Modify the resource | ✅ Allowed | ❌ Blocked |
| Delete the resource | ❌ Blocked | ❌ Blocked |
| Analogy | "Change it if you must, but don't delete it" | "Look, but don't touch" |

### Where locks are typically used

- **Production databases and storage accounts** holding irreplaceable data — **CanNotDelete** (admins can still tune settings).
- **Networking backbone** (hub VNet, VPN/ExpressRoute gateways) whose configuration must not drift — **ReadOnly**.
- **An entire resource group** containing a critical workload — one lock at the group, inherited by every resource inside.

### Locks override RBAC

- A lock applies **regardless of the user's permissions** — even an **Owner** cannot delete a locked resource.
- To make the blocked change, someone with authority must **remove (or modify) the lock first**, then perform the operation, and ideally re-apply the lock afterward. The two-step dance is the feature, not a bug — it makes destruction deliberate.
- **What locks are NOT for:** they protect against **accidental** deletion or change, **not malicious actors** — anyone with permission to manage locks can simply remove one. Malicious-insider scenarios call for RBAC, auditing, and identity controls (Microsoft Entra ID, formerly Azure Active Directory).

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "An administrator with the **Owner** role tries to delete a resource and is blocked" = a **resource lock** is in place, and the fix is to **remove the lock first**. If the resource must still be *updated* but never *deleted*, choose **CanNotDelete**; if it must not change at all, choose **ReadOnly**.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** A **ReadOnly** lock can break operations that look like reads but are actually management calls (e.g., listing storage account keys is a POST action and gets blocked). More broadly: don't pick locks for "prevent **unauthorized** users from deleting" — that's RBAC. Locks stop **authorized** users from making **accidental** mistakes.

## How It Fits Together (mental model)

Picture a company landing in Azure with guidance from the **Cloud Adoption Framework** (Microsoft's collected best practices and documentation for the cloud journey — know the name, but it's supporting context here, not its own bullet). Governance starts with knowing the data: **Microsoft Purview** scans Azure, on-premises servers, Microsoft 365, and a legacy AWS account, **discovering** sources, **classifying** the customer records it finds as sensitive, and drawing **lineage** so auditors can see how data flows into reports. With the data estate mapped, the platform team assigns an **Azure Policy initiative** at the management group — one policy **denies** any resource outside East US, another **audits** VMs without disk encryption, and a third **appends** the mandatory costCenter tag — and the assignment **inherits down** to every subscription and resource group, with the compliance dashboard highlighting stragglers. Finally, the crown jewels — the production SQL database and the hub network — get **resource locks**: **CanNotDelete** on the database (DBAs still tune it; nobody deletes it) and **ReadOnly** on the network resource group. When an Owner fat-fingers a delete, the lock — which applies **regardless of RBAC** — blocks it until the lock is deliberately removed. Purview knows the data, Policy shapes the resources, and locks freeze what must not break.

## Quick Contrast Drills

Four pairings the exam loves to blur — rehearse the one-line distinction:

1. **Azure Policy vs. RBAC:** Policy governs **WHAT** resources may be/look like (regions, tags, SKUs); RBAC governs **WHO** may perform which actions. A deny policy blocks even a fully-permissioned Owner.
2. **CanNotDelete vs. ReadOnly:** both block deletion, but **CanNotDelete still allows modification**, while **ReadOnly blocks modification AND deletion** (reads only).
3. **Microsoft Purview vs. Azure Policy:** Purview governs **data** (discover, classify, trace lineage across the whole estate); Policy governs **resources** (enforce configuration standards inside Azure).
4. **Policy vs. initiative:** a **policy** is one rule; an **initiative** is a **group of related policies** defined, assigned, and tracked as a single unit — assign the initiative once instead of twenty policies separately.

And one more that spans objectives: **resource locks vs. RBAC deny** — a lock blocks an *operation type* (delete/modify) for **everyone regardless of role**, while RBAC withholds *permissions* from **specific identities**. When the scenario stresses "accidental," reach for the lock; when it stresses "unauthorized," reach for RBAC.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Microsoft Purview = unified data-governance and compliance: automated **data discovery**, **sensitive-data classification**, and **end-to-end lineage**, producing a map of the data estate across **Azure, on-premises, Microsoft 365, and other clouds**.
- [ ] Purview spans two areas: **risk & compliance solutions** (Microsoft 365-centered, regulatory requirements like GDPR) and **unified data governance** (whole-estate data mapping).
- [ ] Azure Policy enforces standards on resources: define **policies**, group them into **initiatives**, assign at **management group / subscription / resource group**, and the assignment **inherits downward**.
- [ ] Policy effects: **deny** (prevent creation), **audit** (flag as non-compliant), **append/modify** (auto-remediate, e.g., add a required tag). Existing violators are highlighted, not deleted.
- [ ] Policy vs. RBAC: **Policy = WHAT** resources can be; **RBAC = WHO** can act. Real-exam stems: "only East US region," "require a costCenter tag."
- [ ] Resource locks: **CanNotDelete** (read/modify OK, delete blocked) and **ReadOnly** (no modify, no delete); applied at subscription, resource group, or resource scope and **inherited**.
- [ ] Locks apply **regardless of RBAC** — even an Owner must **remove the lock first**. They protect against **accidents**, not malicious actors.
- [ ] Together: Purview knows your **data**, Policy shapes your **resources**, locks protect the **critical ones** — with the Cloud Adoption Framework as the surrounding best-practice guidance.
