# Objective 3.1 — Describe cost management in Azure

Cloud spending is one of AZ-900's most heavily tested practical topics, because the pay-for-what-you-use model is a genuine mindset shift from buying hardware up front. This objective tests whether you know **what drives an Azure bill up or down** (resource type, consumption, maintenance, geography, network traffic, subscription type, Marketplace), **which estimating tool answers which question** (Pricing calculator vs. TCO Calculator — the exam's favorite trap pair), **what Microsoft Cost Management can do** once you're actually spending (cost analysis, budgets, alerts, recommendations), and **why tags exist** (metadata for organizing resources and attributing cost). Questions are almost always scenario-based: "a company wants to estimate…", "a team wants to be notified when…", "finance wants to see spend per department…". Learn each tool's one-line job description and the keyword that points to it.

## Factors that can affect costs in Azure

Azure bills on consumption, so anything that changes *what* you run, *how much* you use it, or *where* it lives changes the bill. The exam expects you to recognize all seven factors from a scenario:

### Resource type (and SKU/tier/size)

- **What it is:** every resource type is metered differently, and within a type the **SKU/tier/size you pick** sets the rate — a D-series VM costs more than a B-series, Premium SSD more than Standard HDD, a Premium App Service plan more than Free.
- **Why it matters:** configuration choices *inside* a resource also move the meter — storage redundancy level (LRS vs. GRS), database performance tier, provisioned IOPS. "Right-sizing" the SKU is usually the first savings lever.

### Consumption

- **What it is:** with **pay-as-you-go** you pay only for what you use, moment to moment — the default cloud model.
- **Why it matters:** if a workload is steady and predictable, committing to **reserved capacity** (Azure Reservations, 1- or 3-year terms) trades flexibility for discounts of **up to ~72%** versus pay-as-you-go rates. Spiky or short-lived workloads should stay pay-as-you-go and be shut down when idle.

### Maintenance

- **What it is:** cloud resources keep billing until you delete them. When you remove a VM, its **managed disks, public IP addresses, and other associated resources can survive it** and keep accruing charges ("orphaned resources").
- **Why it matters:** disciplined cleanup — often by grouping a workload's resources in one resource group and deleting the whole group — is itself a cost-control practice the exam calls the *maintenance* factor.

### Geography

- **What it is:** the **same service is priced differently in different regions**, because power, labor, taxes, and real estate vary by location.
- **Why it matters:** deploying to a cheaper region can cut costs, but the choice must be weighed against latency to users and data-residency/compliance requirements.

### Network traffic

- **What it is:** **inbound (ingress) data transfer is generally free; outbound (egress) data leaving a region is billed**, and bandwidth rates vary by **billing zone** (geographical groupings of regions).
- **Why it matters:** chatty cross-region architectures pay for every gigabyte that crosses the boundary — keeping resources that talk heavily to each other in the same region avoids egress charges.

### Subscription type

- **What it is:** some subscription offers include allowances and discounts — a **free trial** includes credits and 12 months of free popular services; **Enterprise Agreement (EA)** customers get negotiated discounts; Dev/Test offers give reduced rates for non-production work.
- **Why it matters:** the same meter can cost different customers different effective amounts, purely because of the offer their subscription is under.

### Azure Marketplace

- **What it is:** a catalog of third-party solutions (appliances, images, SaaS) deployable straight into your subscription.
- **Why it matters:** the **vendor's licensing/service fees are billed on top of** the underlying Azure resource charges, under the vendor's own billing terms — a Marketplace firewall costs its VM *plus* the vendor's license.

| Factor | One-line trigger phrase |
|---|---|
| Resource type / SKU | "Chose a bigger size / premium tier" |
| Consumption | "Pay-as-you-go vs. 1-/3-year reservation (~72% off)" |
| Maintenance | "Deleted the VM but disks/IPs still bill" |
| Geography | "Same service, different price per region" |
| Network traffic | "Ingress free, egress billed (varies by zone)" |
| Subscription type | "Free trial credits, EA discounts, Dev/Test rates" |
| Azure Marketplace | "Third-party license fee on top of Azure charges" |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** For predictable, always-on workloads, the answer to "how do we reduce compute cost?" is almost always **reserved instances/reserved capacity (up to ~72% savings)**. For spiky or short-lived workloads, stay pay-as-you-go and deallocate when idle.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** "We deleted the VM, so we stopped paying" — false. Disks and public IPs linger and keep billing until explicitly deleted. Likewise, a VM that is only *stopped from inside the OS* (not **deallocated** in Azure) still incurs compute charges — and even a deallocated VM's disks continue to bill.

## Pricing calculator vs. Total Cost of Ownership (TCO) Calculator

Both are **free web-based estimating tools you use *before* deploying** — no subscription required — but they answer completely different questions:

### Pricing calculator

- **What it is:** **estimates the monthly cost of Azure services you *plan* to deploy.** You add products (VMs, storage, databases…), configure region, SKU/tier, instance count, and hours, and it produces an itemized cost estimate for that future Azure workload.
- **Use case:** an architect finishing a design who must tell management "this solution will cost roughly $X/month" before anything is built.

### TCO Calculator

- **What it is:** **compares what your *existing on-premises* workloads cost today versus what running them in Azure would cost over 3–5 years.** You define your current servers, storage, and network, adjust assumptions (power, cooling, IT labor, licensing, data-center costs), and it reports the projected savings of migrating.
- **Use case:** building the *business case for migration* — the report a CIO shows the board to justify moving to the cloud.

| | Pricing calculator | TCO Calculator |
|---|---|---|
| Question answered | "What will this Azure workload cost per month?" | "What would we save by moving on-prem workloads to Azure?" |
| Input | Azure services you plan to use (SKU, region, quantity, hours) | Current on-prem servers/storage/network + cost assumptions (power, labor, licensing) |
| Output | Estimated monthly/upfront Azure cost | Multi-year (3–5 yr) on-prem vs. Azure cost comparison and savings report |
| When used | Designing a new Azure deployment | Building a business case for **migration** |
| Scope | Azure costs only | Full ownership cost: hardware, electricity, data center, IT labor |

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** Keyword mapping — "**estimate the cost of services we plan to deploy** in Azure" = **Pricing calculator**. "**Compare on-premises costs with Azure**" / "savings from migrating" / mentions of power, cooling, or IT labor = **TCO Calculator**. Neither tool shows what you are *currently spending* in Azure — that's Microsoft Cost Management's cost analysis.

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Both calculators produce **estimates, not quotes** — actual billing depends on actual consumption. And don't reverse them: the Pricing calculator is forward-looking for *Azure* deployments; the TCO Calculator starts from your *on-premises* footprint. A brand-new cloud-native app with no on-prem history has nothing for the TCO Calculator to compare.

## Microsoft Cost Management

- **What it is:** the free tool built into the Azure portal — **Microsoft Cost Management** (formerly *Azure Cost Management + Billing*) — for **monitoring, analyzing, alerting on, and optimizing** what you are *actually* spending after resources are deployed.
- **Why it matters:** the calculators end where real usage begins. Cost Management is how you catch overspending early instead of discovering it on the invoice.

Its main capabilities:

| Capability | What it does |
|---|---|
| **Cost analysis** | Visualizes actual and forecast spend, sliced by resource, resource group, subscription, service, **tag**, or region, over time — spot trends and anomalies |
| **Budgets** | Define a spending limit for a scope (subscription, resource group…) and track consumption against it |
| **Cost alerts** | Notify people when thresholds are hit (three kinds — see below) |
| **Recommendations** | Surfaces Azure Advisor cost recommendations — resize/shut down underutilized VMs, buy reservations, delete idle resources |

The three cost alert types are a classic discriminator question:

- **Budget alerts** — spending or usage reached a threshold **you defined in a budget** (e.g., 80% of $10,000).
- **Credit alerts** — a **prepaid Azure credit balance** (e.g., an EA monetary commitment) is close to being consumed.
- **Department spending quota alerts** — an **EA department's** spend reached a defined percentage of its allotted quota.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "The team wants an **email when spending reaches 80% of** the monthly limit" = create a **budget with a budget alert** in Microsoft Cost Management. A budget by itself **notifies — it does not stop or delete resources** (though it can trigger automation you build yourself).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Older study materials say "Azure Cost Management + Billing" — the current name on the exam is **Microsoft Cost Management**. And keep the alert types straight: **budget** = your defined threshold, **credit** = prepaid credit running out, **department spending quota** = EA department percentage.

## Purpose of tags

- **What they are:** **name/value metadata pairs** you attach to resources — e.g., `env = production`, `costcenter = 1287`, `owner = jsmith`, `project = phoenix`.
- **What they're for:** **organizing resources and reporting on them** across the dimensions *you* care about, which rarely match Azure's physical hierarchy. Chief uses:

| Use | Example |
|---|---|
| **Cost management & reporting** | Group the bill by `costcenter` or `project` in cost analysis |
| Operations management | Tag by criticality/SLA to drive operational grouping |
| Security | Classify data sensitivity (`confidential`, `public`) |
| Governance & compliance | Track regulatory requirements (`ISO27001`) or owners |
| Workload optimization/automation | Scripts target `env = dev` resources to shut down nightly |

- **Where they apply:** tags can be placed on **resources, resource groups, and subscriptions** (not management groups). Critically, tags are **NOT inherited by default** — tagging a resource group does *not* tag the resources inside it.
- **Enforcement:** use **Azure Policy** to require tags on new resources, append missing tags automatically, or inherit tags from a resource group/subscription — turning a naming convention into an enforced rule.

> 💡 **Exam Tip:** "Finance needs the Azure bill **broken down by department/cost center/project**" = apply **tags**, then group by tag in **cost analysis**. Tags are the bridge between the two halves of this objective: the metadata (tags) feeds the reporting tool (Cost Management).

> ⚠️ **Common Pitfall:** Two classic wrong assumptions: (1) tags on a resource group flow down to its resources — they don't, unless an Azure Policy makes it happen; (2) tags control access or enforce anything by themselves — they are pure metadata. Enforcement is Azure Policy's job; access control is RBAC's job.

## How It Fits Together (mental model)

Picture a migration project end to end. Before touching Azure, the company runs the **TCO Calculator** — entering its current servers, storage, network, power, and IT-labor costs — to prove that moving beats staying on-prem over the next 3–5 years. With the business case approved, architects use the **Pricing calculator** to estimate the monthly cost of the specific design, discovering along the way that every **cost factor** moves the number: choosing a smaller **SKU**, a cheaper **region**, and **reserved instances** (~72% off) for the steady database shrinks the estimate, while cross-region **egress traffic** and a third-party **Marketplace** firewall appliance grow it. Once deployed, **tags** (`costcenter`, `env`, `project`) are stamped on every resource — enforced by Azure Policy since tags don't inherit on their own — so that **Microsoft Cost Management** can slice actual spend by department in **cost analysis**, guard each team's limit with **budgets** and **cost alerts**, and flag the orphaned disks and idle VMs (**maintenance**) that its **recommendations** say should be deleted or resized.

## Quick Contrast Drills

Four pairings the exam loves to blur — rehearse the one-line distinction:

1. **Pricing calculator vs. TCO Calculator:** both estimate before you deploy, but Pricing prices a **planned Azure workload per month**, while TCO compares **existing on-prem costs vs. Azure over 3–5 years** to justify migration.
2. **Calculators vs. Microsoft Cost Management:** calculators are **pre-deployment estimates**; Cost Management analyzes and alerts on **actual post-deployment spend**.
3. **Budget vs. budget alert:** the budget is the **defined spending limit**; the alert is the **notification** when a threshold percentage of it is reached — and neither one shuts resources down.
4. **Tags vs. Azure Policy:** tags are **passive name/value metadata**; Azure Policy is the **enforcement engine** that can require, append, or inherit them. Metadata organizes; policy enforces.

## Key Takeaways

- [ ] Azure costs are driven by: resource type (and its SKU/tier/size), consumption, maintenance, geography, network traffic, subscription type, and Azure Marketplace purchases.
- [ ] Reserved capacity (1- or 3-year commitment) saves **up to ~72%** versus pay-as-you-go for stable, predictable workloads.
- [ ] Deleting a VM does not delete its disks or public IPs — orphaned resources keep billing until fully deprovisioned; a VM must be **deallocated** (not just OS-shutdown) to pause compute charges.
- [ ] Prices vary by region (geography), and **inbound data transfer is free while outbound/egress is billed** (rates vary by billing zone).
- [ ] **Pricing calculator** = monthly estimate for planned Azure services; **TCO Calculator** = on-prem vs. Azure comparison over 3–5 years including power, labor, and licensing. Both are estimates, not quotes.
- [ ] **Microsoft Cost Management** (formerly Azure Cost Management + Billing) provides cost analysis, budgets, cost alerts (budget / credit / department spending quota), and recommendations.
- [ ] Budgets and alerts **notify** — they don't stop spending or delete resources by themselves.
- [ ] Tags are name/value metadata for organizing resources and cost reporting; applicable at resource, resource group, and subscription level; **not inherited by default**; enforceable via Azure Policy.
- [ ] AZ-900 itself is scored 1–1000 with a passing score of **700**.

## Self-Check Questions

1. A CFO wants a report showing how much the company would save over five years by moving 40 on-premises servers to Azure, factoring in electricity, cooling, and IT labor. Which tool should be used?
2. An architect has designed a new solution — 6 VMs, a SQL database, and a storage account — and needs a monthly cost estimate before deployment. Which tool fits?
3. A team deleted several VMs last month, yet related charges keep appearing on the invoice. What is the most likely cause?
4. Operations wants an automatic email whenever a subscription's spending reaches 90% of its $5,000 monthly limit. What should be configured, and where?
5. A company tags each resource group with `costcenter`, but cost analysis shows the resources inside those groups with no cost-center value. Why, and what fixes it?
